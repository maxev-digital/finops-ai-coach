# Buy-Sell Agreements: Protecting the Business When an Owner Exits

## Purpose and Overview

A buy-sell agreement (also called a business continuation agreement or buyout agreement) is a legally binding contract among business owners that governs what happens to an ownership interest when a triggering event occurs. Without a buy-sell agreement, the death, disability, divorce, or retirement of a business owner can create a crisis: the remaining owners may be forced into partnership with an heir, a divorcing spouse, or an outside buyer they never agreed to partner with.

A well-drafted, properly funded buy-sell agreement:
- Provides certainty about business ownership transitions
- Ensures the departing owner (or their estate) receives fair value
- Keeps the business in the hands of the intended owners
- Provides liquidity for estate settlement
- Prevents disruption to customers, employees, and operations

## Triggering Events

A comprehensive buy-sell agreement covers multiple triggering events:

**Death**: When an owner dies, the agreement requires their estate to sell the ownership interest — typically at a pre-agreed price or formula — to the remaining owners or the entity.

**Disability**: When an owner becomes permanently or long-term disabled (definition specified in the agreement — often tied to insurance policy definitions), their interest may be bought out. Temporary disability is usually handled differently.

**Retirement or Voluntary Withdrawal**: Owner wishes to exit the business; remaining owners have right of first refusal at the agreed price.

**Divorce**: The divorcing owner must buy out the spouse's community property claim or sell their interest to the entity/partners. This prevents a divorcing spouse from becoming an unwanted co-owner.

**Bankruptcy**: If an owner files bankruptcy, the trustee may seek to liquidate assets including the business interest. The buy-sell agreement can provide the remaining owners the right to purchase the interest before an outside party can acquire it.

**Loss of professional license**: For businesses that require professional licensing (medical practices, law firms, accounting firms), the loss of an owner's license can require a buyout.

**Involuntary transfer**: Creditors obtaining a judgment against an owner may seek the business interest. A buy-sell agreement can provide right of first refusal.

## Structure: Entity Purchase vs. Cross-Purchase

### Entity Purchase (Stock Redemption)

The business entity itself buys out the departing owner's interest. Life insurance is purchased by the entity, with the entity as owner and beneficiary.

**Advantages**:
- Simpler administration: one policy per owner, not one per owner pair
- For 4+ owners, dramatically fewer policies than cross-purchase (4 owners cross-purchase = 12 policies; entity purchase = 4 policies)
- Insurance premium payment is easier through the entity

**Disadvantages**:
- **No basis step-up for surviving owners**: In an entity purchase, the surviving owners do not increase their basis in the company. If the company is later sold, the surviving owners' gain includes the full appreciation even through the buyout years.
- **Corporate-owned life insurance (COLI) rules**: IRC §101(j) notice and consent requirements must be followed or the death benefit may be taxable
- **Alternative minimum tax (C-Corp)**: Life insurance proceeds may generate corporate AMT issues under certain circumstances

### Cross-Purchase Agreement

Each owner individually purchases life insurance on each other owner. When an owner dies, the surviving owners use their policy proceeds to buy out the estate.

**Advantages**:
- **Basis step-up for surviving owners**: When an owner buys the deceased's interest, the surviving owner's basis increases to the purchase price. This reduces capital gains when the business is eventually sold.
- Simpler for two-owner businesses
- Avoids corporate COLI tax issues

**Disadvantages**:
- Number of policies required grows rapidly: n(n-1) policies for n owners (2 owners = 2 policies; 3 owners = 6 policies; 4 owners = 12 policies)
- Unequal premiums: younger owners pay less for coverage on older owners; older owners pay more
- If an owner withdraws, the insurance arrangements become disorganized

**Wait-and-See Buy-Sell**: A hybrid that defers the choice between entity purchase and cross-purchase until a triggering event occurs, allowing the most tax-advantaged structure to be chosen at that time.

## Valuation Methods

Getting the business value right is the most contentious aspect of buy-sell agreements. Three approaches:

### Fixed Value
The owners agree on a specific dollar amount when drafting the agreement. Simple, but stale: the value agreed upon in 2020 may be wildly different from fair value in 2027 after years of growth.

**Requirement for fixed value**: Must be updated annually (or at least every 2-3 years) by the owners. Many agreements are never updated — the agreed value becomes meaningless. Include a mandatory annual review clause.

### Formula Method
Define a calculation that is applied at the triggering event:
- Multiple of EBITDA (e.g., 3.5× trailing 12-month EBITDA)
- Book value
- Multiple of revenue
- Average earnings over 3 years

**Advantage**: Self-updating as financials change; no manual update required.

**Disadvantage**: Formula methods may not accurately reflect fair market value, particularly for businesses with intangible value or unusual characteristics. A formula that makes sense for a mature stable business may over- or under-value a growing or declining business.

### Independent Appraisal at the Triggering Event
An independent, qualified business appraiser (CVA, ABV, ASA) performs a formal valuation when the triggering event occurs.

**Advantage**: Most accurate; reflects actual fair market value at the time
**Disadvantage**: Expensive ($5,000-$25,000+); causes delays; parties may disagree with the appraiser's conclusion; appraiser selection can itself be contentious

**Best practice**: Designate a specific appraisal methodology in the agreement, specify who selects the appraiser (or use two appraisers with an umpire for disputed valuations), and include a timeline for the appraisal process.

## Funding Mechanisms

A buy-sell agreement without funding is merely a promise. The most reliable funding mechanisms:

### Life Insurance (Death Trigger)
The preferred funding for death-triggered buyouts. Benefits are received income-tax-free (subject to COLI rules for corporate-owned policies), immediately available, and exactly the right amount if coverage is sized to the agreed business value.

**Key issue**: Coverage must be updated as business value grows. If the company is worth $3M today and policies were issued for $1.5M three years ago, there is a $1.5M funding gap. Schedule annual coverage reviews alongside valuation updates.

### Disability Buyout Insurance (Disability Trigger)
Separate insurance product specifically designed to fund disability-triggered buyouts. Key features:
- Typically 2-year elimination period (buyout insurance kicks in after long-term disability is confirmed)
- Premiums are typically not deductible; benefits are received tax-free
- Available coverage amounts may be limited by insurer; closely held business values may require multiple policies or may exceed available insurance limits

Business Overhead Expense (BOE) insurance covers the business's operating expenses during an owner's disability period — separate from and complementary to disability buyout insurance.

### Installment Note (Seller Financing)
The entity or surviving owners pay the departing owner's estate over time.
- **Advantage**: No upfront insurance cost
- **Disadvantage**: Credit risk — if the business struggles, payments may not be made; estate cannot be closed while installments are outstanding; surviving owners' personal credit may need to guarantee the note

### Sinking Fund
The business accumulates cash in a dedicated reserve account to fund future buyouts.
- **Advantage**: Certain and guaranteed (if funded)
- **Disadvantage**: Large cash drag on the business; alternative use of capital forgone; cash in a corporate bank account earns low returns vs. business reinvestment; tax inefficiency (C-Corp pays tax on investment income)

Life insurance-funded cash value policies can be a more tax-efficient version of a sinking fund — premiums are not deductible, but the inside cash value builds tax-deferred.

## Key Agreement Provisions

**Right of first refusal**: Before any owner can transfer their interest to an outside party, the other owners (or the entity) must be given the opportunity to purchase it at the same price. This prevents unwanted outside ownership.

**Valuation update requirement**: Mandate annual review of the agreed value (for fixed value agreements) and insurance coverage adequacy.

**Funding adequacy**: Require that insurance coverage be maintained at the agreed valuation; if insurance lapses, the buyout obligation still exists but may be unfunded.

**Disability definition**: Tie the disability trigger to an objective standard, ideally matching the definition in the disability buyout insurance policy.

**Drag-along and tag-along provisions**: If a majority owner sells, drag-along allows them to require minority owners to sell in the same transaction; tag-along allows minority owners to participate in the same sale at the same price.

## Section 1042 Exchange for ESOP Sales

If the buy-sell is structured as a sale to an Employee Stock Ownership Plan (ESOP), a qualifying C-Corporation owner can defer capital gains on the sale proceeds by reinvesting in Qualified Replacement Property (QRP). See the business succession document for full ESOP and 1042 details.
