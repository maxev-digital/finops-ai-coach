# Health Savings Account (HSA) Strategies: The Triple Tax Advantage

## HSA Eligibility Requirements

To contribute to a Health Savings Account (HSA), you must meet all of the following conditions:

1. **Enrolled in a qualifying High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP)**: The health plan must meet minimum deductible and maximum out-of-pocket thresholds. 2025 HDHP requirements:
   - Minimum deductible: $1,650 self-only / $3,300 family
   - Maximum out-of-pocket: $8,300 self-only / $16,600 family

2. **Not enrolled in Medicare** (any part — A, B, C, or D): Once enrolled in any Medicare part, HSA contributions are no longer allowed. This is a critical planning point for those approaching 65 who want to continue contributing.

3. **Not claimed as a dependent** on someone else's tax return

4. **No other disqualifying health coverage**: Cannot have a health FSA (except a limited-purpose dental/vision FSA), HRA (except certain qualifying HRAs), or spouse's employer general-purpose FSA covering you

## 2025 Contribution Limits

| Coverage type | 2025 Limit |
|--------------|-----------|
| Self-only | $4,300 |
| Family | $8,550 |
| Age 55+ catch-up (per eligible spouse) | +$1,000 |

**Married couple both age 55+ on family HDHP**: Maximum contribution = $10,550 ($8,550 + $1,000 catch-up for each eligible spouse — but catch-up contributions must go to separate HSA accounts, one per individual)

Contributions can be made by the account holder, employer, or anyone else (family member, etc.). All contributions count toward the annual limit regardless of source. Employer contributions reduce your personal contribution limit dollar-for-dollar.

**Pro-rata rule for mid-year enrollment**: If you become eligible mid-year, you can contribute the full annual limit using the "last-month rule" — as long as you remain eligible through December 31 of the following year. If you fail to maintain eligibility through that testing period, the pro-rated excess becomes taxable income with a 10% penalty.

## The Triple Tax Advantage

The HSA is the only account that provides all three tax advantages simultaneously:

1. **Contributions are tax-deductible (or pre-tax)**: Contributions through payroll are pre-tax (avoiding FICA taxes as well as income taxes — an additional 7.65% advantage); direct contributions are above-the-line deductible on Form 1040 (deductible regardless of whether you itemize)

2. **Growth is tax-free**: Interest, dividends, and capital gains inside the HSA are never taxed as long as they remain in the account

3. **Qualified withdrawals are tax-free**: Distributions for qualified medical expenses are completely tax-free

Compare:
- Traditional IRA: Pre-tax contributions, tax-deferred growth, taxable withdrawals (only 2 advantages)
- Roth IRA: After-tax contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals (only 2 advantages)
- HSA: Pre-tax contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals (all 3 advantages, for medical expenses)

## Qualified Medical Expenses

IRS Publication 502 provides the definitive list. Broad categories include:
- Insurance deductibles, copays, and coinsurance
- Prescription medications
- Dental care (fillings, crowns, orthodontia)
- Vision care (exams, glasses, contact lenses, LASIK)
- Mental health services (therapy, psychiatry)
- Hearing aids and batteries
- Medical equipment (crutches, blood pressure monitors, glucose monitors)
- Long-term care services (qualified amounts)
- Acupuncture
- Chiropractic care
- Physical therapy

**Cannot be paid with HSA** (without penalty):
- Health insurance premiums in general (exception: see below)
- Gym memberships (unless for a specific condition prescribed by doctor)
- Most cosmetic procedures
- Most over-the-counter drugs without a prescription (though CARES Act expanded some OTC coverage)

**Special exceptions where premiums ARE qualified expenses**:
- COBRA continuation premiums
- Long-term care insurance premiums (qualified amounts based on age)
- Medicare premiums (Part B, Part D, Medicare Advantage) — after age 65
- Health coverage while receiving unemployment compensation

## The Optimal HSA Investment Strategy: The "Stealth IRA"

Most people use their HSA as a healthcare spending account — depositing and immediately withdrawing for current medical costs. This is the least optimal approach.

**The optimal strategy**: Invest the HSA like a brokerage account and pay all current medical expenses out of pocket, leaving the HSA untouched to grow.

**Why this is powerful**:
- At age 65: $20,000 invested in the HSA in 2025 and never touched (at 7% annual return) = approximately $147,000 in 40 years — all tax-free for medical expenses
- Same $20,000 used immediately for current medical costs: nothing left for compound growth

**The receipt-keeping strategy**:
- Pay all medical expenses out of pocket with personal funds
- Save every receipt for qualified medical expenses paid out of pocket
- There is NO deadline for reimbursing yourself from the HSA — you can pay a medical bill in 2025 and reimburse yourself from the HSA in 2045 (as long as the HSA was established before the expense was incurred)
- This creates a massive pool of tax-free "basis" in future years — you can withdraw these reimbursable amounts tax-free for any reason at any time

**Result**: Your HSA becomes the best retirement account available. You contribute tax-free, invest, grow tax-free for decades, and then either:
1. Use distributions for medical expenses (tax-free forever)
2. Reimburse yourself for decades of accumulated receipts (tax-free whenever you choose)
3. After age 65, withdraw for any purpose (taxed as ordinary income — same as a Traditional IRA, but without required minimum distributions)

## HSA After Age 65: Flexibility Increases

Once you turn 65:
- **Medical withdrawals remain tax-free** (as always)
- **Non-medical withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income** but no longer subject to the 20% penalty — the HSA becomes equivalent to a Traditional IRA for non-medical withdrawals
- **Medicare premiums can be paid with HSA funds** (Parts B, D, Medicare Advantage) — a significant benefit since Medicare premiums are otherwise not deductible for most people
- **No required minimum distributions** (unlike Traditional IRAs and 401k plans) — the HSA is not subject to RMD rules, providing flexibility that Traditional retirement accounts lack

## HSA and Medicare: The Contribution Cutoff

Once you enroll in ANY part of Medicare, you can no longer contribute to an HSA. This creates a planning challenge for those approaching 65:

**If you plan to work past 65 and stay on employer health insurance**:
- You can delay Medicare enrollment (provided your employer has 20+ employees and coverage is considered primary)
- Continue contributing to your HSA
- Stop contributing at least 6 months before claiming Social Security (because Social Security enrollment triggers automatic Medicare Part A enrollment, and Part A enrollment retroactively covers the 6 months before Social Security begins — potentially creating excess HSA contributions)

**If you claim Social Security before 65 or at 65**: You are automatically enrolled in Medicare Part A. HSA contributions must stop. Be aware of the 6-month retroactive Part A coverage window when calculating the date contributions should cease.

## Family HSA Strategies

**Spouse with individual HDHP, other spouse on employer plan**:
- The HDHP-enrolled spouse can contribute up to the self-only limit ($4,300)
- The other spouse cannot contribute to an HSA if their employer plan is not a qualifying HDHP

**Both spouses on family HDHP**:
- Combined family limit is $8,550 — shared between both spouses' HSAs
- Each spouse should have their own HSA account to allow individual $1,000 catch-up contributions at age 55+

**Adult children on family HDHP**:
- A dependent child cannot have their own HSA — they are a dependent
- Parents' HSA can pay for the dependent child's qualified medical expenses
- But if an adult child (over 26) is on a parent's family HDHP but NOT a tax dependent — they may be able to have their own HSA

## HDHP vs. Low-Deductible Plan: The Break-Even Analysis

The decision to choose an HDHP over a traditional plan should be a financial analysis:

**HDHP advantage**:
- Lower monthly premium (often $100-$300/month less than low-deductible plan)
- Employer may contribute to the HSA ($500-$2,000/year is common)
- Your own HSA contributions are tax-advantaged

**HDHP disadvantage**:
- Higher out-of-pocket costs if significant healthcare is used
- Requires larger cash reserve to cover the deductible before insurance kicks in

**Break-even calculation**:
Annual premium savings (HDHP vs. low-deductible plan) + Employer HSA contribution + Tax savings on your HSA contribution vs. expected additional out-of-pocket costs under the HDHP

For healthy families, the premium savings and tax benefits frequently outweigh the higher deductible exposure — particularly if the HSA is invested rather than spent.
