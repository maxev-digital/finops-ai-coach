# Charitable Giving Strategies

Source: IRS Publication 526, IRC §170, §664, §2522.

---

## Deductibility Basics

Charitable deductions require itemizing (Schedule A). The 2025 standard deduction is $15,000 (single) / $30,000 (MFJ) — high enough that most taxpayers don't itemize. This is the core problem: average donors lose the deduction entirely.

**AGI Limits on Deductions:**
- Cash to public charity: deductible up to 60% of AGI
- Appreciated property to public charity: deductible up to 30% of AGI
- Gifts to private foundations: 30% (cash), 20% (appreciated property)
- Excess carries forward up to 5 years

---

## Donor-Advised Fund (DAF)

A DAF is a charitable giving account sponsored by a public charity (Fidelity Charitable, Schwab Charitable, Vanguard Charitable, community foundations). You contribute assets, take the tax deduction immediately, then direct grants to specific charities over time.

**Key advantages:**
- Immediate deduction in the year of contribution (regardless of when you grant out)
- Appreciated securities donated directly: no capital gains tax, full FMV deduction
- Bunching strategy: contribute multiple years of giving in one year to clear the standard deduction threshold
- Investments inside DAF grow tax-free until granted
- Can name a successor advisor (family legacy giving)

**Example of bunching:** Couple normally gives $10,000/year. Standard deduction $30,000. $10,000 gives them no incremental benefit. Instead, contribute $50,000 to a DAF in year 1 (5-year lump sum). Itemize $50,000 deduction. Years 2–5: take standard deduction. Net benefit: one large deduction vs. none.

## Qualified Charitable Distribution (QCD)

For IRA owners age 70½ or older. Direct transfer from IRA to qualified charity. Up to $105,000 per individual per year (2025, indexed). Counts toward RMD but excluded from gross income.

**Why this is superior to "withdraw and donate":**
- Withdraw + donate: income goes up, deduction available only if you itemize; SS taxability may increase; IRMAA may trigger
- QCD: income never appears in AGI at all — no SS taxation impact, no IRMAA impact, no state income tax (in states that tax IRA withdrawals)

**Restrictions:** Must go directly from IRA to charity (not to DAF — DAF not a qualified recipient for QCD). Charity must be a public 501(c)(3). Cannot be a gift to a private foundation, supporting organization, or DAF.

## Donating Appreciated Securities

Donating stock, ETFs, or mutual fund shares held more than 12 months:
- Deduct full fair market value (not your cost basis)
- Never pay capital gains tax on the embedded gain
- Works best with assets with large unrealized gains

Donate to charity directly, or contribute to a DAF (especially if splitting among multiple charities).

**Mutual fund shares with embedded gains:** Some long-held mutual fund positions have enormous unrealized capital gains from decades of compounding. These are ideal candidates for charitable donation — the gain disappears entirely.

## Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT)

1. Transfer appreciated assets to an irrevocable CRT
2. Trust sells assets tax-free (charitable organization owns them, no capital gains)
3. You (or other named income beneficiary) receive an annuity or percentage payout for life or a term (up to 20 years)
4. Remainder passes to charity at end of term
5. You receive a partial charitable deduction upfront (present value of the remainder interest)

**CRT types:**
- CRUT (Charitable Remainder Unitrust): fixed percentage of annual trust value (fluctuates)
- CRAT (Charitable Remainder Annuity Trust): fixed dollar amount (doesn't change with trust value)

Best for: large low-basis appreciated assets (real estate, concentrated stock) you'd otherwise hold forever due to capital gains. Converts to diversified income stream while supporting charity.

---

## Charitable Lead Trust (CLT)

The reverse of a CRT. Trust pays an annuity to charity for a term; at the end, the remainder passes to your heirs. Useful for estate planning — reduces taxable estate while supporting charity during your lifetime. Grantor CLT: you retain income tax deduction (complex interaction with grantor trust rules).

## Private Foundations vs. DAFs

| | DAF | Private Foundation |
|-|-----|-------------------|
| Setup | Minutes (open account) | Months (legal formation) |
| Minimum | $5,000–$25,000 typical | $250,000+ practical minimum |
| Control | Advise grants (no legal control) | Full control over grants |
| Deduction limit (cash) | 60% AGI | 30% AGI |
| Deduction limit (appreciated stock) | 30% AGI | 20% AGI |
| Annual distribution requirement | None | 5% of assets per year |
| Excise tax | None | 1.39% on investment income |
| Privacy | Not public | 990-PF is public record |
| Successor | Named successor advisor | Governed by foundation board |

For most donors under $10M: DAF is simpler, more tax-efficient, and achieves the same goals. Private foundations make sense when family governance, grantmaking control, and legacy are the primary drivers.

## Volunteering and Non-Cash Donations

- Out-of-pocket expenses for volunteer work: deductible (mileage at $0.14/mile, supplies)
- Your time: NOT deductible (IRS has never allowed a deduction for the value of volunteer time)
- Non-cash property donations over $500: require Form 8283
- Non-cash property over $5,000 (except publicly traded securities): require qualified appraisal
- Vehicle donations: deduction limited to proceeds the charity receives from selling it (not fair market value), unless charity uses it directly

## Key Planning Scenarios

**High-income year:** Bonus or large capital gain event → bunch charitable giving + donate appreciated securities → maximize deduction while in highest bracket

**Pre-retirement asset conversion:** Convert low-basis concentrated stock to diversified charitable position via CRT, avoid capital gains, receive income stream

**RMD management:** Use QCD to satisfy RMD without increasing AGI — especially valuable near IRMAA thresholds or when SS benefits are nearly fully taxable

**Legacy planning:** Fund DAF with IRA beneficiary designation (IRA → charity is 100% tax-free; IRA → heirs is 100% taxable). Leave appreciated stocks to heirs (stepped-up basis). Leave IRAs to charity.
