# The Bucket Strategy for Retirement Income

## Overview

The bucket strategy (also called time-segmentation or liability-matching) organizes retirement assets into separate "buckets" based on when they will be needed. Each bucket has a different investment objective, time horizon, and risk tolerance. The strategy's primary purpose is psychological and behavioral — protecting the retiree from panic-selling during market downturns by ensuring near-term spending needs are not dependent on stock market performance.

Harold Evensky popularized the two-bucket approach in the 1980s (a "cash flow reserve" plus the investment portfolio). The three-bucket model associated with financial planner Ray Lucia gained wider adoption in the early 2000s and became a widely used framework for retirement income planning.

## Bucket 1: Now (Years 1–3)

**Purpose:** Fund living expenses in the immediate term without requiring any sale of investment assets.

**Time horizon:** 1–3 years of living expenses

**Asset classes:**
- Cash (checking/savings accounts)
- Money market funds
- Short-term CDs (3–12 months)
- Short-term Treasury bills
- I-Bonds (after the 12-month lock-up period)

**Investment characteristics:** No equity exposure. Capital preservation is the sole objective. Accepts minimal return in exchange for absolute stability of principal.

**Why 1–3 years (not just 6 months):** Most equity bear markets resolve within 12–18 months in terms of price recovery, though the 2000–2002 and 2007–2009 downturns were exceptions (3 years and approximately 1.5 years to trough, respectively, with longer recovery periods). A 2-year buffer covers most historically observed bear market durations without the need to sell equities.

**What flows into Bucket 1:**
- Social Security and pension income (received monthly, directly funds near-term expenses)
- RMDs (must be taken annually, can be directed to cash)
- Portfolio dividends and interest from Bucket 2 (if not reinvested)
- Replenishment from Bucket 2 at year-end or when balance falls below a threshold (e.g., 6 months of expenses)

**Inflation exposure:** The one meaningful weakness of Bucket 1 is inflation risk over the 1–3 year holding period. Cash does not keep pace with inflation. However, this is an acceptable, short-duration risk — the exposure is bounded by the amount held and the holding period.

## Bucket 2: Soon (Years 4–10)

**Purpose:** Provide medium-term capital to replenish Bucket 1 as it is spent down. Serve as the "bridge" between current needs and long-term growth.

**Time horizon:** 4–10 years of expenses

**Asset classes:**
- Intermediate-term bonds and bond funds (3–10 year maturities)
- Bond ladders (individual bonds maturing in the 4–10 year window)
- High-quality corporate bonds (investment grade)
- TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) for inflation hedge
- Conservative allocation funds (30–40% equity)
- Dividend-focused stock funds (some advisors include these in Bucket 2 for income generation)

**Investment characteristics:** Low to moderate risk. Minimal equity. Designed to preserve purchasing power and generate reliable income while providing more return than cash.

**Replenishment source:** Bucket 2 is replenished from Bucket 3 (the equity portfolio) during favorable market years when markets have appreciated significantly.

**Bond ladder application:** A common refinement is building a bond ladder in Bucket 2 — individual bonds maturing in years 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10. As each bond matures, the proceeds flow to Bucket 1. This provides predictable cash flows without reliance on bond fund pricing or management decisions.

## Bucket 3: Later (Years 10+)

**Purpose:** Long-term growth to fund spending decades from now and provide inflation protection.

**Time horizon:** 10 years and beyond

**Asset classes:**
- Diversified equity mutual funds and ETFs (U.S. and international)
- Real estate investment trusts (REITs) for inflation protection and income
- Small-cap, value, or other factor-tilted equity strategies
- International equities (developed and emerging markets)
- Growth stocks
- Alternative investments (commodities, infrastructure) in some implementations

**Investment characteristics:** Full market risk. Long time horizon absorbs short-term volatility. Equity allocation consistent with overall risk tolerance but typically 70–100% equity within this bucket.

**The inflation antidote:** Bucket 3 is the portfolio component that fights inflation over 20–30+ years. Cash loses purchasing power; equities historically grow in real terms. Without a substantial equity allocation in the long-horizon bucket, the retiree risks the portfolio not keeping pace with inflation over a long retirement.

**Liquidity constraint:** Bucket 3 assets should ideally not be touched for at least 10 years. During bear markets, this bucket may decline 30–50% on paper — but those are unrealized losses that need not be recognized because Buckets 1 and 2 fund near-term spending.

## Replenishment and Rebalancing Rules

The mechanics of maintaining the bucket system require clear, predetermined rules:

**Replenish Bucket 1 from Bucket 2:**
- Trigger: Bucket 1 balance falls below 6 months of spending (some advisors use 12 months as the minimum)
- Frequency: Annual review (December) or as needed
- Action: Transfer sufficient Bucket 2 assets to Bucket 1 to restore target level (typically 1–2 years of expenses)
- Exception: If a major market downturn is in progress, delay the Bucket 1 replenishment from Bucket 2 and run Bucket 1 lower than normal before triggering the replenishment

**Replenish Bucket 2 from Bucket 3:**
- Trigger: Bucket 3 has grown above a target allocation, signaling a favorable market environment
- Frequency: Annual or semi-annual (not triggered by market downturns — wait for recovery)
- Action: Rebalance Bucket 3 by taking profits, moving into Bucket 2 to restore the 4–10 year reserve
- Rule of thumb: Replenish Bucket 2 only after markets are positive year-over-year (not selling equities in down markets)

**The critical rule:** Never sell Bucket 3 equities to replenish Bucket 1 during a sustained market downturn. If Bucket 1 is running low during a bear market, first exhaust Bucket 2 before touching Bucket 3. The entire point of the structure is to insulate Bucket 3 from being sold at depressed prices.

## Behavioral Value — The Primary Argument

Purely from a mathematical standpoint, the bucket strategy is functionally equivalent to holding an equivalent overall asset allocation under a total-return approach with systematic rebalancing. This has been demonstrated in multiple academic analyses.

**The behavioral value is real and should not be dismissed.**

Retirement is a period of significant financial anxiety. Market crashes — 2000–2002, 2008–2009, March 2020 — produce panic selling by retail investors at exactly the wrong time. The bucket strategy addresses this by making the answer to "what do I do about my portfolio during a market crash?" simple and concrete: **nothing, because my spending for the next 2–3 years is already in cash**.

The investor who "sees" their retirement income sitting in a money market account does not need to make emotional decisions about a crashing stock portfolio. The equity losses in Bucket 3 are distant, abstract, and temporary. The spending security in Bucket 1 is immediate and concrete.

**Research on investor behavior consistently shows** that the single largest determinant of poor investor returns is poorly timed buying and selling driven by fear and greed — not high fees, not suboptimal asset allocation, not poor fund selection. A strategy that prevents catastrophic behavioral errors at the worst possible moment has real, quantifiable value even if it cannot be demonstrated in a Monte Carlo simulation.

## Critique of the Bucket Strategy

Advisors in the "total return" camp make valid points:

1. **Mathematical equivalence:** A bucket strategy with 70% equity total and a total-return portfolio with 70% equity have the same expected return distribution. Labeling funds doesn't change their investment characteristics.

2. **Suboptimal cash allocation:** Holding 1–2 years in cash creates a persistent drag on returns. Money market funds earning 4–5% in high-rate environments leave money on the table versus short-term bonds or equities with longer time horizons.

3. **Artificial complexity:** Maintaining multiple accounts and executing replenishment rules adds operational complexity without guaranteed mathematical benefit.

**The response:** All of these critiques are accurate in a frictionless world with perfectly rational investors. In the real world, if the bucket structure prevents one instance of panic-selling during a 40% bear market, the behavioral value almost certainly exceeds the opportunity cost of suboptimal cash allocation. Advisors should use the framework that their specific client will actually follow.

## Integration with Social Security and RMDs

**Social Security flows into Bucket 1.** Monthly SS payments are essentially Bucket 1 inflows — they reduce the amount of portfolio assets needed in near-term cash. A retiree receiving $3,000/month in SS needs less in Bucket 1 than one with no guaranteed income.

**RMDs are directed to Bucket 1.** Required minimum distributions from Traditional IRAs must be taken annually. Rather than treating them as a problem, route RMDs into Bucket 1 as planned income. RMDs from a $1M IRA at age 75 are approximately $40,650/year — a meaningful Bucket 1 source.

**Pension income functions as Bucket 1.** Any fixed monthly income (pension, annuity, rental income) reduces the amount that must be held in Bucket 1 cash. If guaranteed income exceeds monthly spending, Bucket 1 may be minimal or unnecessary entirely.

## Inflation Awareness Across Buckets

- **Bucket 1:** Direct inflation exposure over 1–3 years. Acceptable, bounded risk.
- **Bucket 2:** TIPS and I-bonds provide explicit inflation linkage. Intermediate bonds provide partial inflation protection.
- **Bucket 3:** Long-term equity returns have historically exceeded inflation by 4–6% annually (real returns). This is the inflation solution for the retiree's 20–30+ year horizon.

The most significant inflation error retirees make is holding too much in Buckets 1 and 2 out of fear of market volatility, leaving too little in Bucket 3 to generate real growth. The result is a portfolio that appears "safe" early in retirement but slowly falls behind inflation over 20 years, producing declining real purchasing power.

**Rule of thumb:** Even the most conservative retiree should hold at minimum 40–50% of total portfolio in Bucket 3 equities. The two-year cash cushion in Bucket 1 provides the psychological security to tolerate this allocation.
