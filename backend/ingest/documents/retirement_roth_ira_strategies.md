# Roth IRA Strategies

## Contribution Rules and Income Limits (2025)

The Roth IRA annual contribution limit in 2025 is **$7,000** ($8,000 for age 50 and older). This limit is shared across all IRAs — total contributions to Traditional and Roth IRAs combined cannot exceed $7,000/$8,000.

**Income phase-out ranges for direct Roth IRA contributions (2025):**

| Filing Status | Phase-Out Range | Full Contribution | No Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single / Head of Household | $150,000 – $165,000 | Below $150,000 | Above $165,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $236,000 – $246,000 | Below $236,000 | Above $246,000 |
| Married Filing Separately (lived with spouse) | $0 – $10,000 | $0 | Above $10,000 |

Within the phase-out range, the maximum contribution is reduced proportionally. Above the upper threshold, no direct Roth IRA contribution is permitted (use backdoor Roth instead — see below).

Contributions require **earned income** (wages, self-employment, alimony in qualifying situations). The contribution limit cannot exceed the taxpayer's earned income for the year.

Contribution deadline is **April 15** of the following year (extensions do not extend this deadline).

## Qualified Distribution Rules

A Roth IRA distribution is **qualified** (completely tax-free and penalty-free) when BOTH of the following are met:

1. **The Roth IRA has been open for at least 5 years** (the 5-year holding period begins January 1 of the tax year for which the first Roth IRA contribution was made — a 2020 contribution starts the clock on January 1, 2020, making it satisfied after December 31, 2024)

2. **One of the following triggering events applies:**
   - Age 59½ or older
   - Death (distributions to beneficiaries)
   - Disability (permanently and totally disabled)
   - First-time home purchase (lifetime maximum $10,000)

If either condition is not met, the distribution is non-qualified. Non-qualified distributions use the ordering rules below to determine tax and penalty treatment.

**5-year clock note:** Each Roth IRA account does NOT have its own 5-year clock — once the first Roth IRA is opened anywhere, the 5-year holding period is met across all Roth IRAs. This means opening any Roth IRA early in one's career satisfies the 5-year rule for all future Roth IRAs.

**Roth 401(k) to Roth IRA rollover:** A Roth 401(k) rolled to a Roth IRA uses the Roth IRA 5-year clock, not the 401(k)'s separate clock. If the Roth IRA has been open 5+ years, the rollover satisfies the qualified distribution test immediately.

## Ordering Rules for Early Withdrawal (Pre-59½)

Roth IRA distributions before meeting the qualified distribution test are taxed and penalized based on a strict ordering of which dollars are considered withdrawn first:

**Layer 1 — Contributions (basis):** Always withdrawn first. No tax, no penalty, regardless of age or holding period. This is the most important planning tool — a Roth IRA owner can always access their actual contributions without consequence.

**Layer 2 — Conversions and rollover contributions:** Withdrawn second, in FIFO order (oldest conversions first). Principal is not subject to income tax (already taxed at conversion). However, each conversion has its own **5-year holding period** — if distributed before 5 years, the 10% early withdrawal penalty applies to that conversion amount (but NOT if the owner is already 59½ or meets another exception).

**Layer 3 — Earnings:** Withdrawn last. Subject to both income tax and the 10% penalty if distributed before meeting the qualified distribution test.

**Planning implication:** Roth IRA contributions make the account more liquid than many assume. A client who has contributed $42,000 over 6 years to a Roth IRA that has grown to $58,000 can withdraw up to $42,000 at any time without tax or penalty. Only the $16,000 of earnings is "locked" until age 59½ (or other qualifying event).

## Backdoor Roth IRA Mechanics

The backdoor Roth is the workaround for high earners who exceed the direct Roth IRA income limits.

**Steps:**
1. Make a non-deductible Traditional IRA contribution ($7,000 or $8,000 for age 50+)
2. Allow the contribution to settle (typically 1–5 business days)
3. Convert the Traditional IRA balance to Roth IRA

**Ideal scenario:** The conversion is tax-free if the only Traditional IRA balance is the just-contributed non-deductible amount (no pre-tax amounts in any Traditional IRA).

**Pro-Rata Rule:** This is the critical complication. When converting any portion of a Traditional IRA to Roth, the IRS looks at the **total value of all Traditional IRAs** (including SEP-IRA and SIMPLE IRA) at December 31 of the conversion year. The conversion is pro-rated between basis (after-tax) and pre-tax amounts.

**Example of pro-rata problem:** Client has $93,000 in a pre-tax rollover IRA from a former 401(k) and contributes $7,000 non-deductible. Total IRA value: $100,000. Basis: $7,000. Non-taxable percentage: 7%. Converting $7,000 to Roth means only $490 is tax-free (7%) and $6,510 is taxable. The $93,000 pre-tax IRA "contaminates" the backdoor Roth conversion.

**Solution to pro-rata rule:** Roll existing pre-tax IRA balances INTO a current employer 401(k) plan that accepts incoming rollovers. This removes the pre-tax IRA balance from the calculation. After the rollover, the only remaining Traditional IRA is the non-deductible contribution, and the conversion is tax-free.

**Form 8606:** Required for both the non-deductible contribution (Part I) and the conversion (Part II). This is how the IRS knows the conversion is not a full taxable event.

## Roth IRA for Estate Planning

Roth IRA is the preferred estate planning vehicle among retirement accounts for multiple reasons:

**No Required Minimum Distributions during owner's lifetime.** The owner can leave the entire Roth IRA untouched, allowing unlimited tax-free compounding. (Roth 401(k) had RMDs until SECURE 2.0 eliminated them starting 2024.)

**Passes to heirs income-tax-free.** Qualified distributions to beneficiaries carry no income tax. The account value is included in the estate for estate tax purposes, but the income tax benefit is a significant distinction from inherited Traditional IRA.

**10-year rule for non-spouse beneficiaries (SECURE Act):** Non-spouse beneficiaries who inherit a Roth IRA after January 1, 2020 must deplete the account within 10 years of the owner's death. However, there are no annual RMDs — they can take everything in year 10 if they choose, allowing 10 more years of tax-free compounding before the tax-free lump sum distribution.

**Eligible Designated Beneficiaries (EDBs)** — surviving spouse, minor children of the deceased owner, disabled/chronically ill individuals, and beneficiaries not more than 10 years younger than the owner — have more favorable options including lifetime stretch distributions.

**Roth IRA in a SLAT or trust:** High-net-worth families sometimes hold Roth IRAs in trust structures for asset protection and estate planning purposes, though the trust must be a "see-through trust" to allow the inherited IRA rules to apply to beneficiaries.

## Young Earner Strategy — Why Roth at Low Brackets

The Roth IRA's value is maximized when contributions are made at the **lowest available tax rate** and distributions occur decades later at potentially higher rates.

**The case for Roth at lower income:**
- A 25-year-old in the 22% bracket paying tax now vs. withdrawing at a potentially higher rate in 40 years
- $7,000 in a Roth IRA at 7% annual return for 40 years = $104,000 tax-free at age 65
- The same $7,000 in a Traditional IRA grows to $104,000 but is taxable at withdrawal

**Paying tax at 12%:** Clients in the 10% or 12% bracket have an especially compelling case for Roth — these are historically low federal rates. The 2017 TCJA rates are scheduled to sunset after 2025 (though legislative outcomes are uncertain). The 12% bracket covers taxable income up to $47,150 single / $94,300 MFJ in 2025.

**Roth IRA in a child's account:** Minors with earned income (from a job, self-employment, or legitimate family business work) can contribute to a Roth IRA up to their earned income amount (maximum $7,000). This is a powerful wealth transfer tool — a $7,000 contribution at age 16 at 7% growth for 50 years grows to approximately $206,000 tax-free.

## Roth Conversion vs. Direct Contribution

These are two distinct ways to fund a Roth IRA and are governed by different rules:

**Direct Contribution:**
- Subject to income phase-outs ($150k–$165k single, $236k–$246k MFJ)
- Requires earned income
- Counted against the $7,000/$8,000 annual contribution limit
- Always tax-free (already paid tax on the contributed dollars)

**Roth Conversion:**
- No income limits (anyone can convert regardless of income)
- Does NOT require earned income
- Does NOT count against the annual contribution limit
- The converted amount is included in ordinary income in the year of conversion
- No 10% penalty on conversion itself (but converted amounts have a 5-year holding period for penalty-free withdrawal before 59½)

**Strategic use of conversions:** Converting Traditional IRA or pre-tax 401(k) dollars to Roth during low-income years (early retirement before Social Security, gap years, business losses) "recharacterizes" future ordinary income into a tax-free asset. The goal is to pay tax at the lowest possible marginal rate today.

**Roth conversion ladder:** A strategy for early retirees who need Roth access before 59½. Convert a specific amount each year, allow 5 years to pass, then access those conversion dollars penalty-free (conversions are Layer 2 in the ordering rules — no tax on withdrawal, no penalty after 5 years).

## Backdoor Roth — Timing Considerations

**Same-year deduction coordination:** Making a non-deductible IRA contribution and immediately converting in the same tax year is clean — no pro-rata issue arises if there are no other IRA balances.

**Year-end IRA balance check:** The pro-rata rule uses December 31 balances. If a client rolls a pre-tax IRA to a 401(k) before December 31, the pro-rata issue is resolved for that year's conversion.

**Two-step backdoor in the same year:** Some custodians require a waiting period between contribution and conversion to avoid "step transaction" arguments from the IRS. The IRS has not formally challenged properly disclosed backdoor Roth transactions, but documenting each step and filing Form 8606 correctly is essential.
