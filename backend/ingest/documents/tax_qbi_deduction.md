# Qualified Business Income (QBI) Deduction — Section 199A

Source: IRS Publication 535, IRC §199A, IRS Form 8995 / 8995-A.

---

## What Is the QBI Deduction

Self-employed individuals, S-Corp shareholders, partnership partners, and certain trust/estate beneficiaries can deduct up to 20% of qualified business income from their federal taxable income. This is an above-the-line deduction — it reduces taxable income without itemizing.

The deduction was created by the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act. It is currently scheduled to expire December 31, 2025 unless extended by Congress.

**Who qualifies:**
- Sole proprietors (Schedule C)
- Single-member LLCs (Schedule C)
- Partnerships and multi-member LLCs (Schedule E)
- S-Corporation shareholders (Schedule E)
- Rental real estate (with active participation — see below)

**Who does NOT qualify:**
- C-Corporation shareholders (C-Corps have their own 21% flat rate)
- W-2 employees (no QBI on wages)

---

## Basic Calculation

Simple calculation (for income below phase-out thresholds):

QBI Deduction = 20% × Qualified Business Income

Qualified Business Income = net income from the business (revenue minus allowable business deductions). Does NOT include:
- W-2 wages paid to yourself as an S-Corp owner
- Capital gains or losses
- Interest income
- Dividends

**Example:** Sole proprietor with $150,000 Schedule C net income. QBI deduction = $150,000 × 20% = $30,000. Taxable income reduced by $30,000 before applying brackets.

## Phase-Out Thresholds (2025)

| Filing Status | Phase-Out Begins | Phase-Out Complete |
|--------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Single / HOH | $197,300 | $247,300 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $394,600 | $444,600 |
| Married Filing Separately | $197,300 | $247,300 |

Below the phase-out: full 20% deduction with no restrictions.

Above the phase-out: two limitations apply, and the deduction may be significantly reduced or eliminated for Specified Service Trades or Businesses (SSTBs).

---

## Specified Service Trades or Businesses (SSTBs)

SSTBs are professions where income depends primarily on the skill or reputation of the owner:
- Health: physicians, dentists, nurses, therapists, veterinarians
- Law: attorneys, paralegals
- Accounting: CPAs, bookkeepers
- Actuarial science
- Performing arts: actors, musicians
- Consulting: business consultants (but NOT engineers or architects)
- Athletics: professional athletes, coaches
- Financial services: investment managers, financial advisors, broker-dealers
- Brokerage services

**SSTB income above phase-out:** QBI deduction phases to zero. An attorney earning $500,000 MFJ gets no QBI deduction.

**SSTB income below phase-out:** Full 20% deduction. Phase-out applies to income between the thresholds.

Non-SSTBs (manufacturing, retail, real estate, technology, engineering, architecture) are not subject to SSTB elimination — they can still claim the deduction above phase-outs subject to the W-2/capital limits below.

---

## W-2 Wage and Capital Limitation (Above Phase-Out)

For non-SSTB businesses above the income threshold, the deduction is limited to the greater of:
- 50% of W-2 wages paid by the business, OR
- 25% of W-2 wages + 2.5% of unadjusted basis of qualified depreciable property

**Implications:**
- S-Corps: must pay reasonable W-2 wages to the owner-employee to support the QBI deduction
- Sole proprietors and partnerships: no W-2s → potential loss of deduction unless they have depreciable property
- Capital-intensive businesses (real estate, manufacturing): benefit from the property-based alternative

**Planning:** S-Corp owners should model optimal salary vs. distribution split — higher W-2 wages support QBI deduction but increase payroll taxes. Lower salary reduces payroll taxes but may limit QBI deduction above thresholds.

## Rental Real Estate and QBI

Rental income can qualify for QBI deduction if the activity rises to the level of a "trade or business." IRS Revenue Procedure 2019-38 provides a safe harbor:
- Maintain separate books and records for each rental activity
- Perform 250+ hours of rental services per year
- Keep contemporaneous time logs documenting the hours

Short-term rentals (average rental period ≤ 7 days) treated as a business by default (like a hotel). Long-term residential rentals: safe harbor documentation required.

Triple-net lease (NNN) rentals where landlord does nothing: generally NOT qualified as a trade or business.

## Planning Strategies

**Income bunching to stay below phase-out:**
If income fluctuates near the thresholds, bunch income or defer it to stay within the full-deduction zone in more years.

**S-Corp salary optimization:**
Below phase-out: salary doesn't matter for QBI (no limitation applies). Above phase-out: model salary level to maximize 50% W-2 wage limit while minimizing payroll taxes. IRS requires "reasonable compensation" — benchmark against industry data.

**Depreciable property basis:**
If you own significant real property, the 2.5% unadjusted basis alternative can be larger than the W-2 wage limit. Run both calculations.

**Retirement plan contributions reduce QBI:**
Self-employed health insurance and SEP-IRA/Solo 401(k) contributions reduce net SE income (and thus QBI). Counterintuitive: maximizing retirement contributions reduces the QBI deduction base.

**Multiple businesses:**
QBI is calculated separately for each business, then combined. Profitable businesses can be offset by loss businesses. Aggregation election available — grouping businesses may help exceed W-2/property thresholds.

## Key Interaction: QBI Deduction and Effective Tax Rate

A sole proprietor in the 22% bracket with $150,000 QBI effectively pays:
- Tax on $120,000 (after $30,000 QBI deduction)
- Effective QBI benefit = $30,000 × 22% = $6,600 saved
- Equivalent to a ~4.4% reduction in effective tax rate on that income

For high-income non-SSTB businesses in the 37% bracket: QBI deduction worth up to $37,000 × 37% = $13,690 per $100,000 QBI (subject to W-2 and capital limits).
