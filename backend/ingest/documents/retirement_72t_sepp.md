# Section 72(t) — Substantially Equal Periodic Payments (SEPP)

## What Is 72(t) SEPP?

Internal Revenue Code Section 72(t)(2)(A)(iv) provides an exception to the 10% early withdrawal penalty for IRA (and qualified plan) distributions before age 59½, provided the distributions are taken as part of a series of **Substantially Equal Periodic Payments (SEPP)**.

The SEPP strategy is used by early retirees, early career changers, and those facing financial hardship who need to access IRA funds before age 59½ without incurring the 10% penalty. The distribution is still fully taxable as ordinary income — 72(t) only removes the penalty, not the income tax.

This is a significant planning tool but comes with rigid commitment requirements. A mistake terminates the entire SEPP program retroactively.

## Three Calculation Methods

The IRS approves three methods for calculating SEPP payments. The same method must be used consistently throughout the SEPP program (with one limited exception described below).

### Method 1: Required Minimum Distribution (RMD) Method

**How it works:** Calculate the annual distribution using the same method as RMDs — divide the prior December 31 account balance by the applicable life expectancy factor from IRS tables.

**Key characteristic:** The payment amount is **recalculated each year** based on the current account balance and the updated life expectancy factor. This means payments go up in good market years and down in bad market years — the amount is not fixed.

**Result:** Produces the **lowest payments** of the three methods. Best for those who need some income but want to preserve as much IRA capital as possible.

**Practical example:** A 50-year-old with a $500,000 IRA. Using the Uniform Lifetime Table, life expectancy factor at 50 is approximately 38.61. First-year distribution: $500,000 ÷ 38.61 ≈ **$12,949**. In subsequent years, the denominator changes (uses updated account balance and updated factor).

### Method 2: Amortization Method

**How it works:** Calculate the annual distribution as if the IRA balance were a fixed-rate loan being amortized over the account owner's single or joint life expectancy (using IRS-prescribed mortality tables). The IRS-approved interest rate must be used.

**Key characteristic:** The payment is **fixed for the life of the SEPP program**. Once calculated, the dollar amount does not change (except for inflation adjustments in some cases).

**Result:** Produces the **highest payments** of the three methods (along with annuitization). Best for those who need maximum income from a 72(t) plan.

**Interest rate constraint:** The IRS limits the interest rate used in the amortization calculation to no more than **120% of the Federal Mid-Term Rate** for either of the two months immediately preceding the month of the first payment. The Federal Mid-Term Rate is published monthly in IRS Revenue Rulings. A higher allowable rate produces higher payments; when rates are low, payments are lower.

**Practical example:** Same 50-year-old with $500,000 IRA. Using a 4.5% assumed interest rate (hypothetical) and single life expectancy of 35 years. Amortized annual payment: approximately **$27,500–$30,000/year** (exact amount depends on precise rates and life expectancy table used).

### Method 3: Annuitization Method

**How it works:** Divide the account balance by an annuity factor derived from the IRS mortality table and the same interest rate constraint as the amortization method. The annuity factor is the present value of a $1 annuity paid for life beginning at the current age.

**Key characteristic:** Like amortization, the payment is **fixed**. The calculation is slightly different from amortization but produces similar (generally slightly lower) payment amounts.

**Result:** Payments are similar to amortization — high relative to RMD method. Less commonly used than amortization because the calculation is more complex and the payments are not meaningfully different.

## IRS Interest Rate Constraint

For both amortization and annuitization methods, the maximum interest rate that can be used is **120% of the applicable Federal Mid-Term Rate** for either:
- The month in which the first payment is made, OR
- Either of the two months immediately preceding the first payment month

The practitioner chooses whichever of these three months produces the most favorable result. When the Federal Mid-Term Rate is rising, using the prior month's higher rate may produce higher payments. When rates are falling, using the current month's rate produces higher payments.

The Federal Mid-Term Rate is published by the IRS each month (Revenue Ruling format). As of 2025, rates are in the 4–5% range, which produces relatively higher allowable SEPP payments compared to the 2020–2021 near-zero rate environment.

## Commitment Rules — The Most Critical Element

SEPP payments **must continue for the longer of:**
1. **Five years** from the date of the first payment
2. **Until the account owner reaches age 59½**

These conditions are evaluated independently and the longer one controls.

**Examples:**
- Person starts SEPP at age 50: Must continue until age 59½ (9 years) — the 5-year condition is shorter.
- Person starts SEPP at age 57: Must continue until age 62 (5 years) — the 5-year condition is longer than the 59½ threshold.
- Person starts SEPP at age 56: Must continue until age 61 (5 years > age 59½).
- Person starts SEPP at age 55: Both conditions require the same endpoint (59½ = approximately 4½ years, but the 5-year rule controls slightly, meaning continue to age 60).

## The Modification Rule — Catastrophic Consequences

Stopping, modifying the amount, or otherwise changing the SEPP schedule before the program completes triggers a **retroactive recapture of the 10% penalty on ALL prior distributions** in the SEPP program, plus interest from the date each distribution was made.

**This is not just a going-forward penalty.** If a 50-year-old has been taking $25,000/year for 7 years ($175,000 total) and modifies the plan in year 8 (one year before the required 9-year completion), the IRS will assess 10% on all $175,000 = $17,500 in penalties plus interest on each year's penalty from the original distribution date. This can easily amount to $25,000–$30,000 in penalties and interest.

**What constitutes modification:**
- Stopping distributions
- Taking extra distributions beyond the calculated amount
- Rolling over any portion of the SEPP IRA
- Depositing new contributions into the SEPP IRA
- Changing the calculation method (other than the one permitted exception)

**Death or disability:** If the account owner dies or becomes disabled during the SEPP program, the program ends without triggering the modification penalty.

## The One Permitted Method Change

The IRS allows account owners who used the amortization or annuitization method to **switch to the RMD method once** during the SEPP program. This is the only permitted modification.

**When used:** If the account has declined significantly in value and the fixed amortization/annuitization payment would deplete the account prematurely, switching to the variable RMD method reduces payments and preserves the account. This is a one-way switch — cannot switch back to amortization.

## IRA Segmentation Strategy

One of the most important planning techniques for 72(t) SEPP is **splitting the IRA before starting the program**.

**The problem:** The SEPP locks the entire IRA used for the calculation. If a 50-year-old has $800,000 in an IRA but only needs $20,000/year in early distributions, they don't need to run the SEPP on the full $800,000 (which might require distributing $35,000+ per year under any calculation method).

**The solution:** Before initiating the SEPP, split the IRA into two IRAs:
- **SEPP IRA:** Sized exactly to produce the desired distribution amount under the chosen calculation method (e.g., $350,000 sized to produce $20,000/year)
- **Non-SEPP IRA:** Remaining balance ($450,000) stays untouched, grows freely, and can be accessed normally after age 59½

The split is a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer, which is not a taxable event and does not count as a modification. The key is to complete the split BEFORE the first SEPP distribution is taken.

**Example sizing:** To generate $20,000/year using amortization at 4.5% for a 50-year-old (35-year life expectancy), the required account balance is approximately $400,000. Instead of running SEPP on $800,000 and being forced to take $40,000/year (or more), split off $400,000 into the SEPP IRA and keep $400,000 in a separate IRA.

## Practical Use Cases

**Early retiree bridge strategy:** A 55-year-old who retires and has a pension starting at 62 and Social Security at 67 needs 7 years of IRA income. SEPP provides a structured way to access IRA funds without penalty, with the program terminating at age 60 (5-year rule). Can be sized to replace needed income.

**Bridge to 401(k) access:** 401(k) plans have a penalty-free separation exception at age 55 (rule of 55). If the retiree is under 55, a 72(t) on a rolled-over IRA can bridge to age 55 when the 401(k) exception applies.

**Healthcare coverage gap:** Early retiree needs IRA income to fund health insurance premiums and living expenses before Medicare at 65. SEPP provides structured penalty-free access.

**Coordination with Social Security delay:** Client retires at 60, wants to delay Social Security to 70. SEPP on IRA from 60–65 provides income bridge without penalty while allowing Social Security to grow at 8% per year.

## Documentation and Filing

The IRS does not require advance approval for a SEPP program. However, proper documentation is critical:

- Maintain a written SEPP schedule showing the calculation method, interest rate used, life expectancy table used, and the required payment amount
- Keep documentation of IRS interest rates used (Revenue Rulings)
- File the distribution correctly on Form 1040: the 1099-R will show Code 1 (early distribution), but the taxpayer claims the 72(t) exception on Form 5329, entering exception code "02" to avoid the penalty

**IRS private letter rulings:** For non-standard situations, an IRS private letter ruling (PLR) can be requested to confirm the SEPP qualifies. PLRs are expensive ($10,000+) and slow (12–18 months) but provide certainty. Most advisors rely on published IRS guidance and Revenue Rulings rather than PLRs for standard SEPP arrangements.
