# Withdrawal Sequencing in Retirement

## The Classic Order — and Why It's Often Wrong

Financial planning textbooks traditionally recommend this withdrawal sequence in retirement:

1. Taxable (brokerage) accounts first
2. Tax-deferred (Traditional IRA, 401k) second
3. Tax-free (Roth IRA) last

The logic: preserve tax-free Roth growth as long as possible, and defer taxes on traditional accounts. This is directionally correct but far too rigid for optimal outcomes.

**Why the classic order is incomplete:**

- It ignores tax bracket management — the difference between spending $5,000 and $50,000 from a Traditional IRA in a given year can be tens of thousands of dollars in avoidable lifetime taxes
- It ignores RMDs, which force traditional IRA withdrawals starting at age 73/75 regardless of preference
- It ignores Medicare IRMAA surcharges triggered by higher income in prior years
- It ignores Social Security taxation cliffs that make marginal rates effectively higher in certain income bands
- It ignores ACA subsidy cliffs for retirees before Medicare eligibility

## The Dynamic Withdrawal Framework

Effective withdrawal sequencing is an annual tax-bracket-management exercise, not a fixed order. Each year, ask:

1. **What is my effective taxable income from non-discretionary sources?** (Social Security, RMDs, pension, dividends/interest in taxable account)
2. **What is my current marginal bracket and how much room remains before the next bracket?**
3. **Can I do Roth conversions in the remaining bracket space?**
4. **Which accounts do I draw from to meet spending needs while minimizing current and future tax?**

The answer changes every year based on Social Security claiming, RMD amounts, market performance, and life circumstances.

## Taxable Account Withdrawals — Advantages and Considerations

**Spend existing taxable income first.** Dividends, interest, and short-term capital gains in a taxable account are already generating taxable income whether or not they are spent. Consuming these proceeds first is tax-neutral — the income exists regardless.

**Tax-loss harvesting opportunity.** Taxable accounts allow ongoing tax-loss harvesting — selling positions at a loss to offset realized gains, potentially reducing the tax cost of rebalancing or spending withdrawals.

**No RMDs.** Taxable accounts impose no mandatory withdrawal requirements. The account can grow indefinitely with only annual tax drag from income and realized gains.

**Stepped-up basis at death.** Assets held in a taxable account at death receive a step-up in cost basis to the fair market value on the date of death. Heirs inherit the asset with zero embedded capital gain. This makes taxable accounts excellent assets to hold until death if the client has long-term hold positions with large embedded gains. Spending down the account defeats this benefit.

**ACA consideration (pre-Medicare).** Withdrawals from taxable accounts generate income: interest, dividends, and realized capital gains. However, returning principal (selling assets at a gain basis close to purchase price) may generate little additional income. This is relevant when managing MAGI for ACA premium tax credit eligibility.

## Tax-Deferred (Traditional IRA / 401k) Withdrawals

**RMD coordination is mandatory.** Starting at age 73 or 75, required minimum distributions from Traditional IRAs and 401(k)s must be taken regardless of the client's preference. This sets a floor on taxable income from these accounts in later retirement.

**Voluntary pre-RMD withdrawals to fill lower brackets.** In the years between retirement and RMD age, many clients are in an unexpectedly low-income window. Voluntarily withdrawing from Traditional IRA to fill the 10% and 12% brackets — and doing Roth conversions with the remaining bracket space — can significantly reduce future RMD amounts and tax burden.

**Beware of bracket stacking.** In later retirement, the combination of Social Security (up to 85% taxable), RMDs, and other income can stack into the 22–32% bracket. Proactive conversions earlier prevent this compression.

## Roth IRA Withdrawals — Last, But Not Just For Longevity

**Primary reasons to preserve Roth IRA:**

- **Tax-free growth indefinitely** — no RMDs, no tax drag, compounds fully
- **Longevity insurance** — if the client lives to 95, later years with higher healthcare costs or required care expenses can be funded from Roth without pushing income into higher brackets
- **Estate asset** — Roth passes income-tax-free to heirs (subject to 10-year rule for non-spouse beneficiaries)
- **Medicare IRMAA buffer** — Roth withdrawals do not count toward MAGI for IRMAA purposes. In years with unusually high income needs, Roth can be tapped without triggering higher Medicare premiums

**When to use Roth earlier:** In years where MAGI is already high (large Roth conversion year, capital gains event, large RMD year), Roth distributions are preferable to adding more Traditional IRA income on top of an already elevated income.

## Roth Conversions as an Active Withdrawal Strategy

Roth conversions are not just a planning exercise for younger clients — they are an ongoing withdrawal sequencing tool in early retirement.

**The conversion opportunity window:**
- After retirement, before Social Security begins: often the lowest-income years of late adulthood
- After Social Security begins, before RMDs: still potentially low-income depending on SS amount
- Before age 73/75 when RMDs will force distributions

**Goal:** In each year of the conversion window, determine how much Traditional IRA can be converted to Roth while staying within the target bracket (often filling to the top of the 22% or 24% bracket). The converted amount pays tax now at the current rate, permanently removes that amount from future RMD calculations, and shifts it to tax-free Roth status.

**Example:** Retiree at 65 has $1.2M in Traditional IRA, $200K in Roth, $300K in taxable. Social Security begins at 70. In years 65–70, income is low — perhaps $30,000 from dividends and part-time work. The 24% bracket tops out at $201,050 (single, 2025). After standard deduction ($15,000), the client can convert approximately $156,050 in this bracket. Each year of conversion reduces future RMDs substantially.

## Medicare IRMAA — The Hidden Withdrawal Tax

Medicare Part B and Part D premiums include an Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount (IRMAA) surcharge for higher-income beneficiaries. The surcharge is based on MAGI from **two years prior**.

**2025 IRMAA thresholds (Part B standard premium = $185/month):**

| 2023 MAGI (Single) | 2023 MAGI (MFJ) | Monthly Premium Surcharge |
|---|---|---|
| ≤ $106,000 | ≤ $212,000 | $0 (standard $185) |
| $106,001–$133,000 | $212,001–$266,000 | +$74/month |
| $133,001–$167,000 | $266,001–$334,000 | +$186/month |
| $167,001–$200,000 | $334,001–$400,000 | +$278/month |
| $200,001–$500,000 | $400,001–$750,000 | +$370/month |
| Above $500,000 | Above $750,000 | +$443/month |

**Planning implication:** A large Roth conversion or one-time capital gain event today creates a two-year lookback window of elevated Medicare premiums. This cost should be factored into the net benefit of the conversion. Roth conversions that push MAGI just over an IRMAA threshold for one year may cost $1,800–$5,000+ in additional premiums in year+2.

## ACA Subsidy Coordination (Pre-Medicare, Ages 60–64)

Retirees who retire before Medicare eligibility at 65 often purchase health insurance through the ACA marketplace. Premium tax credit eligibility is based on MAGI as a percentage of the federal poverty level.

**Key characteristic of Roth withdrawals:** Roth IRA withdrawals (of contributions) do NOT increase MAGI. They are tax-free and do not appear on the tax return. This is a significant advantage for retirees managing ACA subsidy eligibility.

**Traditional IRA withdrawals** increase MAGI dollar-for-dollar. A client managing MAGI below 400% of the poverty level (approximately $58,000 for single, $78,000 for couple in 2025) should draw from Roth instead of Traditional IRA when ACA subsidies are a priority.

**Social Security:** Only the taxable portion of SS benefits increases MAGI for ACA purposes. Clients who have not yet claimed SS have no SS income counted in MAGI.

## Withdrawal Rate and Account Depletion Sequencing

When multiple account types exist, the order of depletion affects estate outcomes significantly:

**Spending taxable first, Roth last** means the taxable account may be fully depleted before death (no stepped-up basis benefit needed if balance is zero), while the Roth IRA grows untouched for decades and passes income-tax-free to heirs.

**If estate taxes are a concern (estates above ~$13.6M federal exemption in 2025):** Traditional IRA assets are subject to income tax by beneficiaries plus estate tax by the estate — double taxation. Roth IRA assets in the estate are still subject to estate tax but not to income tax on distributions. Converting Traditional to Roth reduces the income-tax component of the double-tax problem for large estates.

## Practical Annual Process

For each retirement client each year:

1. Project all non-discretionary income: RMDs (calculate exact amount), taxable Social Security, pension, dividends/interest
2. Determine spending need vs. non-discretionary income gap
3. Identify bracket position and remaining room in current bracket
4. Determine whether Roth conversion opportunity exists above spending need
5. Source spending need from most tax-efficient account given IRMAA, ACA, and bracket considerations
6. Execute conversions up to target bracket if applicable
7. Harvest losses in taxable account before year-end if gains were realized
