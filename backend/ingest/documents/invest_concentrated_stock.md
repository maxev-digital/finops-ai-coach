# Concentrated Stock Positions: Risk Management and Diversification Strategies

## Defining Concentration Risk

A concentrated stock position generally means more than 10% of your investable assets in a single security. In practice, many professionals find themselves with 30-80% in a single stock through:
- RSUs (Restricted Stock Units) that vest and accumulate
- Incentive stock options (ISOs) that have been exercised and held
- Employee Stock Purchase Plan (ESPP) shares
- Employer stock in a 401(k) company match
- Inherited stock with a large unrealized gain
- Founding shares in a company that went public or grew significantly

The risk is not abstract. A single company can lose 50%, 80%, or 100% of its value. Enron, Lehman Brothers, WorldCom, Kodak, Sears, and countless other former blue-chip companies devastated employees who held concentrated positions. In each case, employees suffered a dual blow: loss of employment AND destruction of accumulated wealth simultaneously. This double exposure — job security and financial security tied to the same company — makes employer stock concentration uniquely dangerous.

## Strategies to Reduce Concentration

### 1. Outright Sale (Simplest, Most Tax-Costly)

The simplest approach: sell the stock, pay the capital gains tax, invest the proceeds in a diversified portfolio.

- **Advantage**: Immediate, complete diversification; simple execution
- **Disadvantage**: Triggers capital gains tax (15-23.8% federal for long-term gains; additional state tax; potentially 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax)
- **Appropriate when**: Tax cost is modest (low basis but small position); need for liquidity; tax rates expected to rise; the concentration risk justifies paying the tax immediately

**Systematic selling**: If the tax hit of selling all at once is prohibitive, sell a fixed amount each year. Prioritize selling in years when other income is lower (early retirement, income dip) to fill lower capital gains tax brackets.

### 2. Charitable Donation of Appreciated Stock

Donate appreciated shares directly to a charity or Donor-Advised Fund (DAF) rather than selling.

- **Advantage**: Avoid capital gains entirely on donated shares; deduct the full fair market value at time of donation; double tax benefit vs. selling and donating cash
- **Mechanism**: Transfer shares to the charity or DAF in-kind; the charity sells at full market price and pays no capital gains; you deduct the FMV on Schedule A
- **Limit**: Charitable deduction for appreciated property to public charities is limited to 30% of AGI; excess carries forward 5 years
- **Best implementation**: DAF (Donor-Advised Fund) allows you to donate shares in one year (taking the deduction), then distribute grants to charities over subsequent years
- **Charitable remainder trust (CRT)**: Transfer shares to CRT, receive income stream for life, remainder to charity; avoid capital gains on transfer; income tax deduction for present value of charitable remainder

### 3. Exchange Fund (Pooled Diversification)

An exchange fund allows an investor to contribute concentrated shares and receive a pro-rata interest in a diversified fund containing the contributions of other concentrated-position investors.

- **Tax treatment**: Not a taxable event if properly structured; the investor receives the same cost basis in the fund units as they had in the contributed stock
- **Holding requirement**: Must hold fund interest for at least 7 years (IRS requirement)
- **Diversification**: Fund typically holds 20-30 different concentrated positions from different contributors
- **Limitation**: Very illiquid (7-year lock-up minimum); minimum investment typically $1M-$5M; limited to accredited investors; fund structure allows 20% in "qualified assets" (real estate, bonds) — limits pure equity diversification
- **Appropriate for**: Large concentrated positions, very low basis, long time horizon, investor who can afford the illiquidity

### 4. Protective Put Options

Buy put options on the concentrated stock to cap the downside without triggering a taxable sale.

- **Mechanism**: A put option gives the right to sell shares at a fixed strike price; if the stock falls below the strike, the put offsets the loss
- **Cost**: Option premiums are the cost of protection; annual cost can be 2-5% of position value or more depending on volatility
- **Tax consideration**: Under IRC §1092, buying a put may "toll" the holding period — if you bought puts on stock held <12 months, the holding period pauses; important for ISO holding period requirements
- **Limitation**: Expensive; ongoing cost; does not eliminate the unrealized gain; requires rolling as options expire
- **Appropriate for**: Short-term protection during a vesting/blackout period; insider who cannot sell; temporary hedge while other strategies are implemented

### 5. Prepaid Variable Forward (PVF)

A complex derivative strategy where an investor agrees to sell a range of shares at a future date in exchange for receiving cash (a loan advance) today.

- **Mechanism**: The investor receives 75-85% of the stock's current value as a tax-free loan; at the settlement date (typically 2-5 years), delivers a variable number of shares to the bank depending on the stock's price
- **Tax deferral**: The initial advance is not taxable because it is structured as a loan; taxes are owed at settlement
- **Diversification**: The cash advance can be invested in a diversified portfolio immediately
- **Downside**: Complex; bank counterparty risk; gains are deferred but not eliminated; IRS has challenged certain constructions as anticipatory taxable sales; requires legal and tax counsel
- **Appropriate for**: Large concentrated positions ($5M+), low-basis stock, high bracket, desire to diversify now while deferring tax

### 6. Employer Stock in 401(k): Net Unrealized Appreciation (NUA)

If you have highly appreciated employer stock in a 401(k), the NUA strategy may allow you to pay capital gains rates rather than ordinary income rates on the appreciation. See the NUA strategy document for full details.

### 7. 10b5-1 Automatic Selling Plan

For corporate insiders (officers, directors, employees with access to material non-public information), SEC Rule 10b5-1 allows a pre-established, automatic trading plan for selling shares.

- **Requirements**: Plan must be established when the insider does not possess material non-public information; plan must specify the amount, price, and timing of sales in advance; typically a cooling-off period of 90-120 days before first sale
- **Benefit**: Provides affirmative defense against insider trading allegations because the decision to sell was made in advance, without knowledge of material information
- **Limitation**: Requires careful setup; SEC has increased scrutiny and added restrictions (Rule 10b5-1 amendments 2023); cannot cancel or modify a plan based on MNPI

### 8. Rule 144 Selling Limits for Affiliates

For company affiliates (officers, directors, 10%+ shareholders), SEC Rule 144 limits the volume of shares that can be sold in any 90-day period:
- Volume limited to greater of: 1% of outstanding shares, or the average weekly trading volume over the past 4 weeks
- Filing requirements: Form 4 for insiders within 2 business days of a transaction
- Holding period: 6 months for restricted securities in a public company

## The Behavioral Obstacle

The most common reason concentrated positions are not diversified is emotional, not mathematical. Employees develop deep loyalty and attachment to the company that employed them and feel that selling stock is somehow disloyal or pessimistic. Stock-based compensation creates an illusion of special knowledge — "I know this company better than outside investors, so I should hold it."

The research-supported response:
- The employee may know more about operations, but the market often prices in information that employees discount or emotionally filter
- Knowing a company well is not the same as knowing its stock price will appreciate
- Diversification is not a statement about the company's quality; it is a statement about risk management
- Systematic, pre-committed selling plans (like a 10b5-1 plan or a written policy to sell X shares per quarter) reduce the emotional friction by making the decision algorithmic rather than active

## Summary: Choosing a Strategy

| Strategy | Tax Efficiency | Complexity | Minimum Size | Liquidity |
|----------|---------------|------------|--------------|-----------|
| Outright sale | Low | Low | Any | Immediate |
| Systematic selling | Medium | Low | Any | Annual |
| Charitable donation | High | Low-Medium | Any | Permanent gift |
| Exchange fund | Very high | High | $1M+ | 7-year lockup |
| Protective put | Medium | Medium | $100K+ | Annual renewal |
| Prepaid variable forward | High | Very high | $5M+ | Delayed (2-5 yr) |
| 10b5-1 plan | Low | Medium | Any | Pre-scheduled |

For most employees with moderate concentration ($100K-$2M in a single stock), the practical choices are: systematic annual selling, charitable donation via DAF, or (for insiders) a 10b5-1 plan. For large positions with very low basis, exchange funds or prepaid variable forwards are worth professional evaluation.
