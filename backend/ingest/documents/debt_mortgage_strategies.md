# Mortgage Strategies: Refinancing, Payoff, and Home Equity

## Mortgage Fundamentals

A mortgage is an amortizing loan secured by real property. The monthly payment covers principal repayment, interest, property taxes (escrowed), and homeowner's insurance (escrowed) — the PITI payment.

**Amortization reality**: In the early years of a 30-year mortgage, nearly all of each payment is interest. On a $400,000 mortgage at 6.5%, the first payment is approximately $87 in principal and $2,167 in interest. By month 360 (year 30), the final payment is almost entirely principal. This front-loaded interest structure means that the first years of homeownership provide little equity buildup through principal paydown.

**30-year vs. 15-year mortgage**:
- 30-year: Lower monthly payment, higher total interest paid, longer to build equity
- 15-year: Higher monthly payment, substantially less total interest, faster equity buildup, lower interest rate (typically 0.5-0.75% lower than 30-year)
- Example: $400,000 at 6.5% (30-year): $2,528/month. Same loan at 5.75% (15-year): $3,321/month. Total interest: $510,000 (30-year) vs. $198,000 (15-year) — $312,000 savings with the 15-year

Choosing 15-year vs. 30-year depends on cash flow flexibility requirements and the opportunity cost of the additional monthly payment.

## Mortgage Refinance Analysis

Refinancing replaces an existing mortgage with a new loan, typically at a lower interest rate, different term, or to access equity.

**Break-even calculation**:
Break-even (months) = Total Closing Costs / Monthly Payment Savings

If refinancing from 7% to 5.5% on a $400,000 mortgage:
- Monthly payment savings: approximately $390/month
- Closing costs: $6,000
- Break-even: 6,000 / 390 = 15.4 months

If you will remain in the home and keep the loan for more than 15-16 months, refinancing makes financial sense. If you plan to move within a year or two, the closing costs are not recovered.

**Considerations that complicate the break-even**:
- Resetting the amortization clock: If you have a 7-year-old 30-year mortgage and refinance to a new 30-year, you extend repayment by 7 years. Compare total interest paid over the remaining life of both scenarios, not just the monthly payment savings.
- Tax deductibility: Closing costs are generally not immediately deductible but may be amortized over the loan term for itemizers.

## Rate and Term Refinance vs. Cash-Out Refinance

**Rate and term refinance**: Change the interest rate, loan term, or both without extracting equity. The loan balance after refinance equals the remaining balance (plus closing costs if rolled in). Goal: lower monthly payment or total interest.

**Cash-out refinance**: Borrow more than the existing loan balance; receive the difference in cash. Example: Home worth $500,000, current mortgage balance $300,000, refinance to $380,000 — receive $80,000 cash after paying off the old mortgage.

**Cash-out considerations**:
- Rate: Cash-out refinances typically carry a slightly higher interest rate than rate-and-term refinances
- Risk: You are increasing secured debt; if home values decline, equity cushion shrinks
- Tax deductibility: Interest on cash-out proceeds is only deductible if used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the loan (post-TCJA rule); cash-out used for vacation, debt consolidation, or investment is NOT deductible

## Extra Principal Payments: When They Make Sense

Paying extra principal is a guaranteed, after-tax return equal to your mortgage interest rate. Whether this is the best use of extra money depends on alternatives:

**Always do first**:
1. Capture any employer 401(k) match — this is a 50-100% immediate return
2. Pay off high-interest debt (credit cards at 18%+ are a guaranteed 18%+ return when paid off)
3. Max out HSA (triple tax advantage)
4. Emergency fund (3-6 months of expenses)

**Then decide between extra mortgage payments and additional investing**:
- Mortgage at 6.5% (guaranteed after-tax return) vs. expected stock market return (~8-10% long-run but uncertain)
- For most people with a tax-deferred 401k and taxable account, extra investing beats extra mortgage payments at rates below 6-7%
- For rates above 7%: extra mortgage payments become more compelling vs. uncertain equity returns

**The mortgage interest deduction reality**: Post-TCJA, the standard deduction is $30,000 for MFJ (2025). Most homeowners don't itemize — meaning they receive NO tax deduction for mortgage interest. This makes the effective after-tax cost of the mortgage equal to the stated rate. A 6.5% mortgage costs 6.5% after-tax if you take the standard deduction.

For high-income homeowners who do itemize, the mortgage interest deduction reduces the effective cost: 6.5% × (1 − 0.35) = 4.2% effective rate. In this case, the case for investing over paying off the mortgage strengthens considerably.

## Fixed vs. Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARMs)

**Fixed-rate mortgage**:
- Rate and payment certain for the life of the loan
- Best for homeowners planning to stay long-term (7+ years)
- Higher initial rate than ARM but no reset risk

**Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM)**:
- Initial fixed period (5/1 ARM = fixed 5 years, adjusts annually thereafter; 7/1 = fixed 7 years)
- Lower initial rate than equivalent fixed-rate mortgage (ARM discounts have been smaller in recent high-rate environment)
- After fixed period: rate adjusts based on index (typically SOFR) plus a margin
- Adjustment caps: most ARMs have periodic caps (2% per adjustment) and lifetime caps (5-6% over initial rate)
- Best for: homeowners with a defined timeline who will sell or refinance before the adjustment period; investor properties with planned short-term hold

**Refinancing risk with ARMs**: If rates rise significantly before the adjustment period, you may face higher rates AND refinancing into a higher-rate environment — potentially the worst of both worlds.

## Mortgage Points and Rate Buydowns

**Discount points**: Upfront fees paid to reduce the mortgage interest rate. One point = 1% of the loan amount.

**Break-even analysis for points** (same concept as refinancing):
- 1 point on $400,000 = $4,000 upfront cost
- Rate reduction from 1 point: typically 0.25%
- Monthly payment savings: approximately $60/month
- Break-even: $4,000 / $60 = 67 months (5.5 years)

Points are worth buying if you will keep the loan longer than the break-even. Points paid on a primary residence purchase mortgage are generally deductible in the year of purchase for itemizers.

**Seller-paid temporary buydowns**: In purchase negotiations (especially in high-rate environments), sellers may offer to "buy down" the buyer's rate temporarily:
- 2-1 buydown: Rate is 2% below market in Year 1, 1% below market in Year 2, market rate in Year 3 onward
- Cost to seller: approximately the sum of monthly savings during the buydown period
- Benefit to buyer: reduced payment in early years, useful when cash flow is tight at purchase

## PMI: Private Mortgage Insurance

Conventional loans with less than 20% down payment require PMI. PMI protects the lender (not you) against default losses.

**PMI cost**: Typically 0.5-1.5% of the loan amount annually (varies by down payment, credit score, loan type).

**Example**: $400,000 home, 10% down ($40,000), loan $360,000. PMI at 0.8% = $2,880/year ($240/month). This adds meaningfully to the effective mortgage cost.

**Eliminating PMI**:
- **Automatic cancellation**: Lenders must automatically cancel PMI when the loan balance reaches 78% of the original purchase price (based on the original amortization schedule — not current home value)
- **Request cancellation**: You can request cancellation when the loan balance reaches 80% of the original purchase price (request in writing; lender may require an appraisal if you're relying on appreciation)
- **Refinance**: If home value has increased significantly, refinancing into a new loan at <80% LTV eliminates PMI from the new loan
- **FHA loans**: FHA mortgage insurance premiums work differently — for loans with low down payments, MIP can last for the life of the loan and must be eliminated by refinancing into a conventional loan

## Reverse Mortgage (HECM)

A Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM) allows homeowners age 62 or older to access home equity without monthly mortgage payments. The loan grows over time and is repaid when the homeowner sells, moves out permanently, or dies.

**Key features**:
- Non-recourse: You can never owe more than the home's value
- No monthly payments required (property taxes and insurance must still be paid)
- Available as a lump sum, monthly payments, or line of credit
- Must live in the home as primary residence

**Use in retirement financial planning**:
- **Standby line of credit**: Open a HECM line of credit at 62, don't draw on it. The unused credit line grows at the loan's interest rate — providing a larger future borrowing capacity. During sequence of returns risk (early retirement market downturn), draw from the HECM instead of selling equities at depressed prices. Repay the HECM line when markets recover.
- **Bridge to delayed Social Security**: Use reverse mortgage proceeds from ages 62-70 to delay Social Security, then repay with SS income

**Costs**: HECM upfront fees are significant — HUD mortgage insurance premium (2% of home value), origination fee, closing costs. HUD requires a counseling session with an approved counselor before proceeding. Total upfront cost can be $10,000-$20,000+.

**Who should consult HUD counselor**: Anyone seriously considering a HECM should work with a HUD-approved housing counselor before proceeding — the counselor explains all options and implications.
