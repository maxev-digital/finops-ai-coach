# Life Insurance: Term vs. Permanent — Matching Coverage to Need

## Term Life Insurance

Term life insurance provides a death benefit for a defined period (the "term") at a level premium. It has no cash value component — it is pure mortality protection. If you die during the term, beneficiaries receive the death benefit. If you survive the term, the coverage ends (or continues at sharply higher rates).

### Term Periods Available
- 10-year term: lowest initial premium; appropriate for shorter-horizon needs (debt payoff, business protection)
- 15-year term: moderate cost; covers education funding period for young children
- 20-year term: most popular; covers the full mortgage and income replacement period for families in peak earning years
- 30-year term: highest cost; covers the full working career for young families; locks in rates while young and healthy

### Who Term Insurance Is For
Term is appropriate for virtually all families with:
- A working spouse whose income would need to be replaced
- A mortgage or other debt that would burden survivors
- Minor children who depend on parental income
- A business partner whose death would require a buyout

The cost advantage of term over permanent insurance for the same death benefit is substantial — often 5-10× cheaper per dollar of death benefit for healthy, younger applicants. The money not spent on higher permanent insurance premiums can be invested.

## Permanent Life Insurance Types

### Whole Life Insurance

Provides a guaranteed death benefit that remains in force for the insured's entire life as long as premiums are paid. Features:
- **Guaranteed cash value growth**: Cash value grows at a contractually guaranteed minimum rate
- **Participating vs. non-participating**: Participating whole life policies from mutual insurers (Guardian, Mass Mutual, Penn Mutual, New York Life) may pay dividends — an annual distribution of surplus that can be taken as cash, reduce premiums, or purchase additional "paid-up" coverage. Dividends are not guaranteed but have been paid consistently for over 100 years by top mutual carriers.
- **Fixed, level premium**: Premium is set at issue and does not change
- **Permanent death benefit**: As long as you pay premiums, coverage remains; no expiration

**Whole life cash value** grows modestly (typically 3-5% in dividend-paying whole life policies) and is accessible via policy loans or surrender. Policy loans are not subject to income tax (as long as the policy remains in force) — a tax-free liquidity feature.

### Universal Life Insurance (UL)

More flexible than whole life:
- **Adjustable premiums**: Can pay more in good years, less in difficult years (within limits set by the insurer)
- **Adjustable death benefit**: Can decrease (or sometimes increase) the death benefit
- **Cash value earns interest**: Based on a declared crediting rate (often tied to a minimum guaranteed rate with an interest bonus)
- **Lapse risk**: If the cash value is insufficient to cover the cost of insurance (which increases with age), the policy can lapse — leaving the insured without coverage. UL policies have lapsed on policyholders who underpaid premiums for years, assuming the policy was "funded."

### Variable Universal Life Insurance (VUL)

Like universal life but the cash value is invested in sub-accounts similar to mutual funds:
- Cash value performance is tied to market returns (upside and downside)
- Higher potential growth than whole life or UL but with market risk
- Requires an investment prospectus — treated as securities product
- Higher internal charges than a comparable term + investment portfolio strategy
- Risk: poor market performance depletes cash value, causing policy lapse

### Indexed Universal Life Insurance (IUL)

Cash value is credited based on the performance of a market index (S&P 500, for example) with a "participation rate," a "cap" (maximum credit), and a "floor" (minimum credit — typically 0%, protecting against market losses):
- **Example**: 100% participation in S&P 500 up to a 9% cap; floor of 0% (no loss in a down year)
- **Complexity**: Cap rates, participation rates, and crediting methods vary widely by insurer and can be changed by the insurer. What is sold as a 9% cap today may be reduced to 5% in a low-rate environment.
- **Marketing scrutiny**: IUL illustrations often show aggressive assumed returns that may not reflect realistic policy performance; regulators have imposed illustration standards

## How Much Life Insurance Do You Need?

Several methods to size the death benefit:

### DIME Method
- **D**ebt: Total outstanding debt (mortgage, student loans, car loans, credit cards)
- **I**ncome replacement: Annual income × 10-12 years of income replacement
- **M**ortgage: Payoff of mortgage (if not already included in Debt)
- **E**ducation: Estimated future education costs for each child

Sum these four components for the total coverage need.

### Human Life Value Approach
Present value of future after-tax earnings until retirement, discounted at a risk-free rate. More complex but captures the true economic value of the insured's life to their family.

### Financial Planner Rule of Thumb
10-12× annual gross income as a starting point. For a family with $150,000 annual income, a $1.5M-$1.8M policy is the baseline; adjust for specific debts, lifestyle, and dependents.

## The Laddering Approach

Rather than a single large term policy, purchase multiple policies with different term lengths to match the declining life insurance need over time:

- **30-year term, $1M**: Covers income replacement for the full working career
- **20-year term, $500K**: Additional coverage during the peak obligation years (children young, mortgage large)
- **10-year term, $250K**: Additional coverage while children are in high school and early college

As obligations decrease (mortgage is paid down, children become independent), the shorter-term policies expire and premiums drop naturally. This laddering approach typically costs less in total premiums than buying one large 30-year policy while providing more coverage during the years it is most needed.

## When Permanent Insurance Is Appropriate

After exhausting all tax-advantaged investment accounts and after appropriate term insurance is in place, permanent insurance may be appropriate for:

**Estate liquidity needs**: An illiquid estate (real estate, closely held business) needs a guaranteed source of cash to pay estate taxes without forcing an asset fire sale. Permanent life insurance provides this certainty regardless of when death occurs — unlike term, which expires.

**Permanent death benefit need beyond 30 years**: Certain business structures (buy-sell funded with permanent insurance) or estate structures (ILIT) require a guaranteed death benefit for as long as the insured lives.

**ILIT funding**: An irrevocable life insurance trust is typically funded with permanent insurance — term insurance in an ILIT creates an uncertainty about when coverage might expire.

**Overfunded life insurance as tax shelter**: After maxing all other tax-advantaged accounts (401k, IRA, HSA), some high earners use overfunded whole life (paying premiums far above the base policy requirement to accelerate cash value growth) as a tax-advantaged vehicle. The tax-free policy loans and death benefit create a supplemental retirement income stream. This strategy is complex, requires the right whole life chassis, and should only be considered after all conventional retirement accounts are maximized.

## Insurance Inside 401(k) Annuities

Some group annuity products sold inside 401(k) plans include embedded life insurance riders. These are almost always overpriced relative to standalone life insurance. The insurance cost is deducted from retirement assets annually — defeating the purpose of tax-advantaged retirement savings. Avoid unless there is a specific and unusual underwriting reason (e.g., an uninsurable individual for whom the group's coverage provides access to insurance they cannot get individually).

## Term Conversion Option

Most term policies include a guaranteed right to convert the term coverage to a permanent policy — without new medical underwriting. This is valuable because:
- If your health deteriorates during the term period, you can convert to permanent coverage without a new medical exam
- The permanent policy premium will be based on your age at conversion, not your current health
- This is an option worth paying slightly more for — it is insurance against future uninsurability

Review the conversion window carefully: most policies allow conversion up to a certain age (often 65-70) or up to a certain point in the term period.

## Life Insurance in Divorce

Divorce requires specific attention to life insurance:
- Update beneficiary designations on all policies immediately (ERISA plans cannot be automatically revoked by state divorce decrees)
- Court orders may require that term insurance be maintained on a paying spouse for the benefit of children or as support obligation security
- Property settlements may award cash value in a whole life policy — proper ownership transfer requires insurer cooperation
- Divorce does not automatically end a group employer insurance naming a former spouse
