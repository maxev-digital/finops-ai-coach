# 401(k) and 403(b) Strategies

## Contribution Limits (2025)

The standard employee elective deferral limit for 401(k) and 403(b) plans in 2025 is **$23,500**. Participants age 50 and older may contribute an additional catch-up contribution of **$7,500**, bringing the total to $31,000.

SECURE 2.0 introduced an **enhanced catch-up contribution for participants age 60–63**: $11,250 (instead of the standard $7,500). This higher catch-up applies only to ages 60, 61, 62, and 63. At age 64, the catch-up reverts to $7,500. This is a significant planning window for late-career workers seeking to accelerate retirement savings.

The overall annual additions limit (employee + employer contributions) is **$70,000** in 2025 (or 100% of compensation if less).

## Roth 401(k) vs. Traditional 401(k) Decision Framework

**Traditional 401(k):** Contributions are pre-tax, reducing current-year taxable income. Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Best when current marginal rate exceeds expected retirement rate.

**Roth 401(k):** Contributions are after-tax. Growth and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. No income limits apply (unlike Roth IRA). Best when current marginal rate is lower than expected retirement rate, or when the account will grow significantly over a long time horizon.

**Decision factors:**

- Current marginal rate vs. projected retirement rate
- Time horizon (longer horizon favors Roth due to compounding tax-free growth)
- State tax considerations (moving to a no-income-tax state in retirement favors Traditional now)
- Required Minimum Distribution exposure (Roth 401k eliminated RMDs starting 2024 per SECURE 2.0 — see RMD document)
- Legacy planning (Roth passes tax-free to heirs under the 10-year rule)

A common approach for uncertainty: split contributions between Traditional and Roth to hedge both outcomes (tax diversification).

## Employer Match — Always Capture First

Employer matching is the closest thing to guaranteed, immediate 100% return on investment. A typical 50% match on contributions up to 6% of salary is equivalent to a 3% salary increase — foregone if the employee doesn't contribute at least to the match threshold.

**Priority rule:** Always contribute at least enough to capture the full employer match before directing dollars to any other savings vehicle (HSA, IRA, taxable brokerage). No investment return will consistently beat a 50–100% guaranteed match.

**Match structures commonly seen:**

- Dollar-for-dollar up to 3–6% of salary
- 50 cents per dollar up to 6% of salary
- Tiered matches (e.g., 100% on first 3%, 50% on next 2%)

## Vesting Schedules

Vesting determines when employer contributions legally belong to the employee. Employee contributions are always 100% vested immediately.

**Cliff vesting:** Employee becomes 100% vested after a specific number of years (typically 2–3 years). Zero vesting before that date. Leaving before the cliff date means forfeiting all employer contributions.

**Graded vesting:** Vesting percentage increases annually (e.g., 20% per year over 5 years). Partial vesting occurs each year. ERISA requires full vesting under graded schedules within 6 years.

**Immediate vesting:** All employer contributions are 100% vested from day one. Common at large employers and in competitive hiring markets.

**Planning implication:** When advising a client considering a job change, calculate unvested employer match before recommending departure. A $15,000 unvested match cliff vesting in 14 months has a real retention value.

## Investment Menu Selection

Most 401(k) plans offer 10–30 investment options. Quality varies significantly by plan sponsor.

**Core principles:**

- **Prioritize low-cost index funds.** Expense ratios matter enormously over decades. A 1% annual fee difference on $500,000 over 20 years at 7% growth costs approximately $200,000 in terminal wealth.
- **Target date funds** are appropriate as a single-fund solution for participants who won't actively manage allocation. Verify the underlying expense ratio — some are 0.10–0.15%, others 0.50%+ for actively managed versions.
- **Avoid actively managed funds** unless expense ratio is below 0.50% and the fund has a sustained risk-adjusted performance advantage (rare and not persistent).
- **Avoid annuities inside a 401(k).** Variable annuities in 401(k) plans add an insurance wrapper layer with additional fees (typically 1–2% annually) on top of the underlying fund expenses. The tax-deferral benefit of an annuity is already provided by the 401(k) — the annuity adds no tax benefit and significant cost.

**Benchmark allocation building blocks:**

- U.S. total market or S&P 500 index fund
- International index fund
- Bond index fund
- Money market / stable value for near-term liquidity

## Loans from 401(k) Plans

Most 401(k) plans permit loans up to 50% of vested balance or $50,000, whichever is less. Repayment is typically over 5 years (longer for primary residence purchase).

**The risks:**

1. **Double taxation on repayment.** Loan repayments come from after-tax dollars, then the withdrawal in retirement is taxed again. The interest you pay yourself is still taxed twice.

2. **Opportunity cost.** Assets used for the loan are not invested. In a rising market, the borrowed amount misses market gains. This is especially damaging during extended bull markets.

3. **Default on job separation.** If the employee leaves the company (voluntarily or involuntarily) with an outstanding loan, the remaining balance is typically due within 60–90 days. If not repaid, the balance is treated as a taxable distribution plus 10% penalty if under 59½. TCJA extended this: if the plan offset occurs after separation, the employee has until their tax return due date (including extension) to roll the offset amount into an IRA to avoid tax.

4. **Behavioral risk.** Easy access to 401(k) funds reduces the psychological barrier to spending retirement savings.

**When a 401(k) loan may be justified:** Short-term cash need, no other low-cost borrowing available, high confidence of job stability, and ability to repay aggressively. Still generally inferior to HELOC, personal loan, or other alternatives.

## 403(b) Plan Specifics

403(b) plans cover employees of public schools, nonprofits, hospitals, and churches. They function similarly to 401(k)s with important distinctions:

**Contribution limits:** Same as 401(k) — $23,500 standard deferral, $7,500 catch-up at 50+, $11,250 enhanced catch-up at 60–63.

**15-Year Catch-Up Provision:** Employees of qualifying organizations (schools, hospitals, churches, certain nonprofits) with at least 15 years of service and average contributions below $5,000/year may contribute an additional $3,000 per year, up to a lifetime maximum of $15,000. This catch-up stacks with the age-50+ catch-up and is calculated first. IRS requires the plan to permit this provision.

**Investment menu concerns:** 403(b) plans historically offered annuity products as the primary investment vehicle. Many public school plans are still dominated by high-cost variable annuities from insurance companies. Advisors must review the investment menu carefully and advocate for low-cost options (mutual fund custodians like Fidelity, Vanguard, or TIAA core products).

**Church plans:** Not subject to ERISA, which means less federal oversight and varying vesting/protection rules. Verify plan terms carefully.

## 457(b) Plan Specifics

457(b) plans are available to state and local government employees and certain nonprofits. Key distinctions from 401(k)/403(b):

**No 10% early withdrawal penalty.** This is the critical differentiator. Distributions from governmental 457(b) plans are subject to ordinary income tax but are NOT subject to the 10% early withdrawal penalty regardless of age. This makes 457(b) assets highly accessible for early retirees, career changers, or those facing financial hardship.

**Contribution limits:** Same $23,500 standard limit, same catch-up provisions.

**Coordination with 403(b):** Government employees who have both a 403(b) and 457(b) can contribute the maximum to EACH plan separately — a $47,000 combined deferral opportunity (plus applicable catch-up contributions). This is a significant accumulation advantage.

**Special 3-Year Catch-Up:** Within 3 years of the plan's normal retirement age, participants can contribute up to twice the annual limit ($47,000), using any unused contribution room from prior years. This provision is separate from and cannot be used simultaneously with the age-50+ catch-up.

**Nonprofit 457(b):** Non-governmental 457(b) plans (top-hat plans for highly compensated nonprofit executives) have an important risk: the assets remain the property of the employer and are subject to creditor claims in bankruptcy. This is a meaningful distinction from governmental plans where assets are held in trust.

## Mega Backdoor Roth

The mega backdoor Roth allows significantly higher Roth contributions beyond the standard Roth IRA and Roth 401(k) limits. It requires:

1. **Plan permits after-tax (non-Roth) contributions.** Not all plans do — verify with plan documents.
2. **Plan permits in-service withdrawals or in-plan Roth conversions of after-tax contributions.**

**Mechanics:**

- Contribute after-tax dollars to the 401(k) above the standard deferral limit, up to the annual additions limit of $70,000 (less employee pre-tax/Roth deferrals and employer contributions).
- Convert those after-tax contributions to Roth (either via in-plan Roth conversion or by rolling to a Roth IRA at separation).
- Earnings that accumulate on after-tax contributions before conversion are taxable; the converted principal is not.
- Best executed by converting frequently (monthly) to minimize taxable earnings on the after-tax portion before conversion.

**Maximum mega backdoor Roth potential (2025):** $70,000 total limit − $23,500 employee deferral − employer match (e.g., $10,000) = approximately $36,500 in after-tax contributions eligible for conversion.

## Contribution Timing: Front-Loading vs. Spread

**Front-loading:** Maximizing contributions as early in the year as possible to maximize time invested. A $23,500 contribution in January gains ~12 additional months of market exposure vs. spreading over 12 months.

**The paycheck-matching trap:** If the employer calculates match as a percentage of each paycheck's contribution and the employee maxes out by mid-year (contributing $0 for the rest of the year), they lose the employer match for the remaining pay periods. This is plan-specific — some plans offer a "true-up" match at year-end to compensate; many do not.

**Best practice:** Verify whether the employer plan has a true-up provision. If yes, front-load freely. If no, spread contributions to ensure match is captured every paycheck. The lost employer match from front-loading without a true-up can easily exceed $2,000–5,000/year.
