# Will vs. Trust: Choosing the Right Estate Planning Foundation

## The Last Will and Testament

A will (formally, a Last Will and Testament) is a legal document that directs the distribution of a decedent's assets and the appointment of key parties after death. It requires:
- The testator (person making the will) to be of legal age and sound mind
- Witnesses (typically two) who are not beneficiaries
- In some states, notarization

A will names:
- **Beneficiaries**: Who receives which assets
- **Executor (Personal Representative)**: The person responsible for administering the estate — paying debts, filing taxes, and distributing assets
- **Guardian**: For minor children — who will raise them if both parents die
- **Trustee**: If the will creates a testamentary trust for minor beneficiaries

### The Probate Process

The critical limitation of a will: it must go through probate. Probate is the court-supervised legal process by which a will is validated and an estate is administered. Implications:

**Public record**: Probate proceedings are public. Anyone can look up what you owned, who you named, and how assets were distributed. This is a significant privacy concern for many families.

**Time**: Probate typically takes 6-18 months for straightforward estates; 2-5 years for contested or complex estates. Assets cannot be distributed until probate concludes.

**Cost**: Attorney fees, court filing fees, executor fees, and other costs. In some states (California, Florida), statutory probate fees can be 2-5% of the gross estate value (not net — gross, meaning a $1M home with a $900,000 mortgage generates fees on the full $1M value).

**Multi-state probate**: If you own real estate in multiple states, probate must be opened in each state where real property is located — an "ancillary probate" in each state, multiplying time and cost.

## The Revocable Living Trust

A revocable living trust is a legal entity created during your lifetime that holds title to assets. You (the grantor) typically serve as your own trustee while alive and competent, maintaining full control. You name a successor trustee to take over management if you become incapacitated or die.

### How It Works

You transfer assets into the trust by retitling them (deeds, bank accounts, brokerage accounts) in the trust's name: "Jane Smith, Trustee of the Jane Smith Revocable Living Trust dated January 1, 2025." Assets held in trust pass to beneficiaries according to trust terms — without probate.

### Key Features

**Avoids probate**: Assets titled in the trust bypass probate entirely. This saves time, preserves privacy, and reduces cost in states with expensive probate.

**Revocable**: You can change, amend, or revoke the trust entirely at any time while alive and competent. This means no loss of control.

**No tax benefit**: A revocable living trust provides NO income tax or estate tax benefit. You still own the assets in the eyes of the IRS. They remain in your taxable estate. The grantor trust rules apply — all income and gains are reported on your personal return.

**Incapacity planning**: A significant advantage over a will — a will only takes effect at death. A trust provides a seamless mechanism for a successor trustee to manage assets if you become incapacitated, avoiding a court-supervised conservatorship.

**Multi-state property**: Real estate in multiple states deeded into the trust avoids multiple probate proceedings in each state — one trust controls all property.

## Irrevocable Trust

An irrevocable trust cannot be changed after creation (with limited exceptions by court order or trust modification statutes). You give up control in exchange for benefits:

**Estate tax benefits**: Assets transferred to an irrevocable trust are removed from your taxable estate. For estates above the federal exemption ($13,990,000/person in 2025), irrevocable trusts are the primary planning tool.

**Asset protection**: Assets in a properly structured irrevocable trust are protected from future creditors — useful for physicians, business owners, and others with litigation exposure.

**Trade-off**: You cannot get the assets back. The trust's terms are fixed. This requires careful drafting before funding.

## The Pour-Over Will

Anyone with a revocable living trust should also have a pour-over will. The pour-over will serves as a safety net: if any assets are outside the trust at death (you forgot to retitle a bank account, received an inheritance the day before death), the pour-over will directs those assets "pour over" into the trust at death.

**Important**: Assets covered by the pour-over will still go through probate. The pour-over will does not avoid probate for assets outside the trust — it only ensures those assets end up in the trust structure after probate concludes.

## When a Trust Makes Sense

A revocable living trust is appropriate when:

1. **Probate costs are high in your state**: California and Florida have expensive probate. Avoiding it is worth the trust setup cost ($1,500-$5,000 in attorney fees).

2. **Real estate in multiple states**: Each state requires separate probate. A trust covers all states with one document.

3. **Minor children as beneficiaries**: A trust allows you to specify terms for how and when children receive money (not just a lump sum at 18). "Distribute for health, education, maintenance, and support; full distribution at age 30."

4. **Blended family situations**: Trust terms can balance providing for a current spouse while preserving assets for children from a prior relationship.

5. **Privacy concerns**: Public figure, business owner, or anyone who prefers estate distribution remain private.

6. **Incapacity planning priority**: Avoiding conservatorship if you become incapacitated.

## When a Will Alone May Be Sufficient

For many people, a will combined with properly structured beneficiary designations achieves most of the same goals without the complexity and cost of a trust:

- **Retirement accounts (IRA, 401k)**: Pass by beneficiary designation, completely outside probate
- **Life insurance**: Passes by beneficiary designation, outside probate
- **POD (Payable on Death) bank accounts**: Bank pays named beneficiary at death, outside probate
- **TOD (Transfer on Death) brokerage accounts**: Securities transfer to named beneficiary at death, outside probate
- **Joint tenancy with right of survivorship**: Real estate or financial accounts held jointly pass directly to the survivor

A modest estate where most assets pass by beneficiary designation may require only a simple will to cover the remainder, guardianship appointment, and executor designation.

## The Beneficiary Designation Priority Rule

This is a critical estate planning concept that surprises many people: **beneficiary designations supersede your will**.

A named beneficiary on an IRA, 401(k), life insurance policy, or TOD/POD account will receive that asset regardless of what your will says. If your will leaves everything to your current spouse but your ex-spouse is still named as beneficiary on your life insurance policy, your ex-spouse receives the life insurance proceeds.

This is contract law, not testamentary law — the financial institution is bound by the beneficiary designation on file, not the terms of your will.

Practical implication: **Review and update beneficiary designations after every major life event** — marriage, divorce, birth of child, death of named beneficiary. This is more urgent than updating your will in many cases.

## Healthcare Directive and Durable Power of Attorney

A complete estate plan requires two additional critical documents beyond the will or trust:

**Healthcare Directive (Living Will / Advance Directive)**: Specifies your medical treatment preferences if you cannot communicate. Includes end-of-life care decisions, life support, organ donation. Without this, healthcare providers default to maximum intervention.

**Healthcare Proxy (Medical Power of Attorney)**: Names a specific person to make medical decisions on your behalf if you are incapacitated. Different from the durable power of attorney (financial).

**Durable Financial Power of Attorney**: Names an agent to manage your financial affairs if you are incapacitated. "Durable" means it remains in effect even if you become incompetent (a regular POA terminates at incapacity). Without this, a court conservatorship may be required to manage your finances.

These documents are as important as the will or trust — and are often neglected.

## Trustee Selection

For a revocable living trust or any irrevocable trust, choosing the right trustee is critical:

- **Individual trustee**: Family member, friend — familiar, no cost, but may lack financial sophistication, may predecease you, may create family conflict
- **Successor trustee**: Specify at least two levels of succession (primary + two backups) — a single trustee with no successor is a significant gap
- **Corporate trustee**: Bank trust department or trust company — professional management, continuity, never dies or becomes incapacitated, but costs 0.5-1.5% annually and may be impersonal
- **Co-trustee arrangement**: Individual trustee (family member for personal judgment) + corporate trustee (for investment management and administration) — common for large irrevocable trusts
