# Roth Conversion Strategies

Source: IRS Publication 590-A, 590-B; SECURE 2.0 Act (2022).

---

## What Is a Roth Conversion

A Roth conversion moves money from a Traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, or pre-tax 401(k) into a Roth IRA. The converted amount is added to ordinary income in the year of conversion and taxed at your marginal rate. After conversion, the money grows tax-free and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

There is no income limit on Roth conversions. Anyone at any income level can convert.

---

## Why Convert: The Core Logic

Traditional IRA: tax-deferred. You pay taxes later when you withdraw. If your tax rate in retirement is HIGHER than it is today, you lose by deferring.

Roth IRA: tax-free. You pay taxes now. If your tax rate in retirement is LOWER than today, you lose by converting.

The optimal conversion strategy converts enough each year to fill the current tax bracket without pushing into a higher one — minimizing lifetime taxes.

---

## The Roth Conversion Window

The window is the period between retirement (income drops) and when RMDs begin at age 73 or 75. During this window:
- Earned income is gone (or reduced)
- RMDs haven't started yet (so taxable income is low)
- Social Security may not have started yet

This is often the lowest marginal rate period of a high-earner's life — the ideal time to convert. Once RMDs kick in, they force taxable income up whether you need the money or not.

**Example:** Couple retires at 62. Working income of $300,000 drops to zero. They live on brokerage and savings. RMDs start at 75. They have a 13-year window to convert at potentially 12% or 22% instead of the 32–37% they paid while working.

---

## IRMAA Interaction

Medicare Part B and Part D premiums are based on MAGI from 2 years prior. Large Roth conversions in years 63–70 can trigger IRMAA surcharges at age 65–72. The first IRMAA tier (2025) starts at $106,000 MAGI for singles, $212,000 for couples — adding $74/month per person to Part B.

**Planning rule:** When modeling conversions, include projected Medicare premiums in the cost comparison. The conversion math changes if crossing an IRMAA threshold.

---

## Social Security Taxation Interaction

Up to 85% of Social Security benefits are taxable if combined income (AGI + non-taxable interest + half of SS benefit) exceeds $34,000 (single) or $44,000 (MFJ). Roth conversions increase MAGI and can push more SS benefits into taxable income — effectively raising the marginal cost of the conversion.

If SS hasn't started yet during the conversion window, there's no interaction. Another reason to convert before claiming SS.

---

## Partial Conversion: Bracket-Filling Strategy

Convert just enough each year to fill the current bracket without crossing into the next. Key thresholds (2025, MFJ):
- Fill to top of 12% bracket: up to $96,950 taxable income → rate 12%
- Fill to top of 22% bracket: up to $206,700 taxable income → rate 22%
- Fill to top of 24% bracket: up to $394,600 taxable income → rate 24%

Taxpayers with large traditional IRAs should model the RMD tsunami: what will RMDs be at 75, 80, 85? If projected RMDs will force them into 32%+ bracket, converting at 22–24% today is mathematically superior.

**RMD projection:** Assume 6–7% annual growth. A $1M IRA today becomes roughly $1.8M in 10 years. RMDs at 75 on $1.8M ≈ $73,000/year (factor 24.6). Stacked on Social Security, other income — likely 24–32% bracket.

## The Backdoor Roth (High Income)

Roth IRA income limits (2025): single phase-out $150,000–$165,000; MFJ $236,000–$246,000. Above these limits, direct Roth contributions are not allowed.

Workaround:
1. Contribute $7,000 (or $8,000 age 50+) to a non-deductible Traditional IRA (no income limit)
2. Immediately convert to Roth IRA (no income limit on conversions)
3. Result: Roth IRA contribution at any income level

**Pro-Rata Rule:** If you have other Traditional IRA balances, the IRS treats ALL Traditional IRA money as a pool. The taxable portion of the conversion = (pre-tax IRA balances) / (total IRA balances). If you have $100,000 in pre-tax IRA and add $7,000 non-deductible, then convert $7,000, about 93.5% is taxable. Work around this by rolling pre-tax IRA balances into a current employer 401(k) first.

---

## Roth 401(k) In-Plan Conversion

Some 401(k) plans allow in-service conversion of pre-tax 401(k) balance to Roth 401(k). Taxable event — converted amount is ordinary income. Allows Roth accumulation without leaving the employer plan. Not all plans offer this feature — check Summary Plan Description.

---

## When NOT to Convert

- You expect your retirement tax rate to be significantly lower than today (e.g., very high earner planning to retire with modest needs)
- You'll need the converted funds within 5 years (Roth conversion seasoning rule: each conversion has its own 5-year clock for penalty-free withdrawal of the converted principal if under 59½)
- Converting would push into IRMAA surcharge territory and the premium increase outweighs the conversion benefit
- Estate will be donated entirely to charity (Traditional IRA to charity via QCD is 100% tax-free; Roth's tax benefit is wasted on a charitable bequest)

---

## Decision Framework

1. Project traditional IRA/401(k) balance at RMD age
2. Calculate projected RMD at that balance
3. Add projected Social Security, pension, and other income
4. Estimate future marginal rate with RMDs
5. Compare to current marginal rate
6. If future rate > current rate → convert (fill current bracket each year through the window)
7. Factor IRMAA thresholds into the annual conversion ceiling
