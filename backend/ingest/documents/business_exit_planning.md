# Business Exit Planning: Valuation, Sale Structures, and Tax Optimization

## Why Exit Planning Starts Years Before the Exit

The most valuable businesses are not sold — they are prepared to be sold. Buyers pay premiums for:
- Predictable, growing revenue streams not dependent on the owner
- Clean, GAAP-ready financial statements (not tax-minimized financials)
- Documented systems and processes that can operate without the founder
- Recurring customer relationships (contracts, subscriptions, long-term agreements)
- A capable management team in place

Businesses that are owner-dependent, have opaque financials, or rely on the founder's relationships sell at discounts — or don't sell at all. A 3-5 year preparation timeline is realistic for a transaction-ready business.

## Business Valuation Methods

### Income Approach: Capitalization and DCF

**Capitalization of earnings**:
Value = Normalized annual earnings / Capitalization rate

The "normalized earnings" figure adjusts for non-recurring items, above- or below-market owner compensation, discretionary owner expenses, and one-time events. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization) or Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE = EBITDA + owner's compensation + perks) are common bases.

The capitalization rate represents the required return for this type of business — higher risk (less stable, more owner-dependent, smaller) requires higher rates:
- Stable, growing, well-diversified small business: 15-20% cap rate → 5-6.7× earnings multiple
- Average small service business: 20-33% cap rate → 3-5× earnings multiple
- Riskier or very small businesses: 33-50% cap rate → 2-3× earnings multiple

**Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)**: Project free cash flows 5-10 years forward with explicit growth assumptions; calculate terminal value; discount everything to present value at the weighted average cost of capital. More nuanced but highly assumption-dependent. Common for businesses with defined growth trajectories or variable earnings.

### Market Approach: Transaction Multiples

The most intuitive approach: what did comparable businesses sell for?

**EBITDA multiples by industry (general ranges for small-to-mid businesses)**:
- Professional services (non-SSTB): 3-5× EBITDA
- Healthcare services: 6-12× EBITDA
- Technology / SaaS: 8-20× EBITDA (dependent on growth rate, ARR, churn)
- Manufacturing: 4-7× EBITDA
- Distribution / logistics: 4-6× EBITDA
- Restaurant/retail: 2-4× EBITDA (often SDE-based)
- Construction/trades: 2-4× EBITDA

**Premium factors** that increase multiples:
- Recurring or contracted revenue
- Revenue diversification (no single customer >15-20% of revenue)
- Strong management team (not founder-dependent)
- Proprietary technology, IP, or processes
- National/regional scalability

**Discount factors**:
- Owner dependency
- Customer concentration
- Single location / geographic concentration
- Industry headwinds
- Pending legal, regulatory, or environmental issues

Private transaction databases: DealStats (BizBuySell), Pratt's Stats, Pitchbook. Investment bankers and M&A advisors use these to benchmark actual completed transactions.

## Exit Options

### Strategic Sale to Competitor or Buyer

A competitor, customer, supplier, or private equity-backed platform company acquires the business.

**Advantages**: Typically highest valuation — strategic buyers pay for synergies (eliminating duplicate overhead, acquiring customer relationships, expanding market). Full liquidity immediately.

**Process**: Engage an M&A advisor or investment banker for businesses with $5M+ EBITDA. For smaller businesses ($1M-$5M EBITDA), business brokers or M&A attorneys handle the process. Create a Confidential Information Memorandum (CIM), identify and approach qualified buyers under NDA, run competitive process.

**Timeline**: 6-18 months from engagement to close.

**Structure negotiation**: Buyers typically prefer asset sales (for a stepped-up basis); sellers prefer stock/unit sales (single level of capital gains). This negotiation is economically significant (see tax section below).

### ESOP (Employee Stock Ownership Plan)

Employees acquire the business through a trust funded by a bank loan. The company repays the loan over time through deductible contributions to the ESOP.

**Key features for the selling owner**:
- **Section 1042 rollover**: Qualifying sellers (C-Corp shareholders) who sell to an ESOP can defer capital gains by reinvesting proceeds in Qualified Replacement Property (QRP — diversified US company stocks and bonds). The gain is permanently deferred if QRP is held until death (stepped-up basis eliminates it).
- ESOP must own at least 30% of company stock after the sale
- Must be a C-Corporation (or convert to C-Corp before the 1042 election)
- 1042 election must be made on a timely filed tax return

**Ongoing tax benefits for the company**:
- S-Corp 100% ESOP-owned: no federal income tax on the ESOP-owned portion
- C-Corp: ESOP contributions (used to repay the acquisition loan) are tax-deductible

**Setup costs**: $100,000-$250,000+ in legal, valuation, and administrative fees. Best for businesses with $2M+ EBITDA.

### Private Equity Recapitalization

Sell majority control (60-80%) to a PE firm, retain 20-40% equity stake.

**Rationale**: Receive immediate partial liquidity; benefit from PE firm's capital, expertise, and add-on acquisition strategy; retain meaningful equity for the "second bite" when PE exits in 3-7 years.

**Valuation**: PE firms pay market multiples; management may receive rollover equity at the same valuation.

**For growth-oriented sellers**: If the business has significant growth potential with additional capital, a PE recap can result in total proceeds (first + second sale) that exceed a full immediate sale at current valuation.

## Tax Implications of Business Sale

### Asset Sale vs. Stock Sale

This is the most important tax decision in a business sale.

**Asset sale**:
- Buyer acquires individual assets; receives full depreciation basis step-up on acquired assets
- Tax treatment varies by asset type:
  - Inventory: ordinary income
  - Accounts receivable: ordinary income
  - Furniture/fixtures/equipment: §1245 recapture (ordinary income to the extent of prior depreciation), then capital gain
  - Real estate: §1250 recapture (taxed at max 25%), then capital gain
  - Goodwill / going-concern value: long-term capital gains
  - Covenant not to compete: ordinary income (all 15 years present-valued at closing)
  - Customer lists: capital gains (typically)
- Sellers generally prefer to minimize allocation to covenants, inventory, and equipment; maximize allocation to goodwill

**Stock/unit sale**:
- Buyer acquires equity interests; all proceeds taxed at capital gains rates (if held >1 year)
- Single level of tax — no component-by-component recapture
- Buyer does NOT get basis step-up on the company's assets (buyer absorbs all historic depreciation attributes)

**The negotiation**: Sellers demand a price premium for accepting an asset sale structure — the additional ordinary income and recapture taxes come directly out of seller proceeds. M&A attorneys and CPAs model the after-tax proceeds under both structures to quantify the premium required.

**§338(h)(10) election**: For S-Corp sales, both parties can elect §338(h)(10), which treats a stock sale as an asset sale for tax purposes — the seller pays asset sale taxes, but the buyer gets the asset basis step-up. This allows the parties to split the tax benefit of the step-up (higher price from buyer in exchange for seller accepting asset sale taxation).

### Section 1202 QSBS: The Ultimate Capital Gains Exclusion

IRC §1202 provides one of the most powerful tax benefits in the code for qualified small business stock:

**Requirements**:
- Domestic C-Corporation only
- Acquired at original issuance (not purchased on secondary market)
- Held for more than 5 years
- Gross assets of the corporation at time of issuance (and immediately after) did not exceed $50 million
- Must be an active business in a qualifying trade (excludes professional services in health, law, accounting, financial services, hospitality, finance, insurance, real estate, farming, mining — these are per-category disqualifications)

**Benefit**: Exclude 100% of capital gain from federal taxes (for stock acquired after September 27, 2010), up to the greater of:
- $10,000,000, OR
- 10× the taxpayer's adjusted basis in the stock

**Example**: Founder invested $500,000 in qualifying C-Corp startup in 2018. Sells in 2024 for $12,000,000. Gain = $11,500,000. Excluded: $10,000,000 (the greater of $10M or 10× $500K = $5M). Taxable: $1,500,000. Federal capital gains tax savings: approximately $2,000,000-$2,380,000.

**State conformity**: Critical gap — California and a few other states do NOT conform to §1202 exclusion. California state capital gains tax applies to the full gain. The federal benefit is still enormous; state taxes must be modeled separately.

## Earnout Provisions

An earnout pays the seller additional consideration based on future business performance — post-closing revenues, earnings, or milestones.

**Seller perspective**: Earnouts allow you to capture value for future performance that the buyer is unwilling to pay upfront with certainty. If you're confident in the business's trajectory, earnouts can increase total proceeds.

**Buyer perspective**: Earnouts reduce upfront risk — you pay more only if the business performs as the seller projects.

**Tax treatment**: Earnout payments are generally taxable when received (installment sale treatment); the IRS requires at least an interest component on deferred payments.

**Challenges**: Post-close accounting disputes; buyer may affect business performance in ways that reduce earnouts; definitions of "revenue" or "EBITDA" in earnout formulas are frequently disputed; earn-outs related to the seller continuing to work may be reclassified as compensation (ordinary income) rather than capital gains proceeds.

## Preparation Timeline

**3-5 years before exit**:
- Transition to GAAP-compliant financial statements (or at minimum, clear addback schedules for tax-minimized returns)
- Document all processes: operations manuals, customer management procedures, vendor agreements
- Reduce owner-dependency: develop management bench, cross-train key staff, introduce customers to managers
- Renew long-term customer contracts; eliminate month-to-month arrangements where possible
- Resolve pending legal, environmental, or regulatory issues
- Ensure intellectual property is owned by the company, not the founder personally

**1-2 years before exit**:
- Formal business valuation by accredited appraiser (CVA, ABV, or ASA credential)
- Engage investment banker or M&A advisor for larger transactions ($5M+ EBITDA)
- Evaluate entity structure: is C-Corp conversion beneficial for ESOP or QSBS?
- Execute key employee retention agreements (prevent exodus during sale process)
- Begin tax modeling for various deal structures (asset vs. stock, installment sale, earnout)

**Year of transaction**:
- Manage deal process under NDA; limit employee awareness during due diligence
- Negotiate letter of intent; define price, structure, and key terms
- Conduct due diligence preparation: clean up financial statements, minute books, contracts, IP assignments, employment agreements
- Negotiate and execute definitive purchase agreement
- Post-close transition: earnout performance period; non-compete obligations; consulting agreements
