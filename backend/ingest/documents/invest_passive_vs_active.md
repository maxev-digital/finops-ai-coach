# Passive vs. Active Investing: Evidence and Implementation

## The Core Finding: SPIVA Data

S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes the SPIVA (S&P Indices Versus Active) Scorecard semiannually, tracking the percentage of active funds that underperform their benchmark index after fees. The results are consistent and striking:

- Over 1 year: approximately 55-65% of large-cap active funds underperform the S&P 500
- Over 5 years: approximately 75-80% underperform
- Over 15 years: approximately 85-92% underperform

This is not a recent phenomenon. The data has been consistent since SPIVA began publication in 2002. The figures hold across most major categories — large cap, mid cap, small cap, international developed, emerging markets, and most bond categories. The odds against an investor successfully selecting a top-quartile active fund that maintains its performance over a decade are significant.

## Why Active Management Underperforms

### Cost as the Primary Driver

The arithmetic of active management is unavoidable: the average actively managed fund must underperform the index by the amount of its fees, because active managers collectively own the market. After fees:

- Average active equity fund expense ratio: 0.50-1.50% per year
- Index fund expense ratio: 0.03-0.20% per year
- Annual performance drag from fees alone: 0.30-1.50%

Compounded over 20-30 years, a 1% annual fee disadvantage reduces terminal wealth by approximately 18-26% relative to a lower-cost alternative — a significant drag that must be overcome by genuine skill, which research suggests few managers possess consistently.

### Trading Costs and Tax Drag

Beyond the expense ratio, active funds incur:
- **Transaction costs**: Bid/ask spreads and market impact from frequent trading
- **Tax drag**: Portfolio turnover generates realized capital gains, which must be distributed to shareholders and taxed. High-turnover funds (100-200% annual turnover) generate substantial taxable distributions even in years when the market did not gain

### Survivorship Bias

SPIVA and academic research control for survivorship bias, which inflates the apparent performance of active management in raw data. Funds that fail — closing due to poor performance — are removed from databases. If you look only at currently existing funds, you see only the survivors. Approximately 50% of mutual funds available 20 years ago no longer exist. Adding back the performance of failed funds substantially reduces the average active manager performance record.

### Style Drift and Manager Turnover

Active funds frequently change their investment style (drifting from value to growth, for example) and experience portfolio manager turnover. The manager who produced the historical track record may no longer be managing the fund you're evaluating.

## Factor-Based Investing: The Bridge

Factor investing occupies a middle ground between pure passive (market beta only) and traditional active (manager discretion). Systematic exposures to documented risk factors — value, size, profitability, momentum — can be implemented in low-cost, rules-based ETFs without relying on manager skill.

This is not traditional active management. It is disciplined exposure to systematic return sources:

**Value premium**: Cheap stocks (high book-to-price, high earnings yield) outperform expensive stocks over long periods — documented by Fama and French in 1992, and internationally by subsequent research.

**Small-cap premium**: Small-capitalization stocks have outperformed large-cap stocks over long periods — approximately 2% annualized historically, with significant variation by period.

**Momentum premium**: Stocks with strong recent performance (3-12 months) tend to continue outperforming over the following months — documented by Jegadeesh and Titman in 1993 and subsequently replicated internationally.

**Quality/Profitability premium**: Profitable, financially sound firms outperform unprofitable firms — documented by Novy-Marx and AQR research.

Factor ETFs (Dimensional Fund Advisors, AQR, iShares, Vanguard VFMF/VFQY/VLUE) provide rules-based exposure at 0.15-0.40% expense ratios — far below traditional active funds but above plain index funds.

## When Active Management May Add Value

The case for pure passive is strongest in large-cap US equities — the most efficient, most analyzed, most competitive market. There are narrower areas where active management may have more opportunity:

**Fixed income markets**: Less liquid, less transparent, and less efficiently priced than equity markets. Skilled bond managers may add value through credit analysis and positioning, particularly in corporate bonds, high yield, or emerging market debt.

**Small-cap international**: Less analyst coverage, more pricing inefficiency. The potential for skilled active managers to exploit informational advantages is higher than in US large-cap.

**Alternative assets and private credit**: Private markets require manager selection skill that does not exist in index format — direct lending, real estate, and private equity depend heavily on manager relationships and underwriting.

**Note**: Even in these categories, cost-controlled and factor-based approaches often outperform the average active manager after fees. Active management may add value, but identifying which managers will in advance is extraordinarily difficult.

## Total Cost of Ownership Analysis

When evaluating any fund, the total cost of ownership includes:

1. **Expense ratio**: Annual fund operating expenses as a percentage of assets
2. **Trading spread**: The bid/ask spread when buying and selling ETF shares (relevant for ETFs, not mutual funds)
3. **Tax drag from turnover**: High turnover generates taxable capital gains distributions in taxable accounts; 100% turnover can add 0.5-1.5% to effective annual cost in taxable accounts
4. **Sales loads**: Front-end or back-end loads (common in commission-based channels) — reduce gross returns by 3-5% at purchase or sale

For a taxable account investor in the 32% bracket, a fund with 100% annual turnover may have an effective tax drag of 1%+ annually beyond its stated expense ratio, making a 0.80% expense ratio fund with high turnover effectively more expensive than a 0.20% expense ratio fund might suggest.

## Evaluating a 401(k) Menu

Most 401(k) plans offer a mix of fund options. Key steps:

1. **Identify the lowest-cost index funds** available: Look for total market or S&P 500 index funds with expense ratios below 0.20%. If available at 0.05-0.10%, prefer these over any active alternative.

2. **Self-directed brokerage window**: Many larger plans offer a brokerage window allowing access to virtually any ETF or mutual fund. If your plan's index fund options are expensive (>0.30%), check if a brokerage window is available.

3. **Roll-in after leaving employer**: After leaving a job, a 401(k) can be rolled into an IRA with unlimited investment choice. This eliminates the plan menu limitation entirely.

4. **Evaluate target-date fund expenses**: If using a TDF, check the expense ratio. Some plans offer institutional TDFs at 0.10%; others charge 0.60-0.80% for the same glide path.

## Factor Premiums: Investment Implications

For investors who want to implement factor tilts beyond plain index investing:

**Value premium (Fama-French)**: Historically ~3% annualized above market. Implemented via: Vanguard Value ETF (VTV), Avantis US Small Cap Value (AVUV), Dimensional US Small Cap Value (DFSV). The premium experienced significant underperformance 2007-2020 before recovering strongly 2021-2022.

**Small-cap premium**: ~2% annualized historically. Implemented via: Vanguard Small-Cap ETF (VB), AVUV for small-cap value tilt. Premium is stronger when combined with value.

**Momentum premium**: Strongest documented factor historically but highest turnover, making it least tax-efficient. Implemented via iShares MSCI USA Momentum (MTUM). Less suitable for taxable accounts.

**Quality/Profitability premium**: Implemented via iShares MSCI USA Quality (QUAL), Vanguard VFQY. Lower turnover, tax-efficient.

The practical implementation for most investors: 70-80% core index (VTI/VXUS) + 20-30% factor tilt toward small-cap value (AVUV/DFSV). This adds factor exposure without abandoning the core benefits of broad market indexing.
