# 529 Education Savings Plans: Strategies and Optimization

## 529 Plan Basics

A 529 plan is a state-sponsored, tax-advantaged investment account designed for education savings. Two main types exist: Education Savings Plans (investment accounts, the most common type discussed here) and Prepaid Tuition Plans (locks in future tuition at current prices — available in limited states).

**Federal tax benefits**:
- Contributions: No federal tax deduction (unlike some state deductions — see below)
- Growth: Entirely tax-free within the account
- Qualified withdrawals: Completely tax-free — no federal income tax

**Account features**:
- No contribution limits (per year) from a federal tax perspective — only gift tax limits apply
- No income limit to contribute
- Beneficiary can be changed to any qualifying family member
- Accounts can remain open indefinitely — no "use by" date

## Qualified Education Expenses

The following expenses qualify for tax-free 529 withdrawal:

**College and post-secondary education**:
- Tuition and fees at any Title IV eligible institution (nearly all accredited US colleges and universities, plus some international institutions)
- Books and required supplies
- Room and board (up to what the school's cost of attendance allows for on-campus housing)
- Computers, internet access, and software for educational use
- Special needs services

**K-12 education** (TCJA addition):
- Up to $10,000 per year per beneficiary for K-12 tuition at public, private, or religious schools
- Does NOT include K-12 room and board — only tuition

**Student loan repayment** (SECURE 2.0 addition):
- Up to $10,000 lifetime per beneficiary toward student loan principal and interest
- Up to $10,000 per beneficiary's sibling as well

**Apprenticeship programs**:
- Registered apprenticeship programs registered with the Department of Labor qualify

## Non-Qualified Withdrawals: The Penalty

If 529 funds are withdrawn for non-qualified expenses:
- The **earnings portion** of the withdrawal is subject to income tax (at ordinary rates) AND a 10% federal penalty
- The **contribution/basis portion** is never taxed or penalized — you only contributed after-tax money
- Some penalty exceptions: death or disability of the beneficiary, scholarship (penalty waived up to scholarship amount, but tax still applies to earnings)

## State Deductions and Credits: Where to Open Your 529

Approximately 35 states offer a state income tax deduction or credit for contributions to a 529 plan. Important nuances:

**State-specific plans**: Many states only offer the deduction for contributions to their own state's plan. Contributing to a different state's plan may not qualify for the deduction.

**Deductibility and credit amounts**: Vary widely. Some states offer deductions of $2,000-$10,000 per year; others offer dollar-for-dollar credits on contributions up to a limit.

**Decision framework**:
1. If your state offers a meaningful deduction/credit for its own plan, evaluate whether the deduction value outweighs any investment cost difference vs. a better out-of-state plan
2. If your state has no deduction, or if the plan has very high fees, you are free to choose the best plan nationally regardless of state
3. Top-rated 529 plans (consistently): Utah My529, Nevada Vanguard 529, New York 529 Direct Plan, California ScholarShare — often selected by out-of-state residents for low costs

**Calculating the deduction value**: State income tax rate × deductible amount. A state with 5% income tax and $5,000 deductible generates $250 in state tax savings per year — meaningful for high-contribution years.

## Investment Selection Within 529 Plans

Most 529 plans offer:
- **Age-based (enrollment year) portfolios**: Automatically shift from equity to fixed income as the beneficiary approaches college age — a target-date fund approach for education
- **Static portfolios**: Fixed allocations maintained by the investor; requires manual rebalancing
- **Individual fund options**: Select specific mutual funds; maximum control, maximum complexity

**Age-based portfolio recommendation**: For most families, an age-based portfolio is the right default. It handles allocation changes automatically and prevents over-exposure to equity risk when tuition payments are only 1-2 years away.

**Expense ratio importance**: 529 plan costs vary widely. The investment expenses within 529 plans compound over 18+ years — a 0.10% expense ratio vs. 0.80% is significant. Evaluate expense ratios before committing to a state plan.

## Beneficiary Changes and Rollovers

**Changing the beneficiary**: The account owner can change the beneficiary to any member of the original beneficiary's family:
- Siblings, step-siblings
- Parents, step-parents
- Grandparents
- Cousins, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews
- First cousins
- Spouse

If the new beneficiary is in a younger generation (e.g., niece or nephew), it could be treated as a taxable gift. Changes to the same generation or older generation are generally not taxable.

**Rollover between plans**: Once per 12-month period per beneficiary, a 529 can be rolled from one state's plan to another without tax consequence. This allows moving to a lower-cost or better-performing plan.

## The 529 to Roth IRA Rollover (SECURE 2.0, 2024)

Starting in 2024, 529 funds can be rolled to a Roth IRA for the beneficiary under the following conditions:

- The 529 account must have been open for at least 15 years
- The rollover amount cannot include contributions made in the last 5 years (or earnings on those contributions)
- The lifetime maximum rollover is $35,000
- Annual rollovers are subject to the annual Roth IRA contribution limit ($7,000 in 2025) for that beneficiary
- The beneficiary must have earned income at least equal to the amount rolled in that year (same as regular Roth IRA contribution requirement)

**Planning implication**: A 529 opened at a child's birth can be rolled to a Roth IRA starting at age 15, contributing up to $7,000/year for 5 years = $35,000 lifetime transfer. This provides a tax-free head start on the child's retirement savings — one of the most powerful college planning / retirement planning combinations available.

**Important note on the 15-year rule**: The IRS has not yet provided final guidance on whether a beneficiary change resets the 15-year clock. Consult current guidance before executing this strategy.

## Superfunding: The 5-Year Gift Tax Election

A married couple can contribute up to $190,000 per beneficiary to a 529 in a single year by electing to treat the contribution as five separate $19,000 annual exclusion gifts.

**Requirements**:
- Make the election on Form 709 (gift tax return)
- During the 5-year period, no additional annual exclusion gifts to that same beneficiary (unless within the $38,000/year cap)
- If the donor dies within the 5-year period, the remaining pro-rata portion reverts to the estate

**Use case**: Grandparents with $500,000+ in assets can transfer $190,000 per grandchild into 529 plans in a single year — significant estate reduction with dedicated education purpose.

## 529 vs. Roth IRA for Education Funding

For families who are uncertain whether their child will go to college, comparing 529 to Roth IRA as an education savings vehicle:

| Factor | 529 Plan | Roth IRA |
|--------|----------|----------|
| Contribution limit | High (gift tax limits only) | $7,000/year (with earned income) |
| Federal tax deduction | No | No |
| State tax deduction | Often yes | No |
| FAFSA treatment | Parent asset (5.64% rate) | Not counted if owned by parent |
| Education withdrawals | Tax-free and penalty-free | Contributions always tax-free; earnings may be taxable for education |
| Non-education use | 10% penalty on earnings + income tax | Contributions available anytime; earnings after age 59½ |
| Flexibility if no college | SECURE 2.0 Roth rollover ($35K); change beneficiary | Fully flexible |

**Conclusion**: If retirement savings are fully funded, a 529 is generally superior for education savings due to state deductions, higher contribution limits, and clear qualified purpose. If retirement is underfunded, a Roth IRA's dual-purpose use is more valuable than the 529's dedicated use.
