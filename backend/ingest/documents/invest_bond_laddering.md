# Bond Laddering: Predictable Income and Interest Rate Risk Management

## What Is a Bond Ladder

A bond ladder is a fixed income strategy that involves purchasing individual bonds with staggered maturity dates so that one bond matures each year (or at each regular interval). Instead of holding a single bond fund or buying all bonds at one maturity, the ladder distributes maturities across time.

**Example of a 10-rung Treasury ladder**:
- Year 1: $50,000 in 1-year Treasury maturing 2026
- Year 2: $50,000 in 2-year Treasury maturing 2027
- Year 3: $50,000 in 3-year Treasury maturing 2028
- ... continuing through Year 10: $50,000 in 10-year Treasury maturing 2035

Total invested: $500,000 providing $50,000/year in maturing proceeds plus annual interest throughout.

As each rung matures, the proceeds are either spent (in retirement) or reinvested into a new long-end rung at whatever interest rate prevails at that time — extending the ladder forward.

## How Bond Laddering Solves Interest Rate Risk

Individual bonds held to maturity return their principal regardless of what happens to interest rates in the interim. If you buy a $50,000 Treasury at 4.5% and rates rise to 7%, the market price of that bond falls — but if you hold it to maturity, you receive exactly $50,000 back plus all scheduled interest payments. No mark-to-market loss is realized.

This is the fundamental difference between owning individual bonds and owning a bond fund:
- **Bond fund**: Has an indefinite term; must sell bonds when investors redeem shares; rising rates cause NAV to fall; there is no guaranteed maturity date where you "get your money back"
- **Individual bond ladder**: Each rung has a known maturity date and known future cash flows; rising rates don't affect the cash you receive at maturity

For retirees who need predictable income and cannot afford to see "losses" in their fixed income portfolio, the ladder eliminates the psychological and cash flow disruption of bond fund NAV fluctuations.

## Constructing a Bond Ladder

**Step 1: Determine the ladder's purpose and length**
- Retirement income ladder: typically 10-20 years of maturities, sized to cover spending gap between portfolio withdrawals and guaranteed income (Social Security, pension)
- Education funding: ladder maturities to coincide with tuition due dates
- Capital preservation: 2-5 year ladder for cash reserves

**Step 2: Choose rung sizes**
Equal rung sizes are common, but rung sizes can be customized to match known future cash flow needs (e.g., larger rung in the year tuition is due, larger rung in the first year of retirement before Social Security begins).

**Step 3: Select bond types** (see below)

**Step 4: Reinvest maturing rungs**
When a rung matures, reinvest at the far end of the ladder to maintain the ladder's length. If spending the proceeds in retirement, the ladder shortens by one rung per year — this is by design.

## Bond Types for Ladders

### U.S. Treasury Bonds and Notes
- Backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government — zero credit risk
- Most liquid secondary market in the world
- Interest exempt from state and local taxes (not federal)
- Available at TreasuryDirect.gov or through any brokerage
- 2025 yield context: 2-year ~4.0-4.2%; 5-year ~4.1-4.3%; 10-year ~4.2-4.5%

### I-Bonds (Series I Savings Bonds)
- Inflation-adjusted principal: rate = fixed rate + CPI-based inflation adjustment
- Purchase limit: $10,000 per person per year (plus $5,000 in tax refund); $10,000 per entity (trusts, businesses)
- Cannot be redeemed for 1 year; 3-month interest penalty if redeemed before 5 years
- Interest deferred until redemption or maturity — excellent for tax management
- Particularly valuable for the "near-cash" portion of a ladder where inflation protection is desired

### TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities)
- Principal adjusts with CPI; interest paid on inflation-adjusted principal
- No deflation floor risk beyond the original principal amount (guaranteed to return original face value)
- "Phantom income" issue: inflation adjustments are taxed in the year of adjustment, even though you don't receive the cash until maturity — better held in tax-deferred accounts
- Useful for building an inflation-protected income stream in a retirement ladder

### CDs (Certificates of Deposit)
- FDIC-insured up to $250,000 per depositor per institution
- No secondary market risk — early withdrawal penalty instead of market price
- Rates often comparable to Treasuries; sometimes higher (bank-specific promotions)
- Can ladder across multiple institutions to stay within FDIC limits
- Less liquid than Treasuries; cannot sell before maturity without penalty

### Municipal Bonds
- Interest exempt from federal income tax; may be exempt from state tax if issued in your state of residence
- Higher yields than equivalent-credit Treasuries on a tax-equivalent basis for high-bracket investors
- Credit risk: municipalities can default (Detroit 2013, Puerto Rico 2016) — research issuer carefully; GO bonds (backed by taxing power) safer than revenue bonds
- Tax-equivalent yield = municipal yield / (1 − marginal tax rate); at 35% bracket, a 3.5% muni = 5.4% taxable equivalent

### Corporate Bonds
- Higher yield than Treasuries to compensate for credit risk
- Investment-grade (BBB- and above): moderate credit risk; most suitable for ladders
- High-yield (below BBB-): significant default risk; not suitable for conservative ladders
- Less liquid than Treasuries — wider bid/ask spreads on secondary market

## 2025 Yield Context

The 2022-2023 Federal Reserve rate hike cycle reset bond yields to their most attractive levels since 2007:
- 5-year Treasury: ~4.1-4.3%
- 10-year Treasury: ~4.2-4.5%
- 5-year CD (brokered): ~4.0-4.5%
- Investment-grade corporate 5-year: ~5.0-5.5%

Compared to the 2010s when a 10-year Treasury yielded 1.5-2.5%, building a bond ladder in 2025 provides meaningful income without credit risk. A retiree who locked in 4-4.5% yields across a 10-year Treasury ladder has certainty about that income stream regardless of future rate movements.

## CD Ladder as Alternative

For investors who prefer simplicity and FDIC protection over secondary market liquidity:

**CD ladder construction**:
- Open CDs at multiple banks (to stay under $250,000 FDIC limit per bank)
- Stagger maturities: 1-year, 2-year, 3-year, 4-year, 5-year
- When each CD matures, reinvest at the 5-year (longest) rung if continuing the ladder

Brokered CDs (available through Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard) trade on a secondary market and can be sold before maturity (at market price, without early withdrawal penalty), providing more liquidity than direct bank CDs at the cost of some secondary market price risk.

## Bond Ladder vs. Bond Fund: The Comparison

| Dimension | Bond Ladder | Bond Fund |
|-----------|-------------|-----------|
| Maturity certainty | Known maturity for each rung | No maturity date for the fund |
| NAV fluctuation | Mark-to-market fluctuations occur, but holding to maturity neutralizes them | Fund NAV fluctuates continuously |
| Credit risk | Know exactly which bonds you own | Diversified but opaque to individual bonds |
| Management | Requires monitoring and reinvestment | Automatic, professional management |
| Diversification | Limited by capital; small ladders have concentrated holdings | Highly diversified even with small investment |
| Tax efficiency | Can control when gains/losses are realized | Distributions outside your control |
| Capital required | Need significant capital for diversification ($100,000+) | Can start with $100 in an index fund |
| Liquidity | Individual bonds can be sold on secondary market (with spread) | Same-day redemption at NAV |

**When ladder is preferred**: Retirement income planning, liability matching, psychological need for certainty, large portfolio.
**When fund is preferred**: Smaller portfolio, need for simplicity, convenience, and instant diversification.

## TIPS Ladder for Retirement Income

A TIPS ladder specifically designed to match spending needs in retirement is the theoretical gold standard for inflation-adjusted income security. Wade Pfau, Larry Siegel, and other researchers have documented the "TIPS ladder" retirement strategy:

- Purchase TIPS with maturities covering each year of retirement spending need
- Each year's maturing TIPS provides that year's inflation-adjusted income
- No sequence of returns risk, no longevity risk for the period covered
- Limitation: requires large capital ($1-2M+ to fund 20-30 years of spending); TIPS yields must be positive in real terms to be financially viable; no bequest value

This strategy is used by some pension funds and endowments for liability-matching and can be adapted by high-net-worth retirees willing to trade flexibility for certainty.
