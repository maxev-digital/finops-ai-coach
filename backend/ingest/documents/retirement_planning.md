# Retirement Planning Fundamentals

## Why Starting Early Matters

Compound growth means every dollar saved today is worth significantly more at retirement. A 25-year-old investing $200/month at 7% average annual growth accumulates roughly $525,000 by age 65. The same person starting at 35 accumulates only $243,000 — less than half — despite just a 10-year delay.

## 401(k) Basics

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement account. Contributions are either pre-tax (Traditional) or post-tax (Roth). Traditional contributions reduce your taxable income today; Roth contributions grow tax-free and are withdrawn tax-free in retirement.

2024 limits: $23,000/year ($30,500 if age 50+).

## The Employer Match Rule

If your employer matches 100% of contributions up to 4% of salary, contribute at least 4%. Not doing so is leaving part of your compensation unclaimed. On a $70,000 salary, a 4% match = $2,800/year in free money.

## Traditional IRA vs Roth IRA

Traditional IRA: contributions may be tax-deductible, growth is tax-deferred, withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. Roth IRA: contributions are post-tax, growth and qualified withdrawals are completely tax-free. 2024 limit for both: $7,000/year ($8,000 if 50+). Roth IRA income phase-out begins at $146,000 (single filers).

Roth is generally preferred when you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement. Young earners typically benefit most from Roth.

## The 15% Rule

Financial planners commonly recommend saving 15% of gross income for retirement, including any employer match. Starting after age 35 means targeting 20-25% to compensate.

## Target Date Funds

Target date funds (e.g., "Target 2055 Fund") automatically shift from growth-oriented to conservative allocations as the target year approaches. They are not optimal for every situation but are a solid default for employees who prefer a hands-off approach.

## Social Security

Social Security replaces roughly 40% of pre-retirement income for average earners — not enough on its own. Benefits increase approximately 8% for each year you delay claiming past full retirement age, up to age 70.
