# Social Security Rules: Benefits, Eligibility, and Optimization

## How Social Security Benefits Are Calculated

Social Security retirement benefits are based on your lifetime earnings record. The SSA uses the following calculation:

**Step 1: Index your earnings**
All earnings from age 22 onward are indexed to reflect wage growth in the economy up to age 60. Earnings after age 60 are used at their actual value.

**Step 2: Average your highest 35 years**
The SSA identifies your highest 35 years of indexed earnings and averages them, creating your Average Indexed Monthly Earnings (AIME). Years with zero earnings count as zeros — if you worked fewer than 35 years, zeros drag down the average significantly.

**Step 3: Apply the benefit formula to get your PIA**
The Primary Insurance Amount (PIA) is calculated using a progressive "bend point" formula that provides higher replacement rates for lower earners. The 2025 bend points:
- 90% of the first $1,226 of AIME
- 32% of AIME between $1,226 and $7,391
- 15% of AIME above $7,391

The resulting PIA is the benefit amount you receive at your Full Retirement Age (FRA).

**Planning implication**: Maximizing Social Security requires maximizing the 35 highest-earning years. Even in your late 50s and early 60s, working additional years of high earnings can replace lower-earnings years in the calculation, increasing your benefit. Every $1 increase in your AIME increases your PIA by 15-32% of that dollar depending on which bend point it falls into.

## Full Retirement Age and Claiming Age Adjustments

**Full Retirement Age (FRA)**: The age at which you receive 100% of your PIA.
- Born 1943-1954: FRA = 66
- Born 1955-1959: FRA = 66 + 2 months per year (66 years, 2 months for 1955; 66 years, 10 months for 1959)
- Born 1960 or later: FRA = 67

**Early claiming reduction** (claiming before FRA):
- Age 62 (earliest): 30% reduction from FRA benefit (for those with FRA 67)
- Each month before FRA: 5/9% per month for first 36 months, 5/12% per month beyond that

**Delayed retirement credits** (delaying past FRA):
- Each year past FRA, up to age 70: +8%/year
- Delay from 67 to 70: +24% above PIA
- There are no credits for delaying past age 70

**Breakeven analysis for delay**: Claiming at 70 vs. 67:
- Monthly benefit at 67: $3,000/month (PIA)
- Monthly benefit at 70: $3,720/month ($3,000 × 124%)
- Foregone benefits 67-70: 36 × $3,000 = $108,000
- Monthly difference: $720/month
- Months to breakeven: $108,000 / $720 = 150 months = 12.5 years
- Breakeven age: 70 + 12.5 = 82.5

For a healthy person who lives past 82-83, claiming at 70 provides higher total lifetime benefits.

## Cost of Living Adjustment (COLA)

Social Security benefits are adjusted annually for inflation using the CPI-W (Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers):
- COLA is announced each October for benefits beginning the following January
- 2023 COLA: 8.7% (highest in 40 years, driven by 2022 inflation)
- 2024 COLA: 3.2%
- 2025 COLA: 2.5%
- Long-run average COLA since 2010: approximately 2.5-3.0%/year

The inflation indexing of Social Security is its most powerful feature — no other common retirement income source (except TIPS or I-Bonds) provides automatic inflation protection on the full benefit amount. This makes delayed claiming particularly valuable: you are deferring a larger, permanently inflation-adjusted payment.

## Taxability of Social Security Benefits

Social Security benefits are partially taxable based on "provisional income" (also called "combined income"):

**Provisional income** = AGI + tax-exempt interest + 50% of SS benefits

**Single filer thresholds**:
- Below $25,000: 0% of SS is taxable
- $25,000-$34,000: up to 50% of SS is taxable
- Above $34,000: up to 85% of SS is taxable

**MFJ thresholds**:
- Below $32,000: 0% of SS is taxable
- $32,000-$44,000: up to 50% of SS is taxable
- Above $44,000: up to 85% of SS is taxable

**Important**: These thresholds have NEVER been indexed for inflation since they were established in 1984 and 1993. As benefits increase with inflation and other income rises, more and more retirees pay tax on their Social Security. Today, approximately 60% of SS recipients pay some tax on their benefits.

**Planning implication**: Roth IRA withdrawals are NOT included in provisional income — they do not trigger Social Security taxability. This is one of the most powerful arguments for Roth conversions before collecting Social Security.

## Spouse Benefits, Survivor Benefits, and Divorced Spouse Benefits

### Spousal Benefit

A spouse is entitled to receive the greater of:
- Their own earned Social Security benefit based on their own work record, OR
- 50% of their spouse's PIA (at FRA)

**Not additive**: If your own benefit is $1,200 and your spouse's PIA is $3,000, your spousal benefit would be $1,500 (50% of $3,000). Since $1,500 > $1,200, you receive $1,500 — not $1,200 + $1,500.

**Claiming the spousal benefit early**: Claiming the spousal benefit before FRA reduces it below 50% of the worker's PIA. Claiming at FRA provides exactly 50%.

**The spousal benefit is not enhanced by delaying past FRA**: There are no delayed retirement credits for the spousal benefit beyond the worker's FRA. Delaying the spousal benefit past FRA only makes sense if the spouse's own benefit grows more due to delay.

### Survivor Benefits

A surviving spouse can claim the deceased spouse's benefit after the worker's death. Key rules:
- Surviving spouse can claim as early as age 60 (50 if disabled)
- Claiming before FRA reduces the survivor benefit
- At FRA: 100% of the deceased worker's benefit (including any delayed retirement credits the worker had earned)
- If the worker delayed to 70, the surviving spouse inherits the higher delayed benefit

**Maximization strategy for couples**: The higher earner should almost always delay to 70. When the higher earner dies first, the surviving spouse (often the lower earner) receives the higher earner's larger benefit for the rest of their life. This is the most powerful longevity insurance available to married couples.

### Divorced Spouse Benefit

A divorced spouse may claim on an ex-spouse's record if:
- The marriage lasted 10 or more years
- The claiming spouse is currently unmarried
- The ex-spouse is at least 62 years old (does not need to have filed; for marriages that ended before May 1, 2016, the ex-spouse must have filed first OR been divorced for at least 2 years)

**Key features**:
- The benefit is 50% of the ex-spouse's PIA (same as spousal benefit)
- Filing does NOT affect the ex-spouse's benefit or any benefits paid to a current spouse
- The ex-spouse is never notified you are claiming on their record

This benefit is often not claimed because divorced individuals are unaware of it or assume it doesn't apply if their ex-spouse has remarried (it doesn't matter — the divorced spouse benefit is available regardless of the ex-spouse's remarriage status, as long as the claimant does not remarry).

## Earnings Test for Early Claimers

If you claim Social Security before FRA and continue working:

**2025 earnings limit before FRA**: $22,320/year. For every $2 earned above this limit, $1 of Social Security benefit is withheld.

**Year of reaching FRA**: Higher limit ($59,520 in 2025); $1 withheld for every $3 over the limit. Only applies to months before FRA.

**After FRA**: No earnings limit — work as much as you want with no benefit reduction.

**The withheld benefits are NOT lost**: Benefits withheld due to the earnings test are credited back after FRA — your future monthly benefit is permanently increased to account for withheld months. But the breakeven for this recovery takes years — it is generally better to delay claiming rather than claim early and have benefits withheld.

## Social Security Trust Fund Solvency

The Social Security Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) trust fund is projected to be depleted around 2033-2035, according to the SSA Trustees' 2024 report. At depletion, payroll taxes collected would fund approximately 79-80% of scheduled benefits.

**Important context**:
- Congress has never allowed Social Security benefits to be cut at the benefit-reduction level that pure trust fund depletion would cause
- Historical precedent: the 1983 Social Security reforms averted a similar crisis by increasing the FRA, taxing higher-income beneficiaries, and other adjustments
- Multiple legislative options exist: increase the payroll tax, increase the wage base, means-test benefits, raise the FRA slightly, or combinations

**Planning approach**: 
- Do NOT assume 0% Social Security in retirement projections — this is overly pessimistic
- Consider a modest haircut (80-90% of scheduled benefits) in planning scenarios to reflect some legislative risk
- The political will to maintain Social Security at or near full benefits has been demonstrated through every previous solvency challenge

The best protection against Social Security uncertainty is to build robust retirement savings so that Social Security is a supplement, not the primary income source.
