# Rebalancing Strategies: Maintaining Your Target Allocation

## Why Rebalancing Matters

Investment portfolios drift from their target allocations over time as asset classes produce different returns. Without rebalancing, a portfolio starts at 60/40 equity/bond but drifts to 75/25 after a prolonged bull market — increasing risk beyond the investor's intended level. After a crash, the same portfolio might drift to 50/50 — reducing equity exposure precisely when future expected returns are highest.

The consequences of unmanaged drift:
- **Excessive risk during bull markets**: Portfolio becomes more equity-heavy just as valuations peak; deeper drawdown in the subsequent correction
- **Insufficient exposure during recoveries**: Bond-heavy portfolio after a crash misses the recovery; the investor is locked into a defensive allocation when buying opportunity is greatest
- **Psychological misalignment**: An investor who set a 60/40 allocation based on their risk tolerance is now experiencing the volatility of a 75/25 portfolio they never consciously chose

Rebalancing is the systematic discipline that prevents these outcomes. It enforces a contrarian behavior — selling what has outperformed and buying what has underperformed — which is the structural opposite of the emotional behavior most investors exhibit.

## Rebalancing Methods

### 1. Calendar Rebalancing

Rebalance on a fixed schedule regardless of how much the portfolio has drifted.

- **Annual**: Rebalance once per year, same calendar date; simplest to implement; minimizes transaction frequency
- **Quarterly**: Rebalance every three months; more responsive but generates more transactions and more taxable events
- **Monthly**: Generally not worth the additional transactions and tax cost for the marginal risk reduction provided

**Research finding**: Annual calendar rebalancing produces nearly identical risk/return outcomes as more frequent calendar rebalancing for typical portfolio volatility levels, with far fewer transactions.

### 2. Threshold Rebalancing

Rebalance only when an asset class drifts beyond a defined threshold from its target allocation.

- **5% threshold**: Rebalance when any asset class moves more than 5 percentage points from target (e.g., target 60% equity; rebalance when equity reaches 65% or falls to 55%)
- **Relative threshold**: Rebalance when an asset class moves more than 20-25% relative to its target (e.g., target 60%; rebalance at 75% or 45%)

**Research finding**: A 5% threshold is roughly equivalent in risk/return outcome to annual calendar rebalancing. It responds to market movements (rebalancing more often during high volatility) while ignoring minor fluctuations.

### 3. Hybrid: Calendar Plus Threshold

Check allocation on a quarterly or annual schedule but only rebalance if drift exceeds 5%. This combines the disciplines of both approaches:
- Regular review prevents neglect
- Threshold requirement prevents unnecessary transactions
- Particularly valuable for taxable accounts where every trade may have tax consequences

This hybrid is the approach recommended by Vanguard's internal research and is used in many advisory platforms.

## Rebalancing with New Contributions: Tax-Smart First Step

Before triggering any sales to rebalance, examine whether new contributions can do the work. Directing new money to underweight asset classes is the most tax-efficient form of rebalancing because:
- No asset sales means no realized capital gains
- Achieves the same allocation correction without triggering taxes
- Applies to: new 401(k) contributions, IRA contributions, employer match, dividends being reinvested

**Process**: Before adding new money to an existing allocation, check current weights. If bonds are underweight by 5%, direct the next contribution entirely to bonds. Most 401(k) platforms and brokerage accounts allow you to specify allocation of new contributions separately from the existing portfolio balance.

For most working-age investors contributing regularly, new contributions can handle most or all of the rebalancing need in typical market environments. Large drift corrections may still require asset sales.

## Tax Considerations in Taxable Accounts

Rebalancing in taxable accounts triggers taxable events. The tax cost must be weighed against the benefit of returning to target allocation.

**Key rules:**
- **Avoid selling positions with large embedded gains if possible**: The tax cost of recognizing a 40% gain may exceed the cost of running slightly off-target allocation
- **Use tax-loss harvesting to rebalance**: Sell losing positions (realize the tax loss), simultaneously buy a similar but not substantially identical fund — you reset the allocation AND generate a tax benefit
- **Use dividends and interest for rebalancing**: Redirect dividends from overweight positions to underweight positions rather than reinvesting them in place
- **Cross-account rebalancing**: If your total portfolio spans multiple accounts, consider rebalancing by adjusting the allocation within tax-advantaged accounts (no tax cost) rather than selling in the taxable account

**Practical approach for taxable accounts**: 
1. First, rebalance with new contributions
2. Then, redirect dividends/distributions to underweight assets
3. Then, rebalance within IRA/401(k) where no tax consequence
4. Last resort: sell in taxable — but consider whether tax loss harvesting opportunities exist

## Transaction Costs

Commission-free ETF trading at major brokers (Fidelity, Vanguard, Schwab, Charles Schwab) has eliminated the per-trade cost barrier. However, bid/ask spreads remain for ETFs, particularly for less liquid funds. For highly liquid funds (VTI, VOO, BND), the bid/ask spread is 0.01-0.02% — negligible. For less liquid factor ETFs, spreads may be 0.05-0.15% — still modest but not zero.

Mutual funds (purchased directly from Vanguard, Fidelity Zero funds) trade at NAV with no bid/ask spread — potentially cleaner for rebalancing in taxable accounts where precision matters.

## Over-Rebalancing Risk

More rebalancing is not always better. Excessive rebalancing creates:
- Unnecessary taxable events in taxable accounts (capital gains tax drag)
- Transaction friction (even if minimal with zero commissions, the process takes time and attention)
- Possible tax inefficiency from selling short-term positions (held less than 1 year) at higher short-term capital gains rates

Monthly rebalancing in a taxable account may cost more in taxes than the risk reduction benefit justifies. Annual rebalancing or threshold-based rebalancing with a 5% band is generally the optimal balance.

## Portfolio Size and Rebalancing Feasibility

**Small portfolio ($10,000-$50,000)**: Rebalancing with contributions is usually sufficient. Avoid triggering sales in a taxable account — the tax cost on a small portfolio is disproportionate to the risk correction benefit. Annual review, contribution-directed rebalancing.

**Medium portfolio ($50,000-$300,000)**: Annual or threshold rebalancing within tax-advantaged accounts; contribution-directed rebalancing in taxable. Actual sales in taxable only when drift exceeds 10%+ and no other options.

**Large portfolio ($300,000+)**: Full threshold-based monitoring with cross-account optimization. Formalize the Investment Policy Statement with explicit rebalancing rules. Consider whether tax-loss harvesting opportunities can be used to fund rebalancing.

## Automatic Rebalancing in Retirement Accounts

Most 401(k) and 403(b) platforms offer automatic annual rebalancing — a feature that automatically resets the portfolio to target allocation on a calendar basis. This should be turned on for virtually every participant.

Vanguard, Fidelity, and Schwab brokerage IRAs also offer automatic rebalancing for certain products (managed accounts, robo-advisors). The set-it-and-forget-it nature is valuable for investors who may not review their portfolio regularly.

## Behavioral Benefit of Rebalancing During Volatility

The most valuable aspect of rebalancing may be behavioral: it converts a systematic rule into action, overriding emotional responses during market crises. During a 30% equity drawdown:
- An investor without a rebalancing rule typically freezes or panics sells
- An investor with a 5% threshold rebalancing rule is forced by the rule to buy equities at lower prices

Investors who continued buying and rebalancing during March 2020 (the COVID crash) captured the subsequent recovery fully. Those who stopped — or sold — missed it. Having a written rebalancing policy before a crisis is critical because the policy is impossible to rationally adopt during the crisis when fear dominates.

## Rebalancing Frequency Research

Vanguard research (2019) found that for a 60/40 portfolio, the difference in annualized volatility between monthly, quarterly, and annual calendar rebalancing was approximately 0.1-0.2%. The risk reduction from more frequent rebalancing was minimal relative to the additional transaction cost and complexity. The conclusion: annual calendar rebalancing or 5% threshold rebalancing captures essentially all of the risk-management benefit of rebalancing without excessive trading.
