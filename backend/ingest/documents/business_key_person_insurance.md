# Key Person Insurance: Protecting the Business Against Critical Personnel Loss

## What Is Key Person Insurance

Key person insurance (also called key man insurance) is life and/or disability insurance purchased by a business on the life of individuals whose death or long-term disability would cause significant financial harm to the company. The business is the owner, premium payer, and beneficiary — not the insured individual.

The death benefit or disability benefit is paid to the company to use as it determines. Common uses include:
- Funding the recruitment and training of a replacement
- Covering lost revenue or profits during the transition period
- Paying off business debt or loan guarantees tied to the key person
- Funding a buy-sell agreement when a partner or co-owner dies or becomes disabled
- Reassuring lenders, investors, or customers during a leadership transition

## Who Qualifies as a Key Person

The definition of "key person" depends on the business, but common categories:

**Founders and CEOs**: The business may rely entirely on the founder's relationships, reputation, and vision. A founder's death can eliminate the customer pipeline, demoralize the team, and undermine ongoing contracts.

**Top revenue producers**: A salesperson who generates 30-50% of revenue is a key person. Their death or disability creates immediate cash flow disruption.

**Technical specialists**: An engineer with unique expertise in a proprietary process, a software developer who built and maintains the core product, a physician whose credentials enable the practice — these individuals have knowledge and skills that cannot be immediately replaced.

**Employees with critical external relationships**: The person who manages the relationship with your largest customer; the banker who ensures your line of credit; the primary government contractor contact.

## Company-Owned Life Insurance (COLI)

The business purchases a life insurance policy on the key person. The business pays all premiums, owns the policy, and is the beneficiary.

**Income tax treatment of death benefit**: Death benefits received by a corporation or business under a life insurance policy are generally tax-free under IRC §101(a). However, for corporate-owned life insurance, the COLI best practices rules under IRC §101(j) must be satisfied for the proceeds to remain tax-free.

**COLI best practices / IRC §101(j) requirements**:
Before the policy is issued, the following must occur:
1. The insured (employee) must receive written notice that the employer intends to purchase life insurance covering them
2. The insured must consent in writing to the coverage
3. The notice must state the maximum face amount of the coverage and that the employer will be the beneficiary
4. The insured must be an employee of the employer at the time the policy is issued

If these notice and consent requirements are NOT met, death benefits received by the employer are taxable to the extent they exceed the employer's basis (premiums paid). This can result in a significant unexpected tax liability.

**Who must consent**: Every covered employee. When setting up a COLI program covering multiple employees, each individual must separately receive notice and consent.

**Annual reporting**: On Form 8925, corporations must report the total number of COLI policies in force, the total face amount, and the employer's interest in the policies.

## Tax Treatment of Premiums and Proceeds

**Premiums are NOT deductible**: Corporate-paid COLI premiums are not deductible business expenses. The IRS views them as capital expenditures toward an asset (the policy) that will generate tax-free proceeds.

**Cash value growth is tax-deferred**: The inside build-up of cash value in permanent life insurance (whole life, universal life) accumulates tax-deferred. No tax is owed on the annual increase in cash value within the policy.

**Death benefit is tax-free** (with §101(j) compliance): Subject to the notice and consent requirements above, the death benefit paid to the corporation upon the insured's death is income-tax-free.

**Surrender or withdrawal of cash value**: If the business surrenders the policy or withdraws cash value, any amounts exceeding the premiums paid are taxable ordinary income.

## Key Person Disability Insurance

The risk of disability often exceeds the risk of death during working years. Key person disability insurance addresses the disability trigger.

**Business Overhead Expense (BOE) Insurance**:
- Covers the business's fixed operating expenses (rent, utilities, staff salaries, equipment leases) while the owner-key person is disabled
- Typically has a 30-60 day elimination period
- Benefit period: 12-24 months
- Benefit amount: actual qualified business expenses, not the owner's personal income
- Premiums may be deductible as a business expense (BOE benefits are then taxable when received, but offset deductible expenses)

**Key Person Disability Buyout Insurance**:
- Funds the buyout of a disabled partner's or co-owner's business interest
- Separate from the individual disability income insurance the owner needs personally
- Typically has a long elimination period (12-24 months) to confirm permanent disability before triggering the buyout
- Benefit pays out as a lump sum or series of payments to fund the buy-sell agreement

**Personal Disability Income Insurance**: The key person also needs their own individual disability income policy (see disability income insurance document) — this covers their personal income replacement, not the business.

## Split-Dollar Life Insurance

Split-dollar is an arrangement where the employer and employee share the costs and benefits of a life insurance policy. It can be used as an executive benefit or as a mechanism for the employer to recover premium costs at the employee's death.

**Two regulatory regimes govern split-dollar arrangements (IRS Notice 2002-8)**:

### Loan Regime (More Common)
The employer's premium payments are treated as loans to the employee. The employee accrues interest on the loan balance (at the AFR). At the employee's death, the employer recovers the full loan balance from the death benefit, and the excess passes to the employee's named beneficiaries.

- Tax benefit to the employee: access to insurance coverage (possibly substantial death benefit) with the employer funding the premium as a loan
- Tax to employee: imputed interest on the below-market loan (if AFR not charged) = taxable compensation
- No deduction for employer on the loan advance

### Economic Benefit Regime (Older Arrangements)
The employee is taxed annually on the economic benefit of the current insurance protection (the "PS 58 costs" or current published rates). The employer retains the cash value, and the employee's beneficiaries receive only the pure insurance amount (death benefit minus cash value).

- Taxable income to employee: the Table 2001 rates (published by IRS) for the "economic benefit" of the death protection
- Often used in grandfathered arrangements; the loan regime is generally more tax-efficient for new arrangements

Split-dollar arrangements are complex and require specialized legal and tax counsel. They should not be designed or implemented without expert guidance.

## COLI as Executive Benefit Funding Mechanism

Beyond key person protection, COLI (particularly in its BOLI — Bank-Owned Life Insurance — form for financial institutions) is used as a tax-efficient vehicle to fund nonqualified deferred compensation obligations.

**The structure**:
1. Company has a nonqualified deferred compensation obligation to an executive (promises to pay $2M at retirement)
2. Company purchases a COLI policy on the executive with a large death benefit
3. Cash value inside the COLI grows tax-deferred, financing the deferred comp liability
4. At executive retirement: cash value accessed via tax-free policy loans to fund the deferred comp payments
5. At executive death: death benefit received tax-free; offsets remaining deferred comp liability or provides additional benefit

**Tax advantages**:
- COLI cash value grows tax-deferred (unlike taxable investments used to informally fund deferred comp)
- Death benefit received tax-free (recovers premium and more)
- Net cost to fund executive benefit is significantly lower than using taxable investment alternatives

**Limitations**:
- Requires §101(j) notice and consent compliance
- Internal charges in permanent COLI policies reduce effective investment return
- IRC §264 limits deductibility of interest on COLI policy loans
- Detailed actuarial and tax modeling required to ensure cost-effectiveness

## Sizing Key Person Coverage

Common approaches to sizing key person life insurance:

**Revenue contribution method**: Annual revenue attributed to the key person × 3-5 years (transition period). A salesperson generating $2M/year in revenue: $6M-$10M in coverage.

**Profit contribution method**: Annual profit contribution × capitalization multiple. If the key person's unique skills contribute $300,000/year in profit, and the business trades at 4× EBITDA, the key person's contribution is worth $1.2M.

**Loan guarantee coverage**: If the key person has personally guaranteed business debt, at minimum insure the full guarantee amount.

**Buy-sell funding**: If the key person is a co-owner, the death benefit must be sufficient to fund the buy-sell agreement at the company's current valuation — requires the coverage to be updated alongside business valuation updates.

Lenders sometimes require key person insurance as a condition of business loans, particularly for small businesses heavily dependent on the owner-operator.
