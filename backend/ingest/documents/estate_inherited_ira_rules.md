# Inherited IRA Rules: SECURE Act and SECURE 2.0 Framework

## The Elimination of the Stretch IRA

Before the SECURE Act (effective January 1, 2020), most non-spouse beneficiaries who inherited an IRA could take required minimum distributions over their own life expectancy — the "stretch IRA" strategy. A 30-year-old inheriting a $1M IRA could spread distributions over 50+ years, allowing most of the account to continue compounding.

The SECURE Act ended the stretch IRA for most non-spouse beneficiaries for accounts inherited after December 31, 2019. This is one of the most significant changes to retirement account planning in decades.

## The 10-Year Rule (Non-Eligible Designated Beneficiaries)

Most non-spouse beneficiaries must now empty the inherited account by December 31 of the 10th year following the year of the account owner's death.

**Year counting**: If the owner dies in 2025, the 10-year clock starts. The account must be fully distributed by December 31, 2035.

### Annual RMD Requirement Within the 10-Year Period

The SECURE Act originally seemed to allow complete flexibility — take nothing for 9 years and empty in year 10. The IRS then issued Proposed Regulations in 2022 with a critical clarification:

**If the account owner died on or after their Required Beginning Date (RBD)**: Beneficiaries must take annual distributions in years 1 through 9, then the remaining balance in year 10. Distributions are calculated using the beneficiary's single life expectancy.

**If the account owner died before their RBD**: No annual distributions are required in years 1 through 9. The beneficiary can take distributions at any time in any amount, as long as the account is empty by year 10.

**Required Beginning Date**: April 1 following the year the account owner reached their RMD age (age 73 for those born 1951-1959; age 75 for those born 1960 or later, per SECURE 2.0).

### Planning Implication

For heirs under the 10-year rule with no required annual distributions, the optimal distribution strategy is typically:
- Take zero or minimal distributions in years 1-9 if in a high income bracket currently
- Model the tax impact of different distribution patterns — taking the full balance in year 10 may push the beneficiary into the 37% bracket
- Consider spreading distributions over years based on income projections (retirement, lower income years, bracket management)
- For large inherited IRAs, the 10-year distribution period still compresses what was a lifetime distribution into 10 years — substantial tax planning is required

## Eligible Designated Beneficiaries: Who Can Still Stretch

Five categories of beneficiaries are "Eligible Designated Beneficiaries" (EDBs) who can still take distributions over their own life expectancy (the stretch):

1. **Surviving spouse**: Retains unique options including rollover treatment (see below)

2. **Minor child of the deceased account owner**: Can stretch until reaching age 21. At age 21, the minor's status as an EDB ends and the 10-year rule begins from that point

3. **Disabled individual**: Determined under IRC §72(m)(7) — unable to engage in any substantial gainful activity due to a physical or mental impairment expected to be of long-continued and indefinite duration or to result in death

4. **Chronically ill individual**: Defined under IRC §7702B(c)(2) — requires substantial assistance with daily living activities or severe cognitive impairment

5. **Individual not more than 10 years younger than the deceased owner**: A sibling, friend, or any person whose birth year is within 10 years of the deceased owner can still stretch distributions

For EDBs, the distribution is still based on the single life expectancy table (Uniform Lifetime Table does not apply here — use the Single Life Expectancy Table for inherited IRAs).

## Surviving Spouse: The Special Rules

A surviving spouse has options unique to their status:

### Option 1: Roll into Own IRA
Roll the inherited IRA into the surviving spouse's own Traditional IRA (for inherited Traditional IRA) or own Roth IRA (for inherited Roth IRA). Key effect: the account is now treated as the surviving spouse's own — RMDs are based on the surviving spouse's own RMD rules, using the Uniform Lifetime Table.

**Best for**: Surviving spouse who is younger than the deceased and wants to delay RMDs as long as possible.

**Important**: Once rolled over to the surviving spouse's own IRA, any distribution before age 59½ is subject to the 10% early withdrawal penalty. If the surviving spouse is under 59½ and needs distributions, keeping the account as an inherited IRA avoids the penalty.

### Option 2: Treat as Own IRA
Same tax result as Option 1 — elect to treat the inherited account as the surviving spouse's own.

### Option 3: Remain as Inherited IRA
Keep the account as an inherited IRA. The surviving spouse can take distributions using the more favorable of: (a) their own single life expectancy, or (b) the deceased spouse's remaining life expectancy.

**Best for**: Surviving spouse under age 59½ who needs to take distributions without penalty. Inherited IRA distributions are not subject to the 10% early withdrawal penalty regardless of the beneficiary's age.

**Post-SECURE 2.0 change**: Beginning in 2024, a surviving spouse can use the Uniform Lifetime Table for RMDs from an inherited IRA if the deceased spouse was the same age or older — treating the account as if the surviving spouse were the original owner for RMD calculation purposes. This is favorable for surviving spouses older than the deceased.

## The 5-Year Rule: Non-Designated Beneficiaries

If an IRA is inherited by a non-designated beneficiary — an estate, a charity, or a trust that does not qualify as a see-through trust — the distribution rules differ:

- **Owner died before RBD**: Entire account must be distributed within 5 years of the year of death
- **Owner died on or after RBD**: Distributions can continue over the owner's remaining life expectancy as of the year of death

Estates cannot benefit from the 10-year rule or the stretch — this is a reason never to name an estate as IRA beneficiary.

## Planning Strategies for Account Owners

### Roth IRA Conversions Before Death

Converting Traditional IRA assets to Roth IRA before death provides heirs with inherited Roth IRAs. The benefits:
- Distributions from inherited Roth IRAs are income-tax-free regardless of when taken during the 10-year period
- The account owner pays the income tax now (at their current bracket), converting what would be ordinary income tax to heirs into tax-free distributions
- Heirs still face the 10-year rule for non-EDB beneficiaries, but the distributions have no income tax consequence

For account owners projecting that their heirs will be in high tax brackets during the 10-year distribution period, Roth conversion before death is often the most compelling planning strategy.

### Leave IRA Assets to Youngest Heirs

Among beneficiaries subject to the 10-year rule, there is no advantage to leaving large IRAs to older beneficiaries over younger ones — all face the same 10-year window. However, for EDB beneficiaries, younger individuals have longer expected life spans and thus longer stretch periods. Designating a chronically ill or disabled beneficiary (who qualifies as EDB) for a large IRA may provide valuable planning flexibility.

### Use IRA as Charitable Bequest

Charities do not pay income tax. An IRA left to a qualified charity is received entirely income-tax-free by the charity — 100% of the balance is used for charitable purposes. By contrast, an IRA left to family members is fully taxed as ordinary income.

For charitably inclined individuals, leaving IRAs to charity (and leaving Roth IRAs or taxable brokerage accounts to family) is the most tax-efficient bequest strategy:
- Family receives stepped-up basis assets or tax-free Roth assets
- Charity receives the pre-tax IRA with no income tax
- Estate value to family is maximized

### Trust as IRA Beneficiary: Proceed With Caution

Post-SECURE Act, trusts as IRA beneficiaries are significantly more complex. The trust must meet see-through trust requirements. For conduit trusts (which pass all distributions through to beneficiaries), the 10-year rule applies to the trust as if the oldest trust beneficiary were a direct beneficiary. For accumulation trusts, the analysis is even more complex.

Any trust intended to be an IRA beneficiary should be reviewed by an estate attorney with current knowledge of post-SECURE Act rules. Many pre-2020 trust designs are no longer optimal.
