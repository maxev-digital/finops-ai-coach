# Social Security Optimization

## Full Retirement Age (FRA) by Birth Year

Full Retirement Age is the age at which a worker receives 100% of their Primary Insurance Amount (PIA).

| Birth Year | Full Retirement Age |
|---|---|
| 1943–1954 | 66 |
| 1955 | 66 and 2 months |
| 1956 | 66 and 4 months |
| 1957 | 66 and 6 months |
| 1958 | 66 and 8 months |
| 1959 | 66 and 10 months |
| 1960 and later | **67** |

The vast majority of current pre-retirees (born 1960+) have an FRA of 67.

## Early Claiming: Permanent Benefit Reduction

Claiming before FRA permanently reduces the monthly benefit. The reduction is calculated per month:

- **36 months or fewer before FRA:** Benefit reduced by 5/9 of 1% per month (approximately 6.67% per year)
- **More than 36 months before FRA:** Additional months reduced at 5/12 of 1% per month (approximately 5% per year)

**For a worker with FRA of 67 claiming at 62:**
- 60 months early total
- First 36 months: 36 × (5/9 × 1%) = 20% reduction
- Next 24 months: 24 × (5/12 × 1%) = 10% reduction
- **Total reduction: 30% permanently** — receive 70% of PIA for life

**Example:** PIA = $2,400/month at FRA 67. Claiming at 62 = $1,680/month. Claiming at 67 = $2,400/month. This differential compounds over decades.

## Delayed Claiming: Delayed Retirement Credits (DRCs)

For each month a worker delays claiming past FRA (up to age 70), the benefit increases by **2/3 of 1% per month**, or **8% per year**.

From FRA 67 to age 70 = 36 months × (2/3 × 1%) = **24% increase**.

**Example:** PIA = $2,400/month at FRA 67. Claiming at 70 = $2,400 × 1.24 = **$2,976/month** — 24% higher than FRA and 77% higher than the age-62 benefit.

Delayed credits stop accruing at age 70. There is no benefit to waiting past age 70.

## Breakeven Analysis

The crossover (breakeven) age is when cumulative lifetime benefits from delayed claiming exceed cumulative lifetime benefits from early claiming.

**FRA 67 vs. age 62 claiming:**
- Age 62: 8 extra years of benefits before FRA, but permanently lower amount
- FRA benefit is 43% higher than age-62 benefit (70% vs 100% of PIA)
- Typical breakeven: approximately age 78–80

**FRA 67 vs. age 70 claiming:**
- FRA benefit is 19% lower than age-70 benefit (100% vs. 124%)
- Age 70 claimant "loses" 3 years of benefits by waiting
- Typical breakeven: approximately age 80–83

**Mortality context:** Average male life expectancy at 65 ≈ 84; average female ≈ 87. A healthy non-smoking couple at 65 has a 50%+ probability that at least one spouse lives to 90. Delayed claiming is generally the statistically superior choice for those in average or above-average health.

**When early claiming may be justified:** Serious health condition or family history of early mortality, immediate financial need, or lower-earning spouse who will receive spousal benefit regardless.

## Spousal Benefit

A spouse (including divorced spouse) can claim Social Security based on the worker's record rather than their own work history.

**Maximum spousal benefit:** 50% of the worker's Primary Insurance Amount (PIA at FRA, not the delayed or early amount).

**Conditions for spousal benefit:**
- The worker must be receiving their own Social Security benefit (collecting)
- The claiming spouse must be at least age 62
- Spousal benefit does not accrue delayed credits past FRA — no benefit to delaying spousal benefit past FRA

**Reduced spousal benefit:** If the spouse claims before their own FRA, the spousal benefit is permanently reduced (same early claiming reduction formula).

**Deeming:** Since the 2015 Bipartisan Budget Act, spouses are "deemed" to file for both their own benefit and spousal benefit simultaneously. A spouse cannot strategically claim spousal benefit only while letting their own benefit grow — unless they were born before January 2, 1954 (see restricted application below).

**Spousal benefit vs. own benefit:** Social Security pays the higher of the two. If the spouse's own earned benefit (at their FRA) is more than 50% of the worker's PIA, the spouse will receive their own benefit and the spousal benefit is irrelevant.

## Survivor Benefit

The surviving spouse can claim a benefit based on the deceased worker's record.

**Maximum survivor benefit:** Up to **100% of the deceased worker's actual benefit** — including any delayed credits the deceased worker had accumulated.

**Critical distinction from spousal benefit:** Survivor benefit is based on what the deceased worker *was receiving* (or would have received), not their PIA. If the deceased worker delayed to age 70 and was receiving $3,000/month, the survivor can receive up to $3,000/month.

**Early survivor claiming:** Survivors can claim as early as age 60 (50 if disabled). Claiming before FRA permanently reduces the survivor benefit.

**Key strategy implication:** The higher earner delaying to age 70 maximizes the survivor benefit for the spouse who outlives them. This is often the most important planning decision for married couples — the delayed benefit becomes a de facto joint-and-survivor annuity at the higher amount.

**Survivor benefit at any age:** A surviving spouse can switch between their own benefit and the survivor benefit at any point if one exceeds the other. For example, claim survivor at 60 at a reduced rate while letting own benefit grow to 70, then switch to own benefit.

## Divorced Spouse Benefits

A divorced spouse may claim Social Security based on an ex-spouse's record under these conditions:
- Marriage lasted at least **10 years**
- Both parties are at least age 62
- Claimant is currently unmarried
- The claiming divorce spouse's own benefit is less than what they would receive as a divorced spouse

**No coordination required:** The ex-spouse does NOT need to be collecting, and claiming divorced spouse benefits does NOT affect the ex-spouse's benefit or the ex-spouse's current spouse's benefit. This is a complete independence — multiple ex-spouses can all claim divorced spouse benefits simultaneously.

**Survivor benefits for divorced spouses:** A divorced spouse who was married 10+ years can also claim survivor benefits upon the ex-spouse's death, subject to the same rules as married survivors.

## Restricted Application (Born Before January 2, 1954 Only)

The **restricted application** strategy allows an eligible spouse to claim spousal-only benefits at FRA while deferring their own benefit to grow to age 70.

**Who can use this:** Only individuals born before January 2, 1954 — a very small and dwindling population. This group was "grandfathered" from the 2015 deeming rules.

**Mechanics:** At FRA, file a restricted application for spousal benefit only (explicitly stating the intent to restrict). Collect 50% of spouse's PIA for 3 years. At age 70, switch to own benefit with full delayed credits.

**This strategy is no longer available to the general population.** Anyone born January 2, 1954 or later is subject to deeming and cannot restrict their application.

## Earnings Test (Before Full Retirement Age)

If a beneficiary claims Social Security benefits before FRA and continues working, benefits may be temporarily withheld based on earnings:

**2025 earnings limits:**

- **Before the year of FRA:** $1 withheld for every $2 earned above **$22,320/year** (approximately $1,860/month)
- **In the calendar year of reaching FRA:** $1 withheld for every $3 earned above **$59,520/year** — and only earnings in months before the FRA birthday count
- **At and after FRA:** No earnings limit — earn unlimited income without benefit reduction

**Withheld benefits are recredited:** Benefits withheld due to the earnings test are not permanently lost. At FRA, SSA recalculates the benefit upward to credit the withheld months. The recrediting is permanent and increases the FRA benefit.

**Important:** The earnings test applies only to *wages and self-employment income*, not to investment income, rental income, pension income, or IRA/401(k) distributions.

## Social Security Benefit Taxation

Social Security benefits become taxable when **provisional income** (PI) exceeds certain thresholds:

**Provisional Income = AGI + Tax-Exempt Interest (municipal bonds) + 50% of Social Security Benefits**

| Provisional Income | Single Filer | MFJ Filer | % of SS Taxable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Below lower threshold | Below $25,000 | Below $32,000 | 0% |
| Lower threshold to upper | $25,000–$34,000 | $32,000–$44,000 | Up to 50% |
| Above upper threshold | Above $34,000 | Above $44,000 | Up to 85% |

The maximum taxable percentage is 85% — Social Security benefits are never 100% taxable at the federal level.

**Thresholds are NOT inflation-indexed** — they have not changed since 1983/1993. With inflation, an increasing percentage of retirees pay tax on their Social Security benefits over time.

**Planning implication:** Traditional IRA distributions increase AGI and provisional income. Roth IRA distributions do not. Clients with large pre-tax retirement accounts should consider Roth conversions before Social Security begins to reduce provisional income and the percentage of SS benefits that become taxable.

## Optimal Claiming Strategy for Couples

For married couples, the consensus optimal strategy in most situations:

1. **Higher earner delays to age 70.** This maximizes the survivor benefit (the couple's "life insurance"). The higher earner's delay also produces the largest absolute dollar increase per year of waiting (8% of a larger PIA).

2. **Lower earner claims early (age 62–FRA).** The lower earner's early claiming provides income to the household during the waiting period, reducing the need to draw down investment portfolio while the higher earner's benefit grows.

3. **Net result:** The couple receives income from the lower earner's SS plus portfolio distributions for 5–8 years. When the higher earner claims at 70, household SS income increases substantially. If the higher earner dies first, the surviving spouse switches to the larger survivor benefit for the rest of their life.

**Coordination with IRMAA:** Social Security income (to the extent taxable) increases MAGI, which affects Medicare Part B and D premium surcharges (IRMAA). This is a secondary but real consideration in claiming timing.

**Coordination with RMDs:** The combination of required minimum distributions from traditional IRAs (starting at 73 or 75) and Social Security can push retirees into significantly higher brackets. Roth conversions between retirement and RMD age, and SS delay timing, should be coordinated to minimize this tax compression.
