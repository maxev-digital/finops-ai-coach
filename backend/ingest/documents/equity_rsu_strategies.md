# RSU Strategies: Tax Planning and Diversification for Restricted Stock Units

## RSU Basics

Restricted Stock Units (RSUs) are a form of equity compensation where the company promises to deliver a specific number of shares (or the cash equivalent) to the employee upon meeting vesting conditions — typically continued employment for a defined period.

**Key characteristics**:
- **No purchase required**: Unlike stock options, RSUs require nothing from the employee — you don't buy the shares, you receive them
- **No expiration**: RSUs don't expire worthless; as long as you are employed and the vesting conditions are met, you receive shares
- **Vesting schedules**: Most RSUs vest on a time-based schedule (e.g., 25% per year over 4 years, or monthly over 3 years). Some have performance conditions (performance RSUs)
- **Value at vesting**: Equal to the number of shares vested × the stock price on the vest date

## Tax Treatment at Vesting: The Ordinary Income Event

When RSUs vest, the entire fair market value of the vested shares is recognized as ordinary compensation income — not capital gains. This is a mandatory, unavoidable tax event.

**At vesting**:
- Income recognized = Number of shares vesting × FMV on vest date
- Reported on your W-2 as "wages"
- Subject to federal and state income tax, FICA (Social Security and Medicare taxes)
- Employer withholds taxes (through share withholding or cash supplemental withholding)

**Cost basis established at vesting**: The FMV on the vest date becomes your cost basis in the shares. This is important for calculating future gain or loss when you sell.

**Holding period starts at vesting**: If you hold shares after vesting, the holding period for capital gains purposes begins on the vest date — not any earlier date.

## Tax at Vesting vs. Tax at Sale

**If you sell immediately at vesting** (same day or within a few days):
- You recognize ordinary income at the vest price
- No additional gain or loss (the sale price equals the basis)
- Clean, simple outcome

**If you hold shares after vesting and the price rises**:
- At vesting: ordinary income on the vest price
- At sale: capital gains on the appreciation above vest price
- Hold more than 1 year after vest date → long-term capital gains rate (0/15/20%)
- Hold less than 1 year after vest date → short-term capital gains rate (ordinary income rates)

**If you hold shares after vesting and the price falls**:
- At vesting: ordinary income was recognized on the full vest price
- At sale: capital loss on the decline from vest price — but capital losses can only offset capital gains (with $3,000/year allowed against ordinary income)
- Result: you may have paid ordinary income tax on $100/share and now own a stock worth $60/share — you have a capital loss, but it may not fully offset the ordinary income already paid

**The key insight**: The holding decision after vesting is entirely separate from the compensation income already recognized. Decide whether to hold or sell based on: (1) investment merit of the stock, (2) concentration risk, (3) tax bracket when capital gains would be recognized.

## The Withholding Problem: Tax Underpayment Risk

RSU vesting is treated as supplemental wages, and employers typically withhold at the **22% flat supplemental withholding rate** (the minimum for most supplemental payments). If your actual marginal rate is 32%, 35%, or 37%, the 22% withholding creates a significant underpayment.

**Example**: 1,000 RSUs vest at $50/share = $50,000 in income. Employer withholds 22% = $11,000. If your actual marginal rate is 37%, you owe: $50,000 × 37% = $18,500 in federal tax. Shortfall: $7,500 — due on April 15.

**Remedies**:
1. **Adjust W-4 withholding**: Reduce allowances on W-4 to have more withheld from regular wages each paycheck throughout the year
2. **Make quarterly estimated tax payments**: Each quarter (April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15), pay the expected shortfall on a quarterly basis to avoid underpayment penalties
3. **Sell shares at vesting to cover additional tax**: Even if you plan to hold, selling enough shares to cover the tax shortfall prevents a large April surprise

Underpayment penalties apply if you owe more than $1,000 in taxes on your return AND didn't pay at least 90% of current year tax or 100% of prior year tax (110% if AGI exceeded $150,000). Large RSU vesting events can easily create underpayment penalty situations if not proactively managed.

## Concentration Risk: The RSU Accumulation Problem

RSUs create employer stock concentration through automatic accumulation. An employee receiving RSU grants annually over 4-5 years can accumulate 30-50% of investable assets in a single stock — often without consciously making an investment decision to hold it.

**The dual exposure problem**: You receive compensation when your employer does well, and your investment portfolio also grows when your employer does well. You lose your job AND your savings simultaneously when your employer struggles — the worst possible correlation for financial security.

**Best practice for most employees**: Sell RSUs promptly at vesting and reinvest in a diversified portfolio. The decision to hold concentrated employer stock should be a deliberate, considered investment decision — not inertia.

**The "vest and sell" default**: For employees without a specific reason to hold concentrated stock (expectation of continued significant appreciation, inside knowledge of pending catalysts), vest-and-sell is the rational default. The ordinary income tax is unavoidable at vesting regardless of whether you sell.

## RSU Tax Planning Strategies

### Charitable Donation of Vested RSUs

If you are charitably inclined, donating RSU shares immediately after vesting (or before sale) eliminates the capital gains entirely and provides a charitable deduction at FMV:

1. RSUs vest; ordinary income recognized (unavoidable)
2. Instead of selling, immediately donate shares to a donor-advised fund (DAF) or directly to a charity
3. You deduct the FMV at donation (same as the vest price if donated immediately) — effectively deducting the compensation income
4. No capital gains on the donation
5. Net tax impact: ordinary income + charitable deduction = reduced net tax

This strategy is most powerful when:
- You are charitably inclined anyway
- The ordinary income from RSUs pushes you into a higher bracket
- You have other highly appreciated positions to donate separately for ongoing charitable giving

### Timing Vesting Events

Some employers offer employees the ability to defer RSU vesting or choose among vest dates (less common). If your income will be significantly lower in an adjacent year (planned sabbatical, parental leave, business loss year), deferring vesting into that year reduces the ordinary income tax on vesting.

This is only possible if the plan document allows it and must comply with IRC §409A (nonqualified deferred compensation rules).

### RSU Planning for High Earners Above $1M

When supplemental wages exceed $1,000,000 in a year, the withholding rate on the excess shifts to 37% (the maximum individual rate). For very high earners with large RSU events, this automatic maximum withholding reduces the underpayment risk. However, FICA caps at the Social Security wage base ($176,100 in 2025), so the employer's FICA withholding obligation on RSUs above the SS base is only 1.45% Medicare (or 2.35% including NIIT for high earners with no SS cap).

## RSUs in Private Companies

RSUs in pre-IPO private companies create a unique planning challenge: the RSUs vest (creating a taxable income event), but the shares cannot be sold because there is no public market.

**The tax problem**: You owe ordinary income tax on the FMV of vested shares (based on the most recent 409A independent valuation) — in cash — even though you cannot sell the shares to generate that cash.

**Sources of cash to pay the tax**:
- Employer offers net-settlement (withholds shares and pays taxes; you receive fewer shares but no out-of-pocket tax payment)
- Employer-sponsored tender offer or secondary market opportunity
- Personal savings

**Liquidity planning for private company employees**: Maintain liquid savings sufficient to cover anticipated tax on vesting events. Model the 409A valuation and timing of vest dates to plan cash reserves accordingly.

**409A valuation timing**: If the company has recently raised a round at a significantly higher valuation than the most recent 409A, there may be a window where RSUs vest at a lower 409A valuation — timing vest events relative to 409A updates can matter.

## RSU vs. Stock Options: Quick Comparison

| Feature | RSU | NSO/ISO |
|---------|-----|---------|
| Purchase required | No | Yes (at strike price) |
| Expiration | No | Yes (10 years, 90 days post-termination) |
| Value when price falls below grant price | Yes — still worth FMV at vest | No — option expires worthless below strike |
| Tax at grant | None | None (ISO/NSO) |
| Tax at vest/exercise | Ordinary income on full FMV (RSU) / Spread (NSO) | NSO: ordinary income on spread; ISO: AMT preference item |
| Tax at sale | Capital gains on post-vest appreciation | Capital gains on post-exercise appreciation (ISO: if holding requirements met) |
| Suitable for: | Most employees at any stage | Employees who want upside leverage; executives with high growth expectations |
