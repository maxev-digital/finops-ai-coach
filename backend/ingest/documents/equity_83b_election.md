# The 83(b) Election: Locking In Tax at Grant for Maximum Capital Gains Treatment

## What Is an 83(b) Election

Section 83(b) of the Internal Revenue Code allows a taxpayer who receives "property subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture" to elect to be taxed on the current fair market value at the time of receipt — rather than waiting until the forfeiture restrictions lapse (vesting).

In practical terms, an 83(b) election says to the IRS: "Tax me now on what this is worth today, and treat everything that happens after as capital gain."

Without an 83(b) election, each vesting event is a taxable ordinary income event at the FMV on the vest date. With an 83(b) election, you pay ordinary income tax once at grant (typically when the value is low or zero), and all future appreciation is capital gains.

## The 30-Day Hard Deadline

**The most critical fact about 83(b) elections**: The election must be filed with the IRS within **30 calendar days** of the date of grant or early exercise. This is an absolute, non-waivable, non-extendable deadline.

- Miss the 30-day window by a single day: permanently ineligible; cannot file a late election
- No exceptions: illness, ignorance, attorney or accountant error — none of these are grounds for relief
- Calendar days (not business days): weekends and holidays count
- The IRS will not remind you; you must remember independently

**Filing mechanics**:
1. Complete the election: write a letter to the IRS specifying (a) your name, address, SSN, (b) description of the property received, (c) date of transfer, (d) nature of restrictions, (e) FMV at time of transfer, (f) amount paid for the property if any
2. File one copy with the IRS service center where you file your return (mailed, not electronically — as of current practice)
3. Keep one copy for your records
4. Include a copy with your tax return for the year of grant
5. Provide a copy to the company (optional but strongly recommended — confirms the election)
6. Some advisors recommend filing via certified mail with return receipt to document the filing date

## When 83(b) Makes Sense: The Startup Employee Case Study

**The classic case**: Early-stage startup grants restricted stock to a co-founder at $0.001 per share when the company is worth $0.01/share (per 409A). The stock vests over 4 years.

**Without 83(b)**:
- Year 1: 25% vests; stock now worth $0.50/share; ordinary income on the vest = $0.50 × shares
- Year 2: 25% vests; stock now worth $2.00/share; ordinary income at $2.00 × shares
- Year 3: 25% vests; stock now worth $5.00/share; ordinary income at $5.00 × shares
- Year 4: 25% vests; stock now worth $10.00/share; ordinary income at $10.00 × shares

Total ordinary income: large and growing; tax paid in cash even if the stock is illiquid.

**With 83(b)** (filed within 30 days of grant at $0.001/share):
- At grant: tiny or zero ordinary income ($0.001 × shares)
- Years 1-4: no tax event at each vesting — vesting of already-taxed property
- At eventual sale: all appreciation above $0.001/share = long-term capital gains

The 83(b) election converts years of potentially large ordinary income events into a single minimal tax at grant, with all appreciation taxed at favorable capital gains rates.

## Quantified Benefit: The Tax Math

**Scenario**: 1,000,000 shares restricted stock, grant at $0.01 FMV, sell 5 years later at $10/share.

**Without 83(b)** (assume linear vesting over 4 years, stock appreciates linearly):
- Annual ordinary income during vesting: approximately $250,000 × 4 years = $1,000,000 total ordinary income
- Income tax at 37%: approximately $370,000
- At sale: 1,000,000 shares × $10 − basis ($10/share FMV at last vest) = minimal additional gain

**With 83(b)**:
- At grant: 1,000,000 × $0.01 = $10,000 ordinary income; tax: ~$3,700
- At sale: 1,000,000 × ($10 − $0.01) = $9,990,000 long-term capital gain
- Capital gains tax at 20% + 3.8% NIIT: approximately $2,374,620

**Wait — doesn't the 83(b) result in MORE total tax?**

At first glance: $3,700 + $2,374,620 (83(b)) vs. $370,000 + some capital gains (no 83(b)). Let's complete the no-83(b) calculation:
- No 83(b): $370,000 in ordinary income tax during vesting + basis steps up to $10/share → approximately $0 capital gains at sale
- With 83(b): $3,700 at grant + $2,374,620 at sale = $2,378,320

**Conclusion**: On very large exits, the no-83(b) approach saves more total federal tax due to ordinary income rate capping the gain in the vesting years. However, this ignores that the no-83(b) approach requires cash payment of $370,000 during the vesting years — potentially illiquid years. The 83(b) defers all taxes to the sale event.

**Where 83(b) is unambiguously better**: When the stock price rises dramatically after vesting (company IPOs or is acquired at a high valuation), the no-83(b) approach generates massive ordinary income. The 83(b) converts this to capital gains.

**The breakeven**: 83(b) is superior when the fair market value at grant is low and the stock price rise from vesting-period FMV exceeds the ordinary income rate premium over capital gains rate.

## When 83(b) Does NOT Make Sense

**High current FMV making ordinary income large at election**: If the stock is already worth $50/share when you receive a grant of 100,000 shares, filing an 83(b) triggers $5,000,000 of ordinary income immediately. Unless you are extremely confident in continued appreciation and can pay the tax, this may not be worth it.

**Company not expected to grow significantly**: The 83(b) benefit derives from future appreciation. If the company is unlikely to grow much, paying ordinary income now at a minimal spread doesn't generate meaningful capital gains benefit.

**High probability of forfeiture**: If there is a significant probability you will leave before vesting (high expected turnover, uncertain commitment to the company), filing an 83(b) and then forfeiting the stock leaves you with paid taxes and no refund for the forfeited property. You cannot get a refund of taxes paid under an 83(b) election on subsequently forfeited property.

**Property is already fully vested**: 83(b) only applies to property subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture. Fully vested property is already taxable and cannot benefit from an 83(b) election.

## Profits Interests: The LLC/Partnership 83(b)

Employees and advisors at LLCs and partnerships are often compensated with "profits interests" — a form of equity that participates only in future appreciation above the current enterprise value.

**Key characteristics of profits interests**:
- Issued at no current value (by definition, they only participate in future profits above current value)
- Taxed as capital gains when the holder later sells or the company is sold (if held properly)
- NOT immediately taxable as ordinary income at grant if properly structured (per IRS Revenue Procedure 93-27 and 2001-43)

**The 83(b) and profits interests**: Even though profits interests are not currently taxable, filing an 83(b) election at grant is standard practice. The election:
- Locks in the $0 current value
- Starts the holding period clock for long-term capital gains
- Ensures that future appreciation is characterized as capital gains from the earliest possible date
- Prevents any ambiguity if the IRS later argues the interests had some value at grant

**Without an 83(b) on a profits interest**: If the profits interest vests over time and the company appreciates during vesting, each vesting event could potentially be characterized as an ordinary income event — defeating the fundamental tax advantage of equity compensation in a partnership structure.

Filing an 83(b) on a profits interest is nearly always appropriate and is standard practice recommended by every startup attorney.

## Early-Exercised ISOs and 83(b)

For incentive stock options that allow early exercise (exercise before vesting), the 83(b) election interacts with the ISO holding requirements:

- Early exercise of unvested ISOs creates "property subject to substantial risk of forfeiture"
- Filing an 83(b) election starts the ISO holding period for both the 1-year (from exercise) and 2-year (from grant) requirements
- The 83(b) election on early-exercised ISOs with a minimal spread also minimizes the AMT preference item — if exercised at or near strike price when FMV ≈ strike, the AMT preference is near zero

**Best practice**: Early exercise of ISOs + 83(b) election within 30 days = minimum AMT, earliest capital gains holding period clock, maximum tax efficiency for startup equity.

## Practical Checklist for 83(b) Elections

- [ ] Date of grant or early exercise noted immediately
- [ ] 30-day deadline calculated and calendared
- [ ] Election letter prepared with all required information
- [ ] Letter mailed (certified mail, return receipt) within 30 days
- [ ] Copy of election filed with current year tax return
- [ ] Copy provided to company
- [ ] Copy retained in personal records indefinitely
- [ ] Cost basis documented: FMV at election date × number of shares
