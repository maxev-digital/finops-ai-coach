# Pre-Retirement Planning: The 5-10 Year Window Before Retirement

## The Pre-Retirement Window: Ages 55-65

The 5-10 years before retirement represent the most consequential planning period in a financial life. Decisions made in this window — Roth conversion strategy, Social Security timing, healthcare coverage, asset allocation, debt elimination — will determine retirement income for potentially 30+ years. The window also allows time to course-correct if savings are insufficient.

## Roth Conversion Window Analysis

Pre-retirement is often the optimal Roth conversion opportunity because:
- Earned income may be at or near its peak (last high-income years)
- After retirement, income drops sharply — but RMDs can push income back up at 73+
- The gap between retirement and RMD start age (73 or 75) is a window of relatively low income where conversions can be done at favorable rates

**The modeling process**:

1. Project Traditional IRA/401(k) balance at age 73 using expected growth rate
2. Calculate estimated RMD at 73 using the Uniform Lifetime Table (balance ÷ life expectancy factor)
3. Add RMDs to projected Social Security income
4. Model the resulting tax bracket
5. If the combined income pushes into the 32-37% bracket, converting now at 22-24% is a significant arbitrage

**Example**: A couple with $2M in Traditional IRA at 62, expecting 6% growth to 73 = approximately $4.0M at 73. Year-1 RMD ≈ $4,000,000 / 26.5 = $150,943. Plus $50,000 in combined Social Security. Provisional income suggests 85% of SS is taxable. Total taxable income: ~$200,000. Marginal bracket: 24%.

If they convert $100,000/year from age 62-72 (10 years): reduce future IRA balance by approximately $1.3M (present value of conversions), reducing future RMDs significantly. Converting at 22-24% now avoids distribution at 24%+ later plus reduces IRMAA exposure.

## Social Security Timing Decision

This is one of the most important — and most misunderstood — retirement decisions.

**The breakeven analysis**: Claiming Social Security early (age 62) vs. waiting until full retirement age (67 for those born 1960+) vs. delaying to 70.

For each year past FRA that you delay: benefit increases by 8%/year. From age 62 to 70: total benefit increase of 77%. This is a risk-free, inflation-indexed, 8%/year guaranteed return for deferral.

**For a healthy couple**: The almost universal financial planning recommendation is for the higher earner to delay to 70. Reasons:
- Survivor benefit: if the higher earner dies first, the surviving spouse receives the higher earner's benefit (or their own benefit, whichever is higher). Maximizing the higher earner's benefit maximizes the survivor's guaranteed income.
- The 8% delay credit exceeds the risk-free rate — it is hard to generate equivalent guaranteed income elsewhere
- Delayed Social Security + portfolio withdrawals in the bridge period = better long-term outcome in most scenarios

**The lower earner**: May claim earlier (62-67) to provide household income during the gap between retirement and the higher earner's age 70 claim.

**Poor health exception**: If serious health issues reduce life expectancy below 78-80 years, the breakeven for delayed claiming shifts — early claiming may provide more total dollars over a shorter life. The breakeven for delaying from 62 to 70 is approximately age 80-82.

## Healthcare Coverage Bridge: Medicare Starts at 65

If you retire before 65, you must have health insurance coverage until Medicare begins. This is one of the largest planning gaps for early retirees.

**Options (age 62-65)**:

1. **Employer COBRA**: Continue employer health coverage for up to 18 months at full cost ($1,600-$2,200/month for family). Expensive but maintains existing coverage and provider relationships.

2. **ACA Marketplace**: With income management (minimizing Traditional IRA distributions, using Roth funds instead), you may qualify for substantial ACA subsidies that make marketplace coverage affordable. Model your income carefully — the premium tax credit can save $10,000-$20,000/year for income-managed early retirees.

3. **Spouse's employer coverage**: If one spouse continues working, the retiring spouse can often be covered on the working spouse's employer plan.

4. **Part-time work for benefits**: Working reduced hours for an employer that provides health benefits bridges the gap at lower earned income.

**The ACA subsidy opportunity**: A couple retiring at 62 with $2M in Traditional IRA and $500K Roth can choose minimal Traditional IRA withdrawals (using Roth for living expenses), keeping MAGI well below thresholds for generous subsidies. This is one of the most powerful planning opportunities in pre-retirement.

## Sequence of Returns Risk Preparation

The sequence of investment returns in the first 5-7 years of retirement is the most dangerous period for a retirement portfolio. A severe bear market early in retirement, combined with portfolio withdrawals, can permanently impair the portfolio — even if long-term returns are eventually fine.

**Pre-retirement preparation**:

**Cash buffer**: Build 1-2 years of living expenses in cash or short-term bonds by retirement date. This allows you to live from cash during a market downturn rather than selling equities at depressed prices.

**Bond tent strategy**: Some planners recommend increasing the bond allocation approaching retirement (more conservative) and then gradually increasing equity allocation in retirement as the sequence-of-returns risk period passes. The "bond tent" peaks at retirement and slowly deflates as the portfolio survives the early years.

**Flexible withdrawal planning**: Ability to reduce discretionary spending during down markets reduces forced selling. Pre-identify which expenses can be cut (travel, dining, entertainment) vs. which are fixed (housing, healthcare, food).

**Income floor planning**: 
1. Identify all guaranteed income: Social Security (delayed), pension, immediate annuity
2. Compare guaranteed income to essential living expenses
3. The gap between essential expenses and guaranteed income is the amount the portfolio must reliably provide
4. Consider an income annuity to fill part of that gap and reduce sequence risk exposure

## Debt Elimination Goal

The retirement goal for most people is: retire debt-free or with only a low-rate mortgage.

**High-priority pre-retirement payoffs**:
- Credit card debt (eliminate immediately)
- Auto loans (pay off before retirement)
- Personal loans (pay off before retirement)
- Student loans for your own education (pay off before retirement — carrying student debt into retirement on a fixed income is extremely difficult)
- Second mortgage / HELOC (eliminate)

**The mortgage question**: Some retirees prefer to retire with a paid-off home for psychological security; others prefer to carry a low-rate mortgage and invest the payoff amount. The financial analysis depends on current mortgage rate vs. expected portfolio returns. Psychologically, many retirees value the certainty of no housing payment.

## Catch-Up Contribution Acceleration

The years immediately before retirement are the final opportunity for maximum catch-up contributions:

**Ages 50-59 (2025)**:
- 401(k): $23,500 + $7,500 catch-up = $31,000
- IRA/Roth IRA: $7,000 + $1,000 catch-up = $8,000
- HSA (if HDHP eligible): $4,300 + $1,000 catch-up = $5,300 (self-only)

**Ages 60-63 (SECURE 2.0 enhanced catch-up)**:
- 401(k): $23,500 + $11,250 enhanced catch-up = $34,750
- IRA/Roth IRA: $8,000 (same as age 50+ catch-up)

For a 60-year-old in the highest earnings years, maximizing the enhanced 401(k) catch-up + IRA + HSA can contribute approximately $45,000-$50,000/year tax-advantaged — the final push to maximize retirement assets.

## Estate Plan Review and Update

Pre-retirement is a natural trigger to review estate documents:

**Common stale documents**:
- Wills written 10-15+ years ago that name guardians for children now adults, or don't reflect blended family situations
- Beneficiary designations that list parents who have died, or former spouses
- Powers of attorney naming agents who have predeceased you or moved away
- Trusts that don't reflect current estate tax law or family circumstances

**Key estate planning updates to complete before retirement**:
- Confirm beneficiary designations are current on all retirement accounts and life insurance
- Update will to reflect current family and asset situation
- Ensure durable financial and healthcare powers of attorney name trusted, available agents
- For estates above $7M (single) or $14M (couple): evaluate estate tax planning before the potential exemption sunset

## Long-Term Care Insurance: Last Window

The window for purchasing long-term care insurance or hybrid LTC products closes in the mid-60s:

- **Ages 55-59**: Optimal — healthy enough to qualify easily; premiums still manageable
- **Ages 60-64**: Good window; some health conditions beginning to affect eligibility
- **Age 65+**: Significantly harder to qualify; premiums are much higher; many people are declined

For clients in their late 50s or early 60s who haven't addressed LTC planning, this is an urgent action item. A health crisis at 65 can make LTC insurance permanently unavailable.

**If already past the insurance window**: Model self-funding requirements ($1-2M in liquid assets earmarked) and consider Medicaid planning strategies if assets are insufficient for self-funding.
