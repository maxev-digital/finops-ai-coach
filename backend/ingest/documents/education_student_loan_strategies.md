# Student Loan Strategies: Federal Programs, Repayment, and Forgiveness

## Federal vs. Private Loans: Always Exhaust Federal First

Federal student loans provide protections and flexibility unavailable with private loans:
- **Income-driven repayment**: Payments capped as a percentage of income
- **Loan forgiveness programs**: PSLF, IDR forgiveness
- **Deferment and forbearance**: Temporary payment pause for hardship or return to school
- **Death and disability discharge**: Federal loans are discharged if borrower dies or becomes totally and permanently disabled
- **No credit requirement**: Federal loans do not require a credit check (except Parent PLUS)

Private loans:
- Typically require creditworthy borrower or co-signer
- Interest rates depend on credit history
- No income-driven repayment
- No forgiveness programs
- Limited forbearance options
- Rarely dischargeable in bankruptcy (same as federal)

**The order of operations**: Exhaust annual federal student loan eligibility before considering private loans. If additional borrowing is needed after federal limits, compare private loan rates vs. Parent PLUS loan rates carefully.

## Income-Driven Repayment Plans

Income-driven repayment (IDR) plans cap monthly payments based on a percentage of discretionary income:

**SAVE (Saving on a Valuable Education)**: Replaced REPAYE as of 2023; as of 2025, SAVE is subject to active litigation that may alter its availability and terms. Verify current status before advising on SAVE specifics. Under the original SAVE design: payments are 5% of discretionary income for undergraduate loans (below $0 for very low incomes), 10% for graduate loans; forgiveness after 20-25 years; interest subsidy prevents balance growth when payment doesn't cover monthly interest.

**IBR (Income-Based Repayment)**: 10% (or 15% for older loans) of discretionary income; forgiveness after 20-25 years; available for most federal Direct loans. Continues to operate regardless of SAVE litigation status.

**PAYE (Pay As You Earn)**: 10% of discretionary income; forgiveness after 20 years; available for borrowers who were new borrowers as of October 1, 2007 and received disbursement after October 1, 2011.

**ICR (Income-Contingent Repayment)**: Available for Parent PLUS loans after consolidation into a Direct Consolidation Loan. Less favorable terms than IBR/PAYE; 20% of discretionary income or a 12-year fixed payment, whichever is less; forgiveness after 25 years.

**Discretionary income definition**: Generally, income above 100-150% of the federal poverty level for your family size.

**Enrollment requirement**: You must apply and be enrolled in an IDR plan — it is not automatic. Recertify annually by providing updated income documentation.

## Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF)

PSLF is the most valuable student loan forgiveness program available. Key features:

**Requirements**:
1. Work full-time (30+ hours/week) for a qualifying employer: federal, state, local, or tribal government; or qualifying 501(c)(3) nonprofit organization
2. Be enrolled in a qualifying income-driven repayment plan (PAYE, IBR, SAVE, ICR)
3. Make 120 qualifying monthly payments (10 years of payments)
4. Have qualifying loan types: Direct Loans only (Federal Family Education Loans and Perkins Loans must be consolidated into a Direct Loan first — note: consolidation resets the 120-payment count)

**After 120 qualifying payments**: Remaining loan balance is forgiven — **TAX-FREE** at the federal level (unlike IDR forgiveness, which is generally taxable).

**Annual Employment Certification**: File the Employment Certification Form (now called the PSLF Form) annually or whenever you change employers. This tracks qualifying payments and verifies employer eligibility. Do not wait 10 years to submit — annual submission is essential for tracking.

**Common PSLF mistakes**:
- Not being on a qualifying IDR plan (standard repayment is NOT IDR; PSLF requires IDR for maximum benefit)
- Not having employer certified (voluntary employer certification required, not automatic)
- Being on the wrong loan type (unconsolidated FFEL loans do not qualify; must consolidate to Direct)
- Overpaying on a side income (if you take on consulting work that appears self-employment income, ensure you still count as full-time for the qualifying employer)

**Strategic maximization**: If you qualify for PSLF, the rational strategy is to minimize payments over the 10 years — choose the IDR plan with the lowest monthly payment, do not overpay, do not refinance to a private loan (you would lose PSLF eligibility). The goal is to maximize forgiveness.

## IDR Forgiveness vs. PSLF: Tax Implications

**PSLF forgiveness**: Tax-free federally. State tax varies.

**IDR forgiveness (20-25 year forgiveness)**: The forgiven amount is currently taxable as ordinary income in the year of forgiveness. This can be a very large taxable event — a borrower with $150,000 forgiven after 25 years could owe $40,000-$55,000 in federal income tax in that single year.

**Planning for IDR forgiveness**: Anticipate the taxable event 20-25 years in advance. Aggressive retirement account contributions in the years approaching forgiveness can reduce taxable income. Some financial planners also consider setting aside sinking fund investments to cover the future tax bill, though this planning is speculative that far in advance.

**The PSLF math**: PSLF is dramatically more valuable than IDR forgiveness because (1) it's tax-free and (2) it occurs after only 10 years vs. 20-25 years — less time for interest to accumulate. For PSLF-eligible borrowers, there is rarely a reason to pursue aggressive payoff.

## Aggressive Payoff vs. IDR Strategy

The decision depends on PSLF eligibility:

**If you qualify for PSLF**:
- Do NOT aggressively pay off loans
- Enroll in the IDR plan with the lowest payment
- Every dollar of extra payment is a dollar not forgiven tax-free
- 10 years of minimum IDR payments followed by tax-free forgiveness > 10 years of aggressive payoff

**If you do NOT qualify for PSLF** (private sector, for-profit, self-employed):
- Compare: (1) avalanche payoff — highest interest rate first; (2) income-driven repayment with 20-25 year forgiveness; (3) refinancing to private at lower rate
- For high-income borrowers with stable employment: refinancing federal loans to private at a lower rate and aggressive payoff is often mathematically superior to IDR (though you lose federal protections)
- For lower-income or uncertain income borrowers: IDR provides payment protection — monthly payment adjusts with income changes; not possible with private loans

## Student Loan Interest Deduction (2025)

**Deduction limit**: Up to $2,500 of student loan interest paid during the year is deductible above-the-line (reduces AGI regardless of whether you itemize).

**Income phase-out (2025)**:
- Single: Phase-out begins at $75,000 MAGI, fully phased out at $90,000
- MFJ: Phase-out begins at $155,000 MAGI, fully phased out at $185,000
- MFS: Not eligible

**What counts**: Interest on any qualified student loan (yours or for your spouse's/dependent's education). The loan must have been used for qualified education expenses.

**Practical impact**: At the $2,500 maximum and a 22% bracket, the deduction saves $550/year in federal income tax. For high earners above the phase-out, the deduction provides no benefit.

## Refinancing Decision Framework

### When Refinancing Makes Sense

Refinancing federal loans to a private loan converts the loan to a private loan permanently, eliminating all federal protections. It should only be considered when:

1. **High-income, stable employment**: You earn significantly more than your loan balance and have no PSLF eligibility
2. **Rate differential is meaningful**: Private loan rate is materially below your current federal loan rate (after accounting for lost federal protections)
3. **No expectation of IDR or forgiveness**: You plan to aggressively pay off the loans; the repayment period is short enough that federal protections have minimal value
4. **No public service career plans**: You are committed to private sector employment

### When NOT to Refinance

- You work or plan to work for a qualifying PSLF employer (forfeit forgiveness entirely)
- Income is variable or uncertain (lose IDR payment protection)
- You want to preserve federal deferment/forbearance safety nets
- You are pursuing IDR forgiveness after 20-25 years
- Your income is low enough that IDR payments are minimal

## Parent PLUS Loans

Parent PLUS loans are taken out by parents in their own name, with less favorable terms than direct student loans:
- Higher interest rate than Direct loans
- No automatic income-driven repayment eligibility
- Must consolidate into Direct Consolidation Loan to access IDR (ICR only — less favorable than IBR/PAYE)
- After consolidation, can pursue PSLF if the parent works for a qualifying employer
- Parent PLUS loans are not dischargeable if the parent dies (the debt follows the parent, not the student; the student can request a discharge if the student for whom the loan was made dies)

**Planning advice**: Be very cautious about Parent PLUS borrowing. Unlike student direct loans with annual limits, there is no practical cap on Parent PLUS — parents can borrow up to the cost of attendance minus other aid. Massive Parent PLUS debt can derail parent retirement savings at the worst possible time.

## Common Student Loan Mistakes

1. **Ignoring federal loan repayment options** after graduation — defaulting to standard 10-year plan without modeling IDR or PSLF eligibility
2. **Refinancing immediately** out of federal loans before understanding PSLF eligibility
3. **Not certifying employment annually** for PSLF — discovering after 10 years that a required step was missed
4. **Consolidating loans** without understanding the payment reset implications for PSLF
5. **Paying aggressively on federal loans when PSLF-eligible** — every extra dollar paid is a dollar not forgiven tax-free
6. **Making IBR payments that don't cover interest** without understanding the interest accrual dynamics under different plans
