# SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, Solo 401(k), and Self-Employed Retirement Plans

## Overview

Self-employed individuals and small business owners have four primary retirement plan options: SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, Solo 401(k), and Defined Benefit/Cash Balance plans. Each has different contribution limits, administrative requirements, and suitability profiles. Choosing the right plan depends on income level, business structure, presence of employees, desire for Roth options, and need for maximum tax deferral.

---

## SEP-IRA (Simplified Employee Pension)

### Contribution Limits (2025)

The SEP-IRA contribution limit is the **lesser of 25% of compensation or $70,000**. For self-employed individuals, "compensation" is net self-employment income after the deduction for half of self-employment tax and the SEP contribution itself — effectively approximately 20% of net profit.

**No catch-up contributions.** The SEP-IRA has no age-50+ catch-up provision. The $70,000 ceiling is the absolute maximum regardless of age.

### Setup and Funding Flexibility

SEP-IRA is the easiest plan to establish. A SEP can be opened and funded as late as the **tax return due date including extensions** — October 15 for sole proprietors filing on extension. This makes SEP-IRA the default choice for someone who realizes at tax time that they need a retirement contribution for the prior year.

The plan requires only IRS Form 5305-SEP (or equivalent) — no annual Form 5500 filing required. Any financial institution can open a SEP-IRA.

### Employee Coverage Requirement — The Key Disadvantage

If the employer has eligible employees, **the same percentage of compensation contributed to the employer-owner's SEP must be contributed to every eligible employee's SEP-IRA**. Eligible employees are those who:
- Are at least age 21
- Have worked for the employer in at least 3 of the last 5 years
- Have earned at least $750 in compensation (2025) from the employer in the current year

**Example:** Owner earns $200,000 and contributes 20% ($40,000) to their SEP. The business has 4 employees each earning $40,000. The employer must also contribute 20% ($8,000) to each employee's SEP = $32,000 in additional required contributions.

This makes SEP-IRA significantly less attractive once a business has employees. For sole proprietors and single-member entities with no employees, this limitation is irrelevant.

### SEP vs. Other Plans for One-Person Businesses

At lower income levels (under ~$100,000 net profit), the Solo 401(k) typically allows higher contributions than SEP because of the employee deferral component. At higher income levels, both approach the $70,000 ceiling. For pure simplicity, SEP remains a top choice.

## SIMPLE IRA (Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees)

### Contribution Limits (2025)

- **Employee elective deferrals:** $16,500
- **Catch-up contribution (age 50+):** $3,500 (total $20,000)
- **Enhanced catch-up (age 60–63) per SECURE 2.0:** $5,250 (total $21,750) — this is 150% of the standard $3,500 catch-up

### Employer Contribution Requirements

SIMPLE IRA requires the employer to make one of two mandatory contributions:

1. **Matching contribution:** Match employee deferrals dollar-for-dollar up to 3% of compensation. Employer may reduce the match to as low as 1% in up to 2 of every 5 years.
2. **Non-elective contribution:** Contribute 2% of each eligible employee's compensation regardless of whether the employee contributes. Compensation capped at $350,000 for this calculation.

The employer cannot choose to make no contribution — one of these two options is mandatory. This makes SIMPLE IRA less flexible than SEP for employers who want discretionary contributions.

### The 2-Year Rule

This is the most important SIMPLE IRA restriction: **funds cannot be rolled over or transferred out of a SIMPLE IRA within the first 2 years of participation**. An early distribution within the 2-year period is subject to a **25% penalty** (instead of the standard 10%). After the 2-year period, SIMPLE IRA assets can be rolled to a Traditional IRA or another SIMPLE IRA.

**Planning implication:** If a self-employed person establishes a SIMPLE IRA and later wants to open a Solo 401(k) or roll assets to another plan, they must wait 2 years before doing so without the 25% penalty. Advisors should consider this before recommending SIMPLE.

### Administrative Requirements

SIMPLE IRA has lower administrative burden than a 401(k) plan but more than SEP-IRA. Required annual notice to employees, but no Form 5500 filing. Must be established by **October 1** of the year in which contributions are to begin (unlike Solo 401(k) deadline of December 31 and SEP which can be opened at tax time).

### Who SIMPLE Works Best For

SIMPLE IRA is suited for small businesses (1–100 employees) that want a retirement plan with modest administrative burden, mandatory employer contributions, and do not have a Solo 401(k) option. For businesses with employees too small to justify a full 401(k) plan. Less optimal for high-income self-employed with no employees.

## Solo 401(k) (Individual 401k / Self-Employed 401k)

### Contribution Limits (2025)

Solo 401(k) allows contributions in two capacities:

**As Employee:**
- Standard elective deferral: **$23,500**
- Age 50+ catch-up: **$7,500** (total $31,000)
- Enhanced catch-up age 60–63: **$11,250** (total $34,750)

**As Employer (profit-sharing):**
- Up to **25% of W-2 compensation** (S-corp owners) or approximately 20% of net self-employment income (sole proprietors/partnerships)
- Cannot exceed the difference between total additions limit and employee deferrals

**Total additions limit (employee + employer):** $70,000 in 2025, or $81,250 including the maximum catch-up contribution (age 60–63 enhanced catch-up).

**Example (self-employed, $150,000 net profit):**
- Employee deferral: $23,500
- Employer profit-sharing: ~$28,000 (20% of ~$140,000 net SE income after deductions)
- Total: ~$51,500

At high income levels ($200,000+ net profit), the total approaches and then hits the $70,000 ceiling.

### Roth Option

Unlike SEP-IRA, the Solo 401(k) can include a **Roth option** for the employee deferral portion. Employer profit-sharing contributions must be pre-tax (though in-plan Roth conversion may be available if the plan document permits). No income limits apply to Roth 401(k) contributions.

### Loan Provision

Solo 401(k) plans can include a loan provision. The plan must explicitly allow loans in the plan document. Maximum loan: 50% of vested balance or $50,000, whichever is less. Repayment over 5 years (longer for primary residence).

### Establishment Deadline — December 31

The Solo 401(k) **plan document must be established by December 31** of the year for which contributions are intended. This is a hard deadline — unlike SEP-IRA, you cannot open a Solo 401(k) retroactively after year-end. Employer profit-sharing contributions can be made up to the tax return due date with extensions (April 15, or October 15 with extension), but the plan must exist before January 1.

### Employee Restriction

The Solo 401(k) is available only when the business owner **has no full-time employees other than themselves and their spouse**. Full-time is defined as working 1,000+ hours per year. Adding a non-spouse full-time employee immediately disqualifies the Solo 401(k) structure — the plan must be converted or terminated.

Part-time employees working fewer than 500 hours can generally be excluded. SECURE 2.0 created long-term part-time employee rules that reduce the exclusion threshold to 2 consecutive years at 500+ hours starting in 2025, which advisors should monitor.

### Form 5500-EZ Filing

When Solo 401(k) plan assets exceed **$250,000**, the owner must file **Form 5500-EZ** annually with the IRS. Below this threshold, no annual filing is required.

## Defined Benefit / Cash Balance Plans

### When to Consider

Defined Benefit (DB) and Cash Balance plans are appropriate for **high-income self-employed individuals over age 50** who want to shelter far more than the $70,000 annual additions limit. These plans are actuarially determined — the annual contribution is calculated to fund a specified benefit at retirement.

### Contribution Potential

Depending on age and income, DB/Cash Balance plans can allow annual contributions of **$150,000–$280,000 or more**. The IRS annual benefit limit for defined benefit plans is the lesser of 100% of the participant's average compensation for the high 3 years or **$280,000 in 2025**.

A 55-year-old business owner with 10 years to retirement who wants to fund the maximum benefit may contribute $150,000–$200,000 per year depending on actuarial assumptions. This creates enormous tax deferral for those in the 35–37% bracket.

### Combination Strategies

A DB/Cash Balance plan is often **combined with a Solo 401(k)**:
- Solo 401(k) captures the employee elective deferral ($23,500+)
- DB/Cash Balance plan provides the large employer contribution
- Combined tax deferral can reach $200,000+/year

### Administrative Requirements and Obligations

DB plans require an **enrolled actuary** to calculate annual contribution requirements and certify the plan. Annual Form 5500 filing required. Minimum required contributions must be made regardless of business income in a given year — this inflexibility is the primary risk.

DB plans work best when income is predictable and consistently high. If income drops significantly, the required contribution may still need to be made, creating cash flow strain. Plan termination before retirement age can trigger excise taxes if overfunded.

### Cash Balance Plan Distinction

A Cash Balance plan is a hybrid DB plan where each participant has a "hypothetical account" earning a stated interest credit (e.g., 5%). This makes it more understandable to participants than a traditional DB plan and more portable — participants can receive their account balance as a lump sum at separation. Increasingly popular for small medical practices, law firms, and high-earning consulting firms.
