# Asset Allocation Models and Portfolio Construction

## Risk/Return Tradeoff Fundamentals

Every investment decision involves a tradeoff between expected return and the risk (volatility) required to achieve it. Higher expected returns have historically required accepting greater short-term fluctuations in portfolio value. A portfolio of 100% equities has produced higher long-run returns than a portfolio of 100% bonds, but also experienced far deeper drawdowns — the S&P 500 fell more than 50% in 2000-2002 and again in 2007-2009.

The practical implication: an investor who cannot tolerate a 40% temporary decline in portfolio value should not hold a 100% equity allocation, even if that allocation would produce the best mathematical outcome over 30 years. Behavior is the limiting factor. The best allocation is the one the investor can actually maintain through a severe bear market.

## Modern Portfolio Theory: The Intuition

Harry Markowitz's 1952 work established a foundational insight: diversification across assets that do not move in perfect lockstep reduces portfolio volatility without proportionally reducing expected returns. Two assets with partial or negative correlation, when combined, produce a portfolio whose volatility is less than the weighted average of the individual asset volatilities.

The efficient frontier represents the set of portfolios offering the maximum expected return for each level of risk, or equivalently, the minimum risk for each level of expected return. Portfolios below the frontier are inefficient — you could get more return for the same risk, or less risk for the same return, by adjusting the mix.

The practical implication for financial planning: adding bonds to an equity portfolio does not simply average down returns. Because bonds and stocks are imperfectly correlated (and sometimes negatively correlated during equity crises), adding bonds up to a point improves the risk-adjusted return profile of the overall portfolio. This is why a 60/40 portfolio is not merely a compromise — it is a structurally different risk/return position.

## Common Allocation Models

**Aggressive: 90/10 or 100% Equity**
- Appropriate for: long time horizons (20+ years), high risk tolerance, young investors early in accumulation
- Expected long-run return: highest among standard models (~9-10% historical nominal)
- Maximum historical drawdown: 50%+
- Composition example: 60% US total market, 30% international, 10% bonds

**Growth: 80/20**
- Appropriate for: 15-20 year horizon, above-average risk tolerance
- Modest volatility reduction vs 90/10; small drag on expected return
- Composition example: 50% US, 25% international, 25% bonds/alternatives

**Balanced: 60/40**
- The classic institutional standard; benchmark for moderate risk investors
- Bond component reduces equity drawdowns — in 2008, 60/40 fell roughly 25% vs 50%+ for 100% equity
- 2022 was an exception: both stocks AND bonds fell simultaneously (correlation risk)
- Composition example: 40% US equity, 20% international, 40% bonds

**Conservative: 40/60**
- Appropriate for: shorter time horizon, near-retirement, lower risk tolerance
- Higher income component; lower growth potential
- Better drawdown protection but higher inflation risk over long periods

**Income: 20/80**
- Primarily bond/fixed income focused
- Appropriate for: retirees who need current income and cannot tolerate equity volatility
- Significant inflation risk over 20+ year retirement horizon
- Often supplements guaranteed income sources (SS, pension)

## Lifecycle and Target-Date Funds

Target-date funds (TDFs) implement a glide path — automatically shifting from aggressive equity allocation toward more conservative bonds as the target retirement year approaches. A 2050 Target Date Fund today holds ~90% equity; as 2050 approaches, it automatically reduces equity and increases fixed income.

TDFs are the default investment option in most 401(k) plans and qualify as Qualified Default Investment Alternatives (QDIAs) under ERISA. For investors who will not actively manage their allocation, a TDF is generally preferable to defaulting to money market or stable value.

Key considerations:
- Each fund company's glide path is different — check the "to" vs "through" design (does it become most conservative at retirement date or continue reducing equity years into retirement?)
- Expense ratios vary significantly: Vanguard TDF ~0.10-0.15%; some institutional funds 0.70%+
- A single TDF provides global diversification in one holding

## Factors Affecting Allocation Decisions

**Age and Time Horizon**: The primary driver. More time allows more risk because short-term volatility has more years to recover before withdrawal.

**Risk Tolerance**: Both objective (financial capacity to absorb loss) and subjective (psychological tolerance for volatility). A retiree with a pension covering all expenses may have high objective risk tolerance but low subjective tolerance — both must be addressed.

**Income Stability**: Stable W-2 employment with a pension functions like a bond in the portfolio. A business owner with volatile income has less human capital "bond-like" stability — the financial portfolio may need more stability to compensate.

**Existing Guaranteed Income**: Social Security, defined benefit pensions, and annuity income reduce the need for the investment portfolio to generate income. An investor with SS and a pension covering all expenses can afford to hold more equity since they are not dependent on the portfolio for current income.

**Psychological Tolerance for Volatility**: Past investor behavior during drawdowns is the best predictor of future behavior. Investors who sold in March 2020 should acknowledge that bias and build a less volatile portfolio they can actually hold.

## The 100-Minus-Age Rule and Why It's Outdated

The old rule of thumb: hold your age as a percentage in bonds (age 40 → 40% bonds; age 60 → 60% bonds). This rule was designed for an era when:
- Retirement lasted 10-15 years (life expectancy was lower)
- Defined benefit pensions provided most retirement income
- Bond yields were high (5-8% in the 1980s and 1990s)

Today, the rule is inadequate:
- A 65-year-old today has a 30-year retirement horizon — a 65% bond allocation may not generate enough growth to sustain 30 years of withdrawals
- With near-zero or low bond yields in the 2010s, the income component failed to compensate for lost equity returns
- Many retirees have no pension, making portfolio growth more critical
- Vanguard, Fidelity, and most financial planning organizations now recommend 110-minus-age or 120-minus-age as a more appropriate baseline, acknowledging longer life expectancies

## The Role of Bonds in a Portfolio

Bonds serve specific functions that are often misunderstood:

**Volatility reduction**: The primary role. Bond prices generally rise when equity prices fall sharply, buffering drawdowns. This holds more reliably in deflationary recessions than in inflationary environments.

**Income generation**: Interest payments provide cash flow — useful in the distribution phase of retirement.

**Rebalancing source**: When equities fall, a bond allocation provides assets to sell and buy equities — systematic "buy low" behavior.

**NOT primarily inflation protection**: Standard nominal bonds lose purchasing power in inflation. Inflation protection requires TIPS, I-Bonds, or real assets — not traditional bonds.

The 2022 experience was unusual: the Fed's most aggressive rate-hiking cycle in 40 years caused both stocks AND bonds to fall simultaneously, undermining the traditional diversification benefit. However, that rate cycle also reset bond yields to historically more attractive levels — by 2025, investment-grade bonds yield 4-5%, restoring their income and diversification value.

## Rebalancing Triggers

Without rebalancing, a portfolio drifts toward its best-performing asset. After a prolonged bull market, this creates unintentional risk increase. Two common rebalancing approaches:

**Calendar rebalancing**: Rebalance on a fixed schedule — annually is the research-supported sweet spot. More frequent rebalancing (monthly/quarterly) in taxable accounts creates excess capital gains for minimal benefit.

**Threshold rebalancing**: Rebalance when any asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from its target (e.g., target 60% equity; rebalance when equity reaches 65% or falls to 55%). This is responsive to market movements rather than time.

**Hybrid approach**: Check quarterly; rebalance only if any allocation has drifted more than 5% from target.

## Correlation Benefits and International Diversification

The benefit of international stocks is not simply additional return exposure — it is the partial correlation benefit. US and international stocks do not move in perfect lockstep, so holding both reduces overall portfolio volatility relative to either alone.

- International developed markets (Europe, Japan, Australia): lower correlation to US than domestic mid-cap
- Emerging markets: higher growth potential, higher volatility, lower correlation to US
- REITs: partial correlation to equity; income component; inflation linkage
- Commodities: negative to low correlation during equity stress; inflation hedge

A globally diversified portfolio using Vanguard Total World ETF (VT) or a combination of VTI + VXUS captures this benefit at minimal cost (~0.07% expense ratio).

## 2025 Context for Fixed Income

After the 2022-2023 Fed rate hike cycle, fixed income entered 2025 with yields substantially more attractive than the preceding decade:
- 10-Year Treasury: ~4.2-4.5%
- Investment-grade corporate bonds: ~5-5.5%
- 5-Year CD/Treasury: ~4-4.5%

This dramatically changes the role of bonds in a portfolio. In 2018, a bond allocation yielded ~2% — barely covering inflation. In 2025, a 4-5% bond yield provides meaningful income and a genuine return without the need to chase equity risk. The 60/40 portfolio's expected returns are more compelling in 2025 than they were for much of the 2010s.
