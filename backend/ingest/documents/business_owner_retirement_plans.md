# Retirement Plans for Business Owners: Maximizing Tax-Advantaged Savings

## The Self-Employed Retirement Plan Opportunity

Business owners have access to retirement plan contribution limits that dwarf employee limits. The maximum annual contribution to a self-employed retirement plan is $70,000 in 2025 (plus catch-up contributions if age 50+) — far exceeding the $23,500 limit for a standard 401(k) employee contribution.

This creates an extraordinary opportunity: a profitable self-employed individual or small business owner can shelter $50,000-$300,000+ annually from income tax, dramatically reducing tax liability while building retirement wealth.

## SEP-IRA: Simple, High-Limit, Employer-Only

### How It Works

The Simplified Employee Pension (SEP-IRA) is funded entirely by employer contributions. The same percentage of compensation must be contributed for all eligible employees, including the owner.

**2025 limits**:
- Maximum: Lesser of $70,000 or 25% of W-2 compensation (employees) / approximately 20% of net self-employment income (sole proprietors, after SE tax deduction adjustment)
- No catch-up contributions available (unlike 401k plans)
- Contribution timing: Can be funded until the tax return due date, including extensions (October 15 for self-employed)

**Sole proprietor example**: Net Schedule C income of $200,000. SE tax deduction ≈ $14,000. Reduced SE income ≈ $186,000. 20% contribution limit ≈ $37,200. Contribution reduces federal taxable income by $37,200.

### SEP-IRA Advantages
- **Simplest plan to establish**: Open at any brokerage (Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab) in minutes; no plan documents
- **Late funding**: Can establish and fund until tax return due date (including extensions); can decide retroactively
- **High limits**: Much higher than Roth IRA or Traditional IRA limits
- **No annual administration**: No Form 5500 required for most SEP-IRAs

### SEP-IRA Disadvantages
- **Employee cost**: Must contribute same percentage to all eligible employees. If you have three employees, a 20% contribution rate means the company pays 20% of each employee's salary into their SEP-IRA — very expensive as business grows.
- **No catch-up contributions**: No additional catch-up for owners age 50+.
- **No employee deferral**: Owner cannot make employee-side deferrals — only employer contributions. This is significant limitation for lower-income years.
- **Lower contributions at lower incomes**: For a sole proprietor earning $80,000 net, the SEP contribution limit is only ~$16,000. A Solo 401(k) would allow much more.

## SIMPLE IRA: Good for Small Teams

### How It Works

The Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees (SIMPLE IRA) allows both employee deferrals and employer contributions. Designed for businesses with 100 or fewer employees.

**2025 contribution limits**:
- Employee deferral: $16,500 ($19,500 if age 50-59 or 64+, with new catch-up rules under SECURE 2.0)
- Employer: Required — either 2% of all eligible employees' compensation OR dollar-for-dollar match up to 3% of compensation

**Key feature**: Employees can defer up to $16,500 from their wages — unlike SEP-IRA which has no employee deferral component.

### SIMPLE IRA Advantages
- Allows employees to save more (with deferrals)
- Mandatory employer contributions ensure employees have retirement savings
- Simple administration compared to 401(k)

### SIMPLE IRA Disadvantages
- Mandatory employer contributions cannot be avoided
- Total contribution limit is lower than Solo 401(k) for high earners
- 2-year waiting period: SIMPLE IRA funds cannot be rolled to other accounts (except another SIMPLE) for the first 2 years; early distributions within 2 years have a 25% penalty

## Solo 401(k): The Best Option for Most Solo Business Owners

### How It Works

The Solo 401(k) (also called Individual 401(k), Self-Employed 401(k), or one-participant 401(k)) is a full 401(k) plan available to self-employed individuals with no full-time employees other than themselves and their spouse.

**2025 contribution limits**:

| Component | Limit | Who contributes |
|-----------|-------|----------------|
| Employee deferral | $23,500 | You (the employee) |
| Age 50-59 catch-up | +$7,500 | You |
| Age 60-63 enhanced catch-up | +$11,250 | You (SECURE 2.0) |
| Employer contribution | Up to 25% of W-2 comp (S-Corp) or ~20% of net SE income (sole prop) | Employer |
| Total maximum (under 50) | $70,000 | Combined |
| Total maximum (age 50-59) | $77,500 | Combined |
| Total maximum (age 60-63) | $81,250 | Combined |

### Why Solo 401(k) Beats SEP for Most Sole Proprietors

The key difference: employee deferral + employer contribution.

**At $60,000 net SE income**:
- SEP-IRA maximum: ~$11,180 (20% × $55,900 after SE tax deduction)
- Solo 401(k): $23,500 employee deferral + ~$11,180 employer = $34,680

The Solo 401(k) allows $23,500 more to be sheltered at the same income level because the employee deferral component is available.

**At $200,000 net SE income**:
- SEP-IRA maximum: ~$37,200
- Solo 401(k): $23,500 employee + $37,200 employer = $60,700 (subject to $70,000 cap)

At higher incomes, the gap narrows because the employer contribution limits become the binding constraint, but the Solo 401(k) still allows somewhat more.

### Roth Solo 401(k) Option

Many Solo 401(k) providers (Fidelity, Vanguard, Schwab, E*Trade) offer a Roth 401(k) option within the Solo 401(k). The employee deferral can be designated as Roth (after-tax, but tax-free growth and withdrawal). This is particularly valuable in lower-income years when the tax bracket is lower.

### Solo 401(k) Administration

- **Establish by December 31**: Unlike SEP-IRA, the Solo 401(k) plan must be established by December 31 of the year contributions are desired (for calendar-year taxpayers)
- **Fund until tax return due date**: Employee deferrals must be contributed by December 31; employer contribution can be funded until tax return due date including extensions
- **Form 5500-EZ**: Required once plan assets exceed $250,000; simple single-page form for solo plans

### The Full-Time Employee Disqualification

The Solo 401(k) is only available when the business has NO full-time employees other than the owner and spouse. Full-time = working 1,000+ hours per year. Part-time employees (under 500 hours/year) can be excluded for plan eligibility purposes (though SECURE 2.0 is expanding long-term part-time employee inclusion requirements — monitor for changes).

Once you hire full-time employees, you must convert to a standard ERISA 401(k) plan — more expensive, requires nondiscrimination testing, and must cover eligible employees.

## Defined Benefit / Cash Balance Plans: For High-Income Late Starters

### Who This Is For

A business owner age 50+ with high, stable income who wants to maximize retirement contributions beyond what a Solo 401(k) allows. Cash balance plans can allow $200,000-$300,000+ in annual deductible contributions.

### How It Works

A defined benefit plan promises a specific retirement benefit (e.g., "annual benefit equal to $150,000 at age 65"). An actuary calculates the annual contribution required to fund that benefit based on age, years until retirement, and assumed investment returns. Older participants can contribute much more (larger contribution needed to fund the same future benefit in fewer years).

**Cash balance plan**: A type of defined benefit plan where each participant has a hypothetical "account balance" that grows with employer contributions and an annual "pay credit" — simpler to explain to participants than traditional defined benefit pensions.

### Annual Contribution Potential

| Age | Approximate maximum annual contribution |
|-----|----------------------------------------|
| 45 | $80,000-$130,000 |
| 50 | $130,000-$200,000 |
| 55 | $180,000-$280,000 |
| 60 | $250,000-$350,000 |
| 65 | $300,000+ |

### Common Setup: DB Plan + Solo 401(k) Combination

High-income solo business owners often combine a defined benefit / cash balance plan with a Solo 401(k):
- Solo 401(k): $70,000 (or with catch-up, $77,500-$81,250) employer + employee contributions
- Defined benefit plan: Additional $100,000-$250,000+ in annual deductions

Combined, this can shelter $170,000-$350,000+/year from taxable income — extraordinary for high-income self-employed individuals.

### DB Plan Considerations

- **Actuarial fees**: Required annually — typically $1,500-$3,000/year
- **Minimum contributions**: Defined benefit plans require minimum annual funding — cannot skip a year if business has a bad year; this creates financial risk
- **Form 5500**: Required annually with actuarial certification
- **Termination complexity**: Winding down a DB plan requires careful actuarial analysis and PBGC notification
- **Best for**: Solo business owners with very stable, high income and strong desire to maximize tax deferral; not appropriate for businesses with volatile income

## ERISA Compliance with Employees

Once a plan covers non-owner employees, full ERISA compliance applies:
- **Nondiscrimination testing**: Actual Deferral Percentage (ADP) test and other tests ensure the plan doesn't disproportionately benefit highly compensated employees
- **Safe harbor 401(k)**: Mandatory employer contribution (3% of all eligible employee compensation, or 4% match) eliminates ADP testing — allows owners and highly compensated employees to maximize deferrals
- **Vesting schedules**: Employee contributions are always 100% vested; employer contributions must vest according to legal schedules (immediate, cliff, or graded)
- **ERISA bond**: Plans with assets must have a fidelity bond (typically $100-$500/year)
- **Plan document**: Formal ERISA plan document required; typically provided by record-keeper/TPA
