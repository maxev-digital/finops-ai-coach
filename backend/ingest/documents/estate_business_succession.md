# Business Succession Planning: Transferring or Selling a Business

## Business Valuation Methods

An accurate business valuation is the foundation of any succession plan. Three primary approaches:

### Income Approach

Values the business based on its ability to generate income.

- **Capitalization of Earnings**: Value = Normalized Annual Earnings / Capitalization Rate. The cap rate reflects the risk of the business (a stable, growing business might use a 15-20% cap rate; a riskier business might use 25-35%). A business earning $500,000/year with a 20% cap rate is valued at $2.5M.

- **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)**: Projects future free cash flows over a multi-year forecast period and discounts them to present value using a risk-adjusted discount rate. More accurate for businesses with non-stable growth, but highly sensitive to assumptions.

### Market Approach

Values the business based on comparable transactions or public company multiples.

- **EBITDA multiples**: The most common metric. Industry-specific. Small service businesses: 3-5× EBITDA; manufacturing: 4-7×; technology/software: 8-15×; healthcare: 6-12×. A business with $1M EBITDA in the professional services sector might be valued at $3M-$5M.

- **Revenue multiples**: Used for businesses with low or negative earnings but strong revenue (software, early-stage companies).

- **Comparable transactions**: Private M&A databases (BizBuySell, Pratt's Stats, DealStats) provide actual transaction multiples for similar businesses.

### Asset Approach

Values the business based on its underlying assets.
- Used for holding companies, real estate businesses, or companies being wound down
- Rarely appropriate for going concerns with significant earnings power above asset value

## Exit Options: Paths to Business Transfer

### Strategic Sale

Sale to a competitor, industry consolidator, or larger company seeking market share, capabilities, or geographic expansion.

- **Valuation**: Typically highest — strategic buyers may pay premium for synergies (eliminating duplicate overhead, cross-selling existing customers)
- **Process**: Usually through an investment banker who runs a structured M&A process with multiple potential buyers
- **Timeline**: 6-18 months from engagement to close
- **Tax**: Depends on whether structured as asset sale or stock sale (see below)
- **Consideration**: Business owner loses control immediately; culture and employees may change under new ownership

### ESOP (Employee Stock Ownership Plan)

The company creates an ESOP trust that purchases shares from the selling owner, financed by a bank loan. Employees gradually acquire ownership through their retirement accounts.

**Tax advantages for seller**:
- **Section 1042 rollover**: If the company is a C-Corporation, selling shareholders can defer (potentially permanently) capital gains by reinvesting the proceeds in qualified replacement property (QRP) — diversified stocks and bonds in US companies within 12 months of the sale
- The 1042 election requires the ESOP to own at least 30% of company stock after the sale
- QRP held until death: stepped-up basis eliminates the deferred gain permanently

**Tax advantages for the company**:
- S-Corporation owned by an ESOP pays no federal income tax on the ESOP-owned percentage of earnings
- 100% ESOP-owned S-Corp is essentially federally income-tax-free
- Contributions to the ESOP trust are tax-deductible by the company (used to repay the ESOP loan)

**Employee benefits**: ESOP creates an employee ownership culture; shares vest in employee accounts as retirement savings; strong retention tool.

**Limitations**: Complex and expensive to set up ($100,000-$250,000 in legal and advisory fees); ESOP requires independent annual appraisal; fiduciary obligations; best for businesses with $2M+ EBITDA.

### Private Equity Recapitalization

Sell majority control (typically 60-80%) to a private equity firm while retaining a meaningful equity stake (20-40%).

- **Structure**: Owner receives cash for the majority sale (liquidity event); retains minority stake for the "second bite" at the apple when PE exits in 3-7 years
- **Valuation**: Usually at a multiple of EBITDA with a potential earnout for future performance
- **Growth capital**: PE firm brings capital, operational expertise, and M&A capabilities for growth
- **Risk**: Owner loses majority control; must work within PE firm's governance and reporting requirements; second bite depends on PE successfully growing and selling the business

### Management Buyout (MBO)

The existing management team acquires the business from the owner.

- **Financing**: Often combination of management equity, bank debt, and seller financing (installment note from seller)
- **Advantage**: Continuity — management knows the business; loyal to employees and culture; less disruption than outside buyer
- **Challenge**: Management team often lacks sufficient capital for a full purchase; seller must accept installment payments over time (credit risk); valuation typically lower than strategic buyer

### Family Succession

Transfer of the business to children or other family members.

- **Gift or sale**: Can be structured as a gift (using lifetime exemption), installment sale, or a combination
- **FLP/LLC structure**: Business transferred to FLP with valuation discounts; interests gifted over time
- **Key risk**: Family dynamics; sibling conflicts over management; non-business family members who expect equal shares but are not involved in operations
- **Governance**: Family constitution, buy-sell agreement among family members, outside advisory board — critical for long-term success

## Tax Implications of Business Sale

### Asset Sale vs. Stock Sale

**The fundamental tension**: Sellers prefer stock sales (single level of capital gains tax); buyers prefer asset sales (full depreciation basis reset on acquired assets; no assumption of unknown liabilities).

**Asset sale tax treatment**:
- Inventory: ordinary income
- Receivables: ordinary income
- Equipment/fixed assets: §1245 recapture at ordinary income rates (up to the amount of depreciation taken), then capital gains above original cost
- Real estate: §1250 recapture (unrecaptured §1250 gain taxed at max 25%), then capital gains
- Goodwill and going-concern value: long-term capital gains at 0/15/20%
- Covenant not to compete: ordinary income (it's consideration for a service restriction)

**Stock sale tax treatment**:
- All proceeds measured against stock basis
- Long-term capital gains if held more than 1 year
- Single level of tax — no component-by-component analysis

**Practical impact**: For a $5M deal, the tax difference between asset sale and stock sale can be $200,000-$600,000 in additional tax for the seller in an asset sale vs. stock sale. Sellers typically demand a price premium for accepting an asset sale structure.

### Section 1202 QSBS Exclusion

For Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS), IRC §1202 provides an extraordinary benefit:

**Requirements**:
- Issued by a domestic C-Corporation
- Acquired by the shareholder at original issuance (not secondary market purchase)
- Held by the shareholder for more than 5 years
- The corporation had gross assets of $50M or less at the time of issuance (and immediately after)
- The corporation must be in a qualifying trade or business (excludes professional services, hospitality, finance, real estate)

**Benefit**: Exclude 100% of capital gain on sale (for stock acquired after September 27, 2010), up to the greater of $10M or 10× the adjusted basis.

A founder who invested $500,000 in a qualifying C-Corp that grows to $10M can potentially exclude the entire $9.5M gain — saving approximately $1.9M-$2.4M in federal capital gains tax.

**State conformity**: Not all states conform to §1202 (California notably does not). The exclusion is federal only where the state does not conform.

### Installment Sale

The seller receives payments over time rather than all at once.

- **Tax benefit**: Gain is recognized proportionally as payments are received — spreads the tax over multiple years, preventing a single-year spike into the highest brackets
- **IRS Form 6252**: Reports installment sale income each year
- **Interest component**: The interest portion of installment payments is ordinary income
- **Risk**: Buyer default — seller has repossession rights but the business may be degraded; collection risk is real
- **Interaction with QSBS**: Installment sale treatment is available for QSBS (installment method defers recognition to years of actual payment, but the gain remains potentially excludable)

## Preparation Timeline: 3-5 Years Before Exit

Businesses that are properly prepared for sale achieve higher valuations and more successful transitions:

**3-5 years before exit**:
- Clean up financial statements (transition from tax-minimizing accounting to GAAP)
- Reduce owner-dependency: document processes, cross-train employees, develop management bench
- Renew key customer contracts and supplier agreements
- Resolve any pending litigation, environmental, or regulatory issues
- Begin formal business valuation engagement

**1-2 years before exit**:
- Formal business valuation by credentialed appraiser
- Identify and vet potential buyers (strategic, PE, MBO)
- Execute retention agreements for key employees
- Structure ownership to optimize tax treatment of sale proceeds (entity type, holding periods, QSBS qualification)
- Engage M&A attorney and investment banker if pursuing strategic sale

## Key Person and Buy-Sell Coordination

Every business succession plan should include:
- An updated buy-sell agreement with current valuation methodology and adequate funding
- Key person life and disability insurance to protect the business during the transition period
- Employment and non-compete agreements with key employees
- Governance documents (operating agreement, shareholder agreement) that address succession scenarios

See the buy-sell agreement document for detailed funding and structure guidance.
