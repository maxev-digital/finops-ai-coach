# Gifting Strategies: Transferring Wealth During Your Lifetime

## Annual Gift Tax Exclusion

The annual gift tax exclusion allows transfers of up to $19,000 per recipient per year in 2025 without any gift tax consequence and without using any of the lifetime exemption.

Key features:
- **Per recipient, not per donor**: You can give $19,000 to an unlimited number of people — children, grandchildren, nieces, nephews, friends, anyone
- **No gift tax return required**: Gifts within the annual exclusion are not reported on Form 709 unless combined with taxable gifts in the same year
- **Present interest requirement**: The gift must be a completed gift of a "present interest" — the recipient must have immediate use and enjoyment of the gift. Future interest gifts (like most contributions to irrevocable trusts) do not qualify without special provisions (Crummey powers)
- **Gift splitting**: Married couples can split gifts on a gift tax return, allowing each spouse's annual exclusion to apply. In 2025, a married couple can give $38,000 per recipient per year ($19,000 × 2) by filing a gift splitting election

For a family with three adult children and six grandchildren, a married couple can transfer $38,000 × 9 = $342,000 per year with no gift tax return and no lifetime exemption usage.

## 529 Plan Superfunding (5-Year Gift Tax Election)

A special rule allows front-loading contributions to a 529 education savings account by treating them as if spread over 5 years for gift tax purposes.

**2025 superfunding limits**:
- Single: $95,000 per beneficiary (5 × $19,000)
- Married filing jointly: $190,000 per beneficiary (5 × $38,000)

**Rules**:
- An election must be made on Form 709 (gift tax return) in the year of the contribution
- No additional annual exclusion gifts can be made to the same beneficiary during the 5-year period without gift tax consequences
- If the donor dies during the 5-year period, the remaining pro-rated balance returns to the estate

**Planning use case**: Grandparents with substantial assets who want to fund grandchildren's education can transfer $190,000 per grandchild in a single transaction with no gift tax and no lifetime exemption usage. For four grandchildren, that's $760,000 transferred in one year.

## Direct Educational Tuition Payments

Payments made directly to qualifying educational institutions for tuition are completely excluded from gift tax — not subject to the annual exclusion limit, and not counted against the lifetime exemption.

**Requirements**:
- Payment must be made directly to the educational institution (not to the student or parent)
- Covers tuition only — room, board, books, supplies, and other fees do NOT qualify
- Institution must be an eligible educational organization (college, university, vocational school, private K-12 school — any institution that normally maintains a faculty and curriculum and has enrolled students)

**No dollar limit**: There is no cap on the amount. Paying $80,000/year in tuition directly to a private university is fully excluded, not subject to any limit.

**Timing**: The exclusion applies to payments made in the same calendar year. Paying ahead for future semesters may not qualify if the institution does not currently have the student enrolled.

## Direct Medical Expense Payments

Direct payments to medical care providers are excluded from gift tax with no dollar limit.

**Requirements**:
- Must be paid directly to the medical provider (not reimbursed to the patient)
- Must be for medical care as defined by IRC §213(d): diagnosis, treatment, prevention of disease; equipment, supplies, diagnostic devices; transportation to medical care; health insurance premiums; long-term care insurance premiums

**Practical uses**:
- Pay medical bills directly for elderly parents or other family members
- Pay long-term care facility costs directly
- Pay health insurance premiums for uninsured adult children (paid directly to the insurance company)

**Combination strategy**: Using direct tuition payments + direct medical payments + annual exclusion gifts allows substantial wealth transfer from older generations without any gift tax or lifetime exemption usage.

## Intrafamily Loans

Lending money to family members at the IRS Applicable Federal Rate (AFR) — typically far below commercial lending rates — allows the family member to benefit from a below-market loan while the lender (estate) technically earns the AFR interest.

**Requirements**:
- Loan must charge interest at least equal to the AFR (published monthly by IRS; short-term, mid-term, long-term rates)
- Loan must be properly documented with a promissory note
- Interest must actually be paid and reported as income by the lender
- A bona fide creditor-debtor relationship must exist

**Gift tax treatment**: If interest is charged below the AFR, the IRS treats the foregone interest as a gift — imputed gift of the below-market interest amount.

**Estate planning use**: A parent lends a child $1,000,000 at the AFR (e.g., 4.5% mid-term rate). If the child invests the funds and earns 8%, the child keeps the 3.5% spread. The parent/estate earned only 4.5% — all future appreciation on the borrowed funds remains outside the parent's estate. If structured as a demand loan at minimum AFR, the loan is simple; term loans are slightly more complex.

**Self-Canceling Installment Note (SCIN)**: A note that automatically cancels at the seller's death. Used in business sales. The seller receives interest during life; at death, the remaining balance is cancelled (not in estate). Requires actuarial pricing to avoid gift tax implications.

## Family Limited Partnership Gifts

A Family Limited Partnership (FLP) or Family LLC is a business entity in which family members hold interests. Gifts of limited partnership interests can receive valuation discounts:

**Lack of control discount**: Limited partners cannot force distributions, liquidate the partnership, or make management decisions. This reduced control is worth less than a pro-rata share of the underlying assets — discounts of 15-35% are commonly supported.

**Lack of marketability discount**: FLP interests have no ready market (unlike publicly traded stocks). The illiquidity reduces fair market value — additional discount of 10-25%.

**Combined discount**: An FLP interest representing $200,000 of underlying asset value may be valued at $130,000-$150,000 for gift tax purposes — allowing more assets to be transferred using the same amount of annual exclusion or lifetime exemption.

**IRS scrutiny**: The IRS closely examines FLPs. Legitimate non-tax business purposes are required (investment management, family business succession, asset protection). FLPs must be operated as real business entities — distributions must follow economic arrangements, not just when taxes call for it. FLPs created solely to achieve valuation discounts without economic substance have been challenged and collapsed by IRS.

## Gifts During Life vs. Inheritance at Death: Basis Implications

This is a critical planning distinction that many families overlook:

**Gifts (lifetime transfers)**: The recipient takes the donor's cost basis (carryover basis). If you gift stock purchased for $50,000 that is now worth $500,000, your heir receives it with a $50,000 basis — when they eventually sell, they owe capital gains on $450,000.

**Inheritance (transfer at death)**: The heir receives a stepped-up basis equal to the fair market value on the date of death. If you leave that same stock worth $500,000 at death, your heir's basis is $500,000 — zero capital gains tax if sold immediately. The $450,000 of embedded gain is permanently erased.

**Strategic implication**: For highly appreciated assets below the estate tax threshold, it is generally better to hold and bequeath them at death (to capture the step-up) rather than gifting them during life (which preserves the gain and creates future capital gains tax for the heir). Conversely, gifting assets with little embedded gain (e.g., cash, recently purchased assets) during life is less costly.

**Exception**: Assets expected to appreciate dramatically before death may be worth gifting now despite losing the step-up — the future estate tax savings on the appreciation may outweigh the embedded capital gains tax.

## "Basis Step-Up Laundering" Warning

An IRS-challenged strategy involves gifting highly appreciated assets to elderly parents or grandparents who have low remaining life expectancy (so the assets receive a stepped-up basis at their death), then inheriting those assets back. The IRS applies IRC §1014(e): if appreciated property received as a gift within 1 year of the donor's death is inherited back by the original donor or their spouse, the basis is NOT stepped up — it returns to the original carryover basis. This technique does not work when the donor and heir are the same person or spouses.
