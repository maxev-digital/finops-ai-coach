# Retirement Transition: The Financial and Psychological Guide to Your First Retirement Years

## The Year-of-Retirement Financial Priorities

The year you stop working represents a fundamental shift in financial life. Several irreversible decisions and time-sensitive actions must be executed correctly.

### Final Year of High Earned Income: Last Opportunities

**Maximum retirement contributions**: The year of retirement (if you work for any part of the year) is your last chance to make earned-income-based retirement contributions. If you retire in July:
- Can make full-year 401(k) contributions based on compensation earned through your last pay date
- Can make full-year IRA/Roth IRA contributions based on earned income (you must have earned income equal to the contribution amount)
- Can make HSA contributions for months you were enrolled in an HDHP before Medicare enrollment

**Traditional IRA deduction and Roth IRA eligibility**: Once you stop working, all current-year IRA contributions must be based on earned income from that year. A spouse who worked qualifies their non-working partner for spousal IRA contributions.

**Tax loss harvesting opportunity**: High-income year; any accumulated tax losses can offset up to $3,000 of ordinary income plus capital gains. Review taxable portfolio for loss harvesting candidates.

**Roth conversion timing**: The year of retirement may be a low-income year (if retirement is mid-year) — potentially the first year in a favorable Roth conversion window. Begin modeling the conversion strategy.

## Income Replacement Percentage: What the Research Shows

The common planning benchmark of "80% income replacement in retirement" is an average that masks significant variation:

**Early retirement "Go-Go" years** (ages 60-75): Higher spending. Travel, activities, dining, leisure — spending may approach 90-100% of pre-retirement income for active retirees pursuing deferred activities.

**Mid-retirement "Slow-Go" years** (ages 75-85): Spending declines naturally. Less travel, less activity, lower transportation costs, lower work-related expenses. Spending may drop to 70-80% of pre-retirement income.

**Late retirement "No-Go" years** (ages 85+): Spending rises again briefly due to healthcare and long-term care costs, then declines as health limits activity further.

**Planning implication**: The biggest error is assuming a flat retirement spending profile. Model the spending curve:
- Higher spending in the go-go years (budget for them — don't be conservative and deprive yourself)
- Healthcare costs will rise — budget specifically for them
- The go-go years are irreplaceable; you cannot spend them later

## Social Security Claim Execution

**For those delaying to 70**: File online at ssa.gov approximately 3 months before your target start month. Social Security benefits start the month after the month you file for your first payment month — filing in January means your first payment arrives in February for January benefits.

**Coordinating with portfolio withdrawals**: If the higher earner is delaying to 70, you need an income bridge for the gap between retirement and age 70. Sources:
- Lower earner's earlier Social Security claim
- Portfolio withdrawals (Traditional IRA or Roth, depending on tax situation)
- Part-time work income
- Pension income if applicable

**The first-year decision quirk**: If you file before April 1 of the year following the year you turn 70, your first payment can be retroactively applied to up to 6 months of past benefits. This is sometimes done if someone forgot to file at 70 — you can receive 6 months of lump-sum back payments. Note: accepting retroactive benefits effectively means you claimed at 69.5, not 70.

## Medicare Enrollment Execution

For people retiring at or near 65, Medicare enrollment must be coordinated precisely:

**If retiring and losing employer coverage at 65**: 
1. Enroll in Medicare Part A and Part B (if not already enrolled)
2. Choose between Original Medicare + Medigap/Part D vs. Medicare Advantage
3. The choice of Medigap vs. Advantage is guaranteed issue during the 6-month open enrollment window — do not delay this decision

**If already past 65 with employer coverage**: 
- Special Enrollment Period begins when employer coverage ends (or when active employment ends, whichever comes first)
- 8 months to enroll in Part B without late enrollment penalty
- COBRA does NOT extend the SEP — the clock starts when active employer coverage ends

**If retiring before 65**: See healthcare bridge options in the pre-retirement document.

## Healthcare and Dental Budget: The Underestimated Expense

The Fidelity Research Center (2024) estimates that a married couple retiring at 65 will spend an average of $315,000 on healthcare costs in retirement (in today's dollars). This figure includes:
- Medicare premiums (Parts B, D, and Medigap or Advantage)
- Cost-sharing (deductibles, copays, coinsurance)
- Dental, vision, and hearing costs (not covered by traditional Medicare)
- Long-term care costs are often excluded from this estimate — add them separately

**Dental costs in retirement**: Medicare does not cover routine dental care. Dental procedures in retirement — fillings, crowns, dentures, implants — can cost $5,000-$20,000+ over a lifetime. Budget a separate dental fund or purchase standalone dental insurance.

**HSA for healthcare costs**: The most important use of accumulated HSA funds is paying for Medicare premiums and healthcare costs in retirement — tax-free. HSA funds are ideal for this purpose: Part B premiums ($185/month in 2025), Part D premiums, Medigap premiums, dental, vision, hearing.

## Transitioning Account Access: Which Accounts, When

Understanding which retirement accounts are available without penalty at different ages:

**All ages**: Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) available without penalty anytime. Money you contributed is your own; the 10% penalty and taxes only apply to earnings distributions before age 59½.

**Age 55 (Rule of 55)**: Penalty-free distributions from a 401(k) at your CURRENT employer if you separate from service in the year you turn 55 or older. This applies to the 401(k) at the company you retired from — not IRAs, not old 401(k)s.

**Age 59½**: Penalty-free distributions from Traditional IRA, 401(k), Roth IRA earnings. The 10% early withdrawal penalty ends.

**Age 63**: Enhanced catch-up contribution window ends (for 401(k) enhanced catch-up, this is the age 60-63 window under SECURE 2.0).

**Age 65**: HSA distributions for non-medical purposes taxed as ordinary income (no penalty — effective conversion to Traditional IRA for non-medical use).

**Age 73**: Required minimum distributions begin for Traditional IRA, 401(k), 403(b) (for those born 1951-1959). Age 75 for those born 1960+.

## Adjusting Investment Allocation for Distribution Phase

Retirement is not the finish line — it is the start of the distribution phase, which may last 25-35 years. The portfolio must:
- Continue growing to outpace inflation
- Withstand down markets without requiring heavy selling
- Provide reliable income

**The retirement asset allocation**: Many target-date funds become too conservative at retirement (40-50% bonds). For a 65-year-old with a 25-year+ horizon, 50-60% equity is typically appropriate — enough growth to sustain purchasing power.

**Post-retirement equity maintenance**: If you reduce equity too much at retirement, you risk running out of money in your 80s because of inflation eroding purchasing power. A portfolio earning 4% with 3% inflation grows only 1% real — not enough to sustain withdrawals over 30 years.

**Reducing concentration**: Any remaining employer stock, concentrated individual positions, or risky speculative positions should be systematically reduced as you enter retirement. The sequence-of-returns risk that was theoretical during accumulation becomes concrete.

## The First RMD: Planning the Initial Required Minimum Distribution

**RMD start date**: April 1 of the year following the year you reach age 73 (or 75 for those born 1960+). 

**The first-year choice**:
- Take the first RMD in the year you turn 73: taxable income spread over two calendar years
- Delay the first RMD to April 1 of the following year: two RMDs in one calendar year (the delayed first year's RMD + the second year's RMD), potentially pushing into a higher bracket

For most retirees, taking the first RMD in the year they turn 73 is preferable — it avoids the double-RMD year that can spike income and trigger higher IRMAA, higher Social Security taxation, and higher tax rates.

**Calculating the RMD**: IRA balance on December 31 of the prior year ÷ the Uniform Lifetime Table factor for your age.

## Withdrawal Sequencing in Retirement

**General guidance** (see also: income_withdrawal_sequencing.md for full detail):
1. Required Minimum Distributions (cannot avoid)
2. Taxable brokerage account (capital gains rates apply; step-up basis at death if held)
3. Traditional IRA / 401(k) (ordinary income; delay to allow Roth to grow)
4. Roth IRA (last resort — let it compound longest for heirs or late-life use)

**Roth conversions within retirement**: The window between retirement and RMD start age remains an opportunity for additional Roth conversions — particularly if investment returns push Traditional IRA balance higher than projected, creating a future RMD problem.

## The Psychological Transition: Often Harder Than the Financial

Financial planners frequently observe that the psychological challenges of retirement are underestimated by people in the accumulation phase. Studies (Encore Career, Project Age, Robert Wood Johnson Foundation) document that:

**Loss of professional identity**: For many high-achieving professionals, career is identity. "What do you do?" has one answer for decades. Retirement removes this anchor, creating a crisis of purpose for many retirees — particularly in the first 1-2 years.

**Loss of structure**: The workday provides structure — places to be, problems to solve, colleagues to interact with. Without it, days can feel formless and unmotivating.

**Social isolation risk**: Work provides social contact that is lost in retirement. Without deliberate replacement, isolation is a significant risk — with real health consequences. Loneliness is correlated with cognitive decline, cardiovascular disease, and mortality.

**The transition planning recommendation**: Develop retirement activities, social connections, and structure BEFORE the retirement date — not after. Having a compelling answer to "what am I going to DO in retirement?" reduces the adjustment period significantly. Some individuals benefit from a phased retirement (part-time work for 1-2 years) that provides structure during the transition.

Financial plans address the numbers; great financial planners also help clients think through the non-financial dimensions of retirement that will determine whether their well-funded retirement is actually satisfying.
