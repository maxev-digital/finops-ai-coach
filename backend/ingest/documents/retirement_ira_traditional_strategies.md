# Traditional IRA Strategies

## Contribution Limits (2025)

The annual Traditional IRA contribution limit in 2025 is **$7,000** ($8,000 for age 50 and older). This limit is shared with Roth IRA — combined contributions to all IRAs cannot exceed $7,000/$8,000 total.

Contributions must be made from **earned income** (wages, salary, self-employment income, alimony in some cases). Investment income, rental income, and pension income do not qualify.

The contribution deadline is the **tax return due date** (April 15), not December 31. Extensions do not extend the IRA contribution deadline.

## Deductibility Rules and Phase-Outs (2025)

Traditional IRA contributions may or may not be tax-deductible depending on whether the contributor (or spouse) is covered by a workplace retirement plan and their income level.

**Contributor covered by a workplace plan (2025 phase-out ranges):**

| Filing Status | Phase-Out Range | Fully Deductible Below | No Deduction Above |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single / Head of Household | $79,000 – $89,000 | $79,000 | $89,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $126,000 – $146,000 | $126,000 | $146,000 |
| Married Filing Separately | $0 – $10,000 | $0 | $10,000 |

**Contributor NOT covered by workplace plan, but spouse IS covered (2025):**

Phase-out: **$236,000 – $246,000** MAGI. Deductibility is based on household income even though the contributor personally has no workplace plan.

**Neither spouse covered by workplace plan:** Contributions are fully deductible at any income level.

**Partial deductibility:** Within the phase-out range, the deduction is prorated. The deductible amount is calculated using the ratio of (phase-out ceiling − MAGI) / phase-out range width × contribution limit. Round to nearest $10, minimum $200 if any deduction remains.

## Non-Deductible Traditional IRA Contributions and Form 8606

When a contributor exceeds the deductibility phase-out but still has earned income, they may make **non-deductible Traditional IRA contributions**. The contribution creates "basis" in the IRA — after-tax dollars that will not be taxed again on withdrawal.

**Form 8606** is required to be filed with the tax return for any year a non-deductible contribution is made, and for any year a distribution is taken from an IRA that contains basis.

Form 8606 tracks cumulative basis across all years. Example: $7,000 non-deductible contribution each year for 5 years = $35,000 of basis. If the IRA grows to $70,000, then 50% of any distribution is tax-free (representing basis return).

## Basis Tracking — Critical Importance

Without a complete Form 8606 history, the IRS has no way to identify non-deductible basis in a Traditional IRA. In the absence of records, the IRS default is to treat the entire IRA as pre-tax — meaning the taxpayer pays ordinary income tax on the entire withdrawal, effectively paying double tax on their after-tax contributions.

**This is a real problem:** Clients who made non-deductible contributions decades ago and lost the Form 8606 records face a significant tax problem at distribution. The taxpayer has the burden of proof.

**Mitigation:** Reconstruct basis using old tax returns, IRS transcript requests (Form 4506-T), or financial institution records. File corrective Form 8606 if prior filings were missed (may require amended returns or a request for relief).

**When non-deductible Traditional IRA makes sense:** When the client is above the Roth IRA income limit AND above the Traditional IRA deductibility limit AND the backdoor Roth is unavailable or inadvisable. The non-deductible contribution still grows tax-deferred, and only the earnings (not basis) are taxed on withdrawal. This is the "worst case" IRA scenario but still better than a fully taxable account for long-term growth.

**When it makes less sense:** If the client has existing pre-tax IRA balances, the pro-rata rule on Roth conversions makes backdoor Roth messy (see Roth IRA document). In this case, non-deductible contributions may just create basis tracking complexity with limited benefit.

## Spousal IRA

A non-working spouse (or one with minimal earned income) can contribute to a Traditional IRA as long as the household has sufficient earned income. The working spouse's earned income can support both spouses' IRA contributions.

**2025 rule:** Household earned income must be at least equal to total IRA contributions. Example: Working spouse earns $50,000. Both spouses can contribute $7,000 each to their respective IRAs ($14,000 total), which is well below the $50,000 household earned income threshold.

**Deductibility** of spousal IRA follows the same rules: if the working spouse is covered by a workplace plan, the non-working spouse's deductibility is subject to the $236,000–$246,000 MFJ phase-out range.

**Practical value:** Builds separate retirement account ownership for the non-working spouse. Important for divorce protection, credit protection, and individual account flexibility in retirement.

## IRA Rollover Rules

**Direct transfer (trustee-to-trustee):** Funds move directly from one IRA custodian to another. No tax withholding, no 60-day clock, unlimited frequency. This is always preferable to indirect rollover.

**Indirect rollover (60-day rule):** The account owner receives a check and deposits it into another IRA within 60 days. The full distribution must be redeposited — including any amount withheld for taxes (the withheld portion must be made up from other funds). If the 60-day deadline is missed, the distribution becomes taxable (and penalized if under 59½).

**One-rollover-per-12-months rule:** The IRS limits indirect (60-day) rollovers to **one per 12-month period** across ALL IRAs combined (not per IRA). This is a calendar-rolling 12-month lookback, not a calendar year rule. Violating this rule causes the second rollover to be a taxable distribution.

**Important distinction:** The one-per-12-months rule applies only to IRA-to-IRA indirect rollovers. It does NOT apply to:
- Direct trustee-to-trustee transfers (unlimited)
- Rollovers from employer plans (401k, 403b) to IRA (unlimited)
- Rollovers from IRA to employer plan (unlimited)
- Roth conversions (unlimited)

**Rollover from employer plan to IRA:** When rolling a 401(k) to a Traditional IRA, the entire pre-tax balance transfers without current tax. After-tax (non-Roth) 401(k) contributions can be directed to a Roth IRA in the same rollover transaction — keeping pre-tax amounts in Traditional IRA and rolling after-tax amounts directly to Roth IRA (a "split rollover"). This avoids pro-rata issues.

## When Traditional IRA Beats Roth IRA

The Traditional IRA is superior in several situations:

**1. Currently in a higher tax bracket than expected retirement bracket.** The classic case — someone in the 32–37% bracket now who expects to be in the 22–24% bracket in retirement. The pre-tax deduction is worth more now than the tax-free growth benefit over time.

**2. Need the current-year deduction.** A high-income year (unusual bonus, business sale) can be partially offset by maxing pre-tax retirement contributions. The Traditional IRA deduction directly reduces AGI.

**3. High state income tax now, retiring to a no-income-tax state.** Contributing pre-tax in a high-tax state (e.g., California 9.3%, New York 6.85%) and withdrawing in a no-income-tax state (e.g., Florida, Texas, Nevada) arbitrages the state tax differential.

**4. Shorter time horizon to retirement.** With fewer years of compounding, the tax-free growth advantage of Roth is smaller. The immediate deduction from Traditional may deliver more after-tax value in a 5–10 year window.

**5. Charitable giving in retirement.** Qualified Charitable Distributions (QCDs) allow direct transfers from Traditional IRA to charity, completely bypassing income recognition. For charitably inclined clients, Traditional IRA assets are effectively worth 100 cents on the dollar to the charity. A Traditional IRA with charitable intent can be more tax-efficient than Roth.

## Creditor Protection Considerations

**401(k), 403(b), and other ERISA-qualified plans** have broad federal creditor protection under ERISA. Protected from most creditors regardless of state law (exceptions: divorce/QDRO, IRS tax liens).

**IRAs** have weaker federal protection. Under BAPCPA (bankruptcy law), IRAs are protected up to **$1,512,350 (inflation-adjusted)** in federal bankruptcy proceedings. Traditional and Roth IRA limits are combined.

**Rollover IRAs** (funds rolled from ERISA plans) have unlimited protection in federal bankruptcy, the same as the source plan. Courts have generally maintained this protection, though some state-level complications exist.

**State law variation:** Outside of bankruptcy, IRA protection depends entirely on state law. Some states (e.g., Texas, Florida) provide robust unlimited protection. Others provide partial or minimal protection.

**Planning implication:** For clients in high-liability occupations (physicians, contractors, business owners), preserving assets inside ERISA-qualified plans rather than rolling to IRA may be preferable from a creditor protection standpoint. This is especially relevant when considering whether to roll a former employer 401(k) to an IRA.

## Early Withdrawal — Traditional IRA

Distributions before age 59½ are subject to ordinary income tax plus a **10% early withdrawal penalty**. Exceptions to the penalty (but not the income tax) include:

- Death or disability
- Substantially Equal Periodic Payments (72(t) SEPP)
- First home purchase (lifetime maximum $10,000)
- Qualified higher education expenses
- Health insurance premiums while unemployed
- Unreimbursed medical expenses exceeding 7.5% of AGI
- IRS levy
- Reservist distributions
- Birth or adoption (up to $5,000)

The 10% penalty is waived but the distribution is still fully taxable as ordinary income (on pre-tax contributions and all earnings; basis from non-deductible contributions is returned tax-free proportionally).
