# Financial Planning for New Graduates: Building the Right Foundation

## The New Graduate Financial Priority Stack

The first few years after college are the most important years for building lasting financial habits. The decisions made about savings rate, debt strategy, and investment approach in your 20s have outsized impact due to compound growth over 40+ years.

**Priority sequence**:

1. **Emergency fund: $1,000-$2,000 minimum** — before any aggressive debt payoff or investing. One unexpected expense without an emergency fund sends you back to credit card debt.

2. **Capture the full employer 401(k) match** — this is a mandatory 50-100% immediate return. A 3% employer match on a 3% contribution to a $70,000 salary = $2,100 in free money. Never leave this on the table.

3. **Pay off high-interest debt** — credit cards (18-25%), personal loans above 7-8%

4. **Emergency fund to 3-6 months** — liquid, accessible savings covering essential expenses

5. **Roth IRA contributions** — $7,000/year maximum in 2025; early-career low tax bracket makes Roth contributions especially powerful

6. **Increase 401(k) beyond the match** — maximize the $23,500 annual contribution limit over time

7. **Address moderate-interest debt** — student loans at 4-6%; compare against investing returns

8. **Taxable investing** — after all tax-advantaged space is utilized

## Why Roth IRA Is Ideal for New Graduates

The tax math is compelling. A new graduate earning $55,000 is in the 22% marginal tax bracket (single filer). They contribute $7,000 to a Roth IRA, paying 22% tax on the contributions now.

At age 65, the same person is in the 32-35% bracket at peak earnings (or in the 24-28% bracket as a retiree managing RMDs). Had they contributed to a Traditional IRA at 22% and withdrawn at 32%, they would have done the tax math backward — pre-paying tax at 22% is almost always better than deferring to 32%.

**The 40-year compounding argument**: $7,000 invested in a Roth IRA at age 25, growing at 7% annually, becomes approximately $104,000 by age 65 — completely tax-free. The same contribution to a 22% Traditional IRA generates the same $104,000 before tax, but after the 24% distribution rate, you net approximately $79,000. The Roth advantage: $25,000 from a single year's contribution.

Over 40 years of maxing Roth IRA contributions at $7,000/year (inflation-adjusted): the difference is potentially hundreds of thousands of dollars in tax savings.

## Student Loan Strategy for New Graduates

The decision framework depends on loan type and income/debt ratio:

**Federal loans at 4-6%**: 
- If employed at a government or nonprofit employer: investigate PSLF immediately. Ten years of qualifying IDR payments followed by tax-free forgiveness may be worth more than aggressive payoff.
- If private sector: compare guaranteed loan payoff return (4-6%) vs. expected investment returns (7-9% historical) vs. Roth IRA tax benefit (22% bracket, 40 years of compounding). The Roth IRA tax benefit often outweighs aggressive federal loan payoff at 4-6%.

**Private loans at 7%+**: More compelling to aggressively pay these off — the guaranteed return from eliminating 7%+ debt is hard to beat.

**Practical approach for most new graduates**:
- Enroll in IDR (SAVE or IBR) for federal loans if PSLF is possible
- Make minimum payments on federal loans below 6%
- Pay extra on private loans or federal loans above 6%
- Max Roth IRA contributions simultaneously

## Health Insurance Decisions

**Always take employer coverage**: Even as a healthy young adult, one emergency room visit can be $10,000-$50,000. Medical debt is the leading cause of personal bankruptcy in the United States. Health insurance is not optional.

**Parents' plan until 26**: The ACA requires parents' employer health plans to cover children until age 26. Compare the cost: are you on the parent's plan for free, or would you pay a premium? If you can stay on the parent's plan without cost or with minimal cost, and the coverage is good, this may be the best short-term option.

**HDHP + HSA strategy**: If your employer offers a High-Deductible Health Plan with an HSA contribution, this is often the optimal choice for a healthy young adult:
- Lower monthly premium than PPO/HMO plans
- Employer may contribute to your HSA
- You contribute pre-tax funds; invest them; let them compound tax-free
- As a young, healthy person, you may have minimal medical expenses — the HSA becomes a stealth retirement account
- Building HSA savings in your 20s means decades of tax-free compounding

## Building Credit: The Long-Term Value

Good credit saves tens of thousands of dollars over a lifetime through lower interest rates on mortgages, car loans, and other credit.

**Building credit correctly**:
- Open one or two credit cards (preferably rewards cards with no annual fee initially)
- Use them for regular purchases (groceries, gas, Netflix)
- Pay the full statement balance every month — never carry a balance
- Keep credit utilization below 30% of available credit
- Do not close old accounts — length of credit history matters
- Check credit report annually at AnnualCreditReport.com

**What NOT to do**:
- Apply for multiple credit cards at once (multiple hard inquiries)
- Miss payments — even one 30-day late payment damages credit score significantly
- Open retail store credit cards for a one-time discount
- Co-sign loans for others unless prepared to take full responsibility

**Timeline**: A new graduate with no credit history can build a 720+ credit score within 2-3 years by following the above practices.

## Disability Insurance Gap: The Biggest Overlooked Risk

Young workers have minimal savings to fall back on if they cannot work. Disability insurance is critical:

- Check employer group disability: what is the definition of disability (own-occ vs. any-occ)? What is the benefit amount (typically 60% of salary)? Is it enough?
- Group disability typically pays 60% of salary with a $10,000-$15,000/month cap — sufficient for most entry-level salaries
- If pursuing a high-earning career (physician, attorney, engineer): begin researching individual own-occupation disability insurance now, while young and healthy — premiums are lowest in your 20s-30s

## Investing Fundamentals: Starting Simple

New graduates often overthink investment selection. Simple is better:

**Three-fund portfolio**:
1. Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF (VTI) or equivalent — 70%
2. Vanguard Total International Stock ETF (VXUS) — 20%
3. Vanguard Total Bond Market ETF (BND) — 10%

Total annual expense ratio: approximately 0.04%. Set up automatic investment. Rebalance annually. Do not change the allocation based on news or market conditions.

**Target-date fund alternative**: Choose the Fidelity Freedom or Vanguard Target Retirement fund with a target year closest to your expected retirement (2060 or 2065 for someone starting work today). Set contributions to automatically invest in this one fund. Never change it.

**The most important investment decision**: Not what to own, but whether to consistently invest 15-20% of income starting now. The specific fund choice is secondary to the behavior of consistent saving and investing.

## The Financial Adulting Checklist

Essential tasks in the first year after starting a job:

- [ ] Open a Roth IRA (Fidelity, Vanguard, or Schwab — all have no minimums)
- [ ] Set 401(k) contribution to at least capture full employer match
- [ ] Set 401(k) investment to lowest-cost index fund available (or target-date fund)
- [ ] Designate beneficiaries on 401(k) and IRA accounts
- [ ] Enroll in employer health insurance
- [ ] Enroll in employer life insurance (free through employer; at minimum, 1-2× salary)
- [ ] Open emergency fund savings account (high-yield savings; HYSA typically 4-5% in 2025)
- [ ] Open one credit card; set up autopay of full statement balance
- [ ] Review student loans: enroll in IDR if appropriate; understand payoff timeline
- [ ] Create a simple will if you have a partner, parents who depend on you, or any assets you want to direct at death (online services like Trust & Will: $100-$200)
- [ ] Check credit report annually
- [ ] Review insurance: renter's insurance ($15-$30/month; covers personal belongings and provides liability coverage)

## The Power of Starting Early: The Numbers

The most important single financial fact for a new graduate: time is the dominant variable in wealth accumulation.

**$500/month starting at 25** vs. **$500/month starting at 35** (7% annual return, both retiring at 65):
- Starting at 25: 40 years → approximately $1,318,000
- Starting at 35: 30 years → approximately $613,000

The 10-year head start more than doubles the terminal wealth — not from contributing twice as much (you contribute $60,000 more starting at 25), but from having 10 more years of compounding on the same monthly contributions.

The most powerful financial decision a new graduate can make is to immediately begin saving, even small amounts, and automate it so it continues regardless of competing pressures.
