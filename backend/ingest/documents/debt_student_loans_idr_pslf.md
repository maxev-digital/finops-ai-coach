# Student Loans: New Graduate Financial Strategy and Decision Framework

## The New Graduate's Debt Priority Framework

For a new graduate entering the workforce with student loan debt, competing financial priorities must be sequenced correctly:

### Priority Sequence for New Graduates with Student Loans

1. **Emergency fund: $1,000-$2,000 minimum**: Before aggressive loan repayment, a small emergency fund prevents reverting to credit cards for unexpected expenses.

2. **Capture employer 401(k) match**: Even if carrying student loan debt, a 50-100% employer match is an immediate return that exceeds any student loan interest rate. Max the match first.

3. **High-interest debt**: Any private loans at 7%+ or any other high-interest debt (credit cards) should be addressed before federal loans at 4-6%.

4. **Federal loan strategy determination** (income-driven vs. aggressive payoff): This is the most consequential decision — and it must be made before choosing a repayment approach.

5. **Additional tax-advantaged savings**: After loan strategy is determined, use Roth IRA (ideal for low-bracket new graduates), HSA if HDHP-eligible.

6. **Student loan aggressive payoff OR IDR optimization**: Based on PSLF eligibility and income-to-debt ratio analysis.

### The Income-to-Debt Ratio Heuristic

A simple framework for federal loan repayment strategy:

| Federal loan balance / Annual income | Suggested approach |
|--------------------------------------|-------------------|
| Below 1× (e.g., $40K debt, $60K income) | Aggressive payoff; IDR provides minimal long-term benefit |
| 1×-2× (e.g., $80K debt, $60K income) | Hybrid — IDR if PSLF-eligible; aggressive payoff otherwise |
| Above 2× (e.g., $150K debt, $60K income) | IDR almost always; PSLF if eligible; IDR forgiveness is likely |

High debt-to-income ratios are common among medical, dental, and law school graduates where advanced degrees command higher salaries over time. For these borrowers, IDR and PSLF analysis is essential.

## Prioritizing Student Loans vs. Retirement Savings

A critical tension: should extra money go to student loans or retirement accounts?

**Always fund retirement first to capture employer match** — this is never in question.

**Beyond the match**: Compare guaranteed loan payoff return vs. expected investment return:
- Federal loan at 5%: paying off earns a guaranteed 5% after-tax return
- Roth IRA for a new graduate in the 22% bracket: expected long-run return of 7-9% with 40+ years of tax-free compounding; the tax benefit is enormous due to low current bracket
- The Roth IRA's compounding advantage over 40 years typically favors contributing to Roth over aggressively paying 5% student loans (the tax-free compounding premium exceeds the guaranteed loan payoff return)

**Inflection point**: At loan rates above approximately 6-7%, loan payoff becomes more competitive with retirement investing (beyond the employer match). The math becomes loan-favorable when the guaranteed return matches or exceeds the risk-adjusted after-tax investment return.

## Prioritizing Student Loans vs. Home Purchase

Many graduates face: should I aggressively pay student loans or save for a home down payment?

**Factors favoring home purchase**:
- Housing costs in your market are rising faster than loan payoff benefit
- You have stable, long-term housing plans
- Rental costs exceed equivalent mortgage costs
- DTI allows mortgage qualification despite student loan payments (IDR payments are now counted differently for mortgage qualification — many mortgage programs use 1% of loan balance per month or the actual IDR payment, whichever is greater)

**Factors favoring loan payoff first**:
- High loan balance raises DTI above mortgage qualification thresholds
- Loan interest rates are high (7%+)
- Housing market uncertainty or career mobility likely to require relocation within 5 years

**Practical guidance**: For federal loans at 4-6%, a concurrent approach (moderate loan payments on IDR while saving for down payment) often makes sense. For private loans at 7%+, accelerating payoff may take priority.

## Refinancing Private Student Loans: The Decision

Private loan refinancing converts federal loans to private or restructures existing private loans at a new interest rate.

**When refinancing makes financial sense**:

1. **Significant interest rate differential**: Refinancing federal loans at 7-8% to private at 4-5% for a physician with stable employment and high income — savings of 2-3% on $200,000 = $4,000-$6,000/year.

2. **No PSLF eligibility**: Working in the private sector, no intention to pursue public service.

3. **High income, strong credit profile**: Medical resident transitioning to attending physician position; income rise makes standard repayment very manageable; federal protections have less value.

4. **Short remaining repayment horizon**: Federal protections matter less if you'll pay off in 3-5 years regardless.

**Do NOT refinance when**:
- Any current or future PSLF eligibility exists — refinancing eliminates PSLF permanently
- Income is uncertain or variable — federal IDR payment protection is critical
- You're relying on federal deferment or forbearance safety nets (private loans have limited options)
- You may return to school (federal loans offer deferment for enrollment; private loans may not)

**Comparison calculators**: Use lender comparison tools (Credible, SoFi, Earnest, Splash) to compare multiple private lenders simultaneously. Personal finance modeling sites (Student Loan Planner) provide detailed PSLF vs. refi comparisons.

## The Student Loan Interest Deduction

**2025 deduction limits**:
- Maximum deduction: $2,500 of student loan interest paid
- MAGI phase-out: Begins at $75,000 (single) / $155,000 (MFJ); fully phased out at $90,000 (single) / $185,000 (MFJ)
- Filing status: Married Filing Separately cannot claim this deduction

**Above-the-line deduction**: Reduces AGI regardless of whether you itemize — claimed on Schedule 1 of Form 1040.

**What qualifies**: Interest paid on any qualified student loan — including loans for yourself, your spouse, or your dependent. The loan must have been taken to pay qualified higher education expenses.

**Interest vs. fees**: Only actual interest qualifies — not origination fees or insurance premiums, though origination fees paid at issuance may be treated as points and amortized as interest over the loan term.

**Practical value**: Maximum deduction of $2,500 × 22% = $550 in federal tax savings. Meaningful but not transformative. For high earners above the phase-out, the deduction provides no benefit.

**During IDR/PSLF years**: For borrowers on IBR or SAVE with very low payments, most or all of the payment may be interest — the deduction is available to the extent interest is actually paid (not accrued).

## The Refinancing Math: Private Loan Case Study

**Scenario**: $150,000 in private graduate student loans at 7.5% average rate. New private loan refinance rate: 5.0%. 10-year repayment.

**Original loan payment**: $1,780/month
**Refinanced payment**: $1,593/month
**Monthly savings**: $187/month
**10-year total interest savings**: $22,440

**Considerations**: Assumes stable employment for 10 years; no need for federal protections; no PSLF eligibility. These savings are real — but only realized if you keep the loan at 5% and don't refinance again at a higher rate or face payment difficulties.

## Common Mistakes New Graduates Make with Student Loans

1. **Not enrolling in IDR when eligible for PSLF**: Standard repayment with PSLF-eligible employer — losing the PSLF benefit by overpaying.

2. **Automatically enrolling in 10-year standard repayment** without modeling IDR/PSLF: The default enrollment is not always optimal.

3. **Refinancing immediately without researching PSLF**: Many graduates don't know about PSLF; refinancing before learning about it locks in a permanent mistake.

4. **Not recertifying income annually on IDR**: Failure to recertify returns you to 10-year standard repayment — potentially a much larger payment.

5. **Paying extra on loans when IDR + PSLF is optimal**: Extra payments on PSLF-eligible loans are never forgiven — they reduce the forgiven balance.

6. **Assuming PSLF requires a specific employer from the start**: PSLF credits are cumulative across qualifying employers — you can accumulate qualifying payments at multiple qualifying employers over 10 years.

7. **Confusing the 10-year PSLF period with a single employer**: 120 qualifying payments across any combination of qualifying employers counts — career changes between qualified employers are fine.
