# Net Unrealized Appreciation (NUA) Strategy

## What Is NUA?

Net Unrealized Appreciation (NUA) refers to the increase in value of **employer stock** held inside a qualified retirement plan (401k, profit-sharing, ESOP) from the time the stock was contributed or purchased within the plan to the date of distribution.

The NUA strategy is a tax optimization technique that allows the plan participant to pay **long-term capital gains rates** on the appreciation component of employer stock rather than ordinary income rates — which would apply if the stock were rolled to an IRA and later distributed.

This distinction is critical. In a high ordinary income bracket, the difference between 37% ordinary income and 20% (plus 3.8% NIIT) capital gains can represent tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars in lifetime tax savings.

## How the Strategy Works

### Step 1: Lump-Sum Distribution

The NUA strategy requires a **lump-sum distribution** — the entire balance of the qualified plan must be distributed within a single calendar year. This is a strict requirement. Partial distributions or distributions spread across multiple years do not qualify for NUA treatment.

### Step 2: In-Kind Distribution of Employer Stock

The employer stock is distributed **in-kind** (as actual shares, not liquidated for cash) to a taxable brokerage account. The remaining non-stock plan assets (mutual funds, bonds, other investments) can be rolled to an IRA.

### Step 3: Tax Treatment at Distribution

At the time of distribution, the participant pays **ordinary income tax only on the cost basis** of the employer stock — what the plan paid for the shares (typically the price at the time contributions were made or stock was purchased in the plan). The cost basis is usually far below the current fair market value.

**No 10% early withdrawal penalty** applies to the in-kind distribution of employer stock to the extent of the cost basis, if the distribution otherwise qualifies as a lump-sum distribution (typically requires separation from service, reaching age 59½, death, or disability).

### Step 4: Tax Treatment at Sale

When the participant later **sells the employer stock** from the taxable brokerage account:
- The **NUA** (FMV at distribution minus the plan's cost basis) is taxed at **long-term capital gains rates** — regardless of how long the shares are held after distribution.
- Any **additional appreciation after distribution** (the stock rises further in value) is taxed as short-term or long-term capital gains depending on holding period after the distribution date.

## Numerical Example

**Scenario:** 401(k) contains employer stock with the following characteristics:
- Plan's cost basis in the stock: **$50,000**
- Fair market value at distribution: **$300,000**
- NUA: **$250,000**

**Option A — NUA Strategy (in-kind distribution):**
- At distribution: Pay ordinary income tax on $50,000 basis (e.g., at 32% = $16,000 tax)
- At sale: Pay 15% long-term capital gains on $250,000 NUA = $37,500 tax
- Total federal tax: ~$53,500

**Option B — Roll to IRA:**
- No immediate tax
- At withdrawal (say $300,000 lump sum): Pay ordinary income tax at 32% on all $300,000 = **$96,000 tax**

**NUA strategy saves approximately $42,500** in this example. At higher ordinary income rates (37% + state taxes), the savings are even larger. The savings grow further if the stock continues appreciating after distribution (the post-distribution appreciation is taxed at the shareholder's actual holding period rate, not NUA rate).

## When NUA Strategy Wins

**Large appreciation:** NUA is most valuable when the stock has grown substantially inside the plan. If the plan's cost basis is 15–20% of the current value, the NUA component is 80–85% of the total value being taxed at preferential rates.

**High ordinary income bracket in retirement:** If the participant will be in the 32–37% bracket on IRA withdrawals (large pre-tax IRA balance, pension income, required minimum distributions), then saving 15–22 percentage points on the NUA portion has significant dollar value.

**Intent to sell soon:** NUA treatment applies to the stock at distribution. If the plan is to sell the stock relatively quickly after distribution, the NUA is immediately monetized at capital gains rates. Long-term hold means the stock sits in a taxable account accumulating dividends and potential additional capital gains, which may diminish the advantage.

**State tax rates:** In high-income-tax states (California, New York, New Jersey), the state also taxes ordinary income at a higher rate than capital gains in many cases. The NUA strategy may produce additional state tax savings.

## When IRA Rollover Wins

**Modest appreciation:** If the employer stock has not appreciated significantly — cost basis is 60–70% of FMV — the tax advantage of NUA is reduced. The ordinary income on the full cost basis is paid upfront with modest gain to show for it in capital gains treatment.

**Tax deferral preference:** Rolling to an IRA defers all taxes until withdrawal. If the participant will not need the money for many years, the additional compounding on the untaxed amount inside the IRA may outweigh the eventual higher tax rate.

**Charitable intent:** If the employer stock will eventually be donated to charity, rolling to an IRA and using Qualified Charitable Distributions (QCDs) eliminates income tax entirely. Alternatively, donating appreciated stock from the taxable account after an NUA distribution achieves the same result and avoids capital gains.

**Low expected withdrawals:** If the participant has other income sources (Social Security, pension, other accounts) and won't be withdrawing large amounts from the IRA, effective tax rates in retirement may be lower than assumed.

**No current liquidity for the basis tax bill:** The NUA strategy requires paying ordinary income tax on the cost basis at distribution. If the participant doesn't have liquid assets outside the plan to cover this tax bill, a forced sale of the NUA stock to pay the taxes defeats the purpose.

## Lump-Sum Distribution Requirements

The IRS requires a **true lump-sum distribution** for NUA treatment. This means:

1. The **entire balance** of the plan must be distributed within a single tax year (January 1 – December 31)
2. The distribution must be triggered by one of four qualifying events: separation from service after age 55, reaching age 59½, death, or disability. **Note:** The age-55 separation from service rule applies only to qualified plans, not IRAs.
3. If the employer has multiple plans of the same type, ALL plans of that type must be distributed (a participant with two 401(k) accounts from the same employer must distribute both)
4. Prior IRA rollovers of prior distributions can disqualify NUA treatment in certain circumstances — consult a tax professional

The requirement to distribute the **entire plan** in one year means the participant cannot be selective. If the 401(k) has $800,000 in employer stock and $400,000 in mutual funds, both must be distributed. The mutual funds can be rolled to an IRA; only the employer stock is kept in the taxable account for NUA treatment.

## Tax Reporting: 1099-R and 1099-B

The plan administrator issues a **Form 1099-R** for the distribution:
- Box 1: Gross distribution (total FMV)
- Box 2a: Taxable amount (cost basis of employer stock + value of other assets not rolled over)
- Box 6: Net unrealized appreciation (the NUA amount — this is the key box for planning)

When the employer stock is later sold, the broker issues a **Form 1099-B**:
- The cost basis for the stock equals the FMV at the time of distribution from the plan
- The NUA is reported separately as long-term capital gain regardless of holding period
- Post-distribution appreciation is taxed based on actual holding period

The participant should retain the 1099-R showing the NUA in Box 6 to support the tax treatment when the stock is sold — sometimes years later.

## Age and Timing Considerations

**Younger participants:** The NUA strategy is available to those who separate from service at any age (not just retirement age), but those under 55 may face the 10% early withdrawal penalty on the cost basis portion unless another exception applies (age 59½, disability, etc.). The 10% penalty does not apply to NUA itself.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** The 3.8% NIIT applies to NUA gains for taxpayers above the $200,000/$250,000 MAGI threshold, making the effective long-term capital gains rate 18.8% or 23.8% rather than 15% or 20%. This reduces but does not eliminate the NUA advantage.

**Qualified Opportunity Zone (QOZ) planning:** A participant who triggers NUA gain could potentially reinvest that gain into a Qualified Opportunity Zone fund to defer the capital gains tax, layering two tax strategies. Requires specialized planning.

## Planning Checklist

Before recommending NUA strategy, confirm:
- [ ] Plan contains employer stock with significant appreciation (NUA/FMV ratio preferably above 50%)
- [ ] Full lump-sum distribution is feasible this calendar year
- [ ] A qualifying triggering event exists (separation, age 59½, death, disability)
- [ ] Client has liquidity to pay ordinary income tax on cost basis at distribution
- [ ] Client's expected tax rates on ordinary income in retirement are high (benefit is maximized above 22–24% brackets)
- [ ] Concentration risk of holding employer stock post-distribution is addressed in financial plan
- [ ] State tax treatment confirmed (some states tax NUA as ordinary income)
