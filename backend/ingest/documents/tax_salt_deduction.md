# SALT Deduction and State Tax Planning

Source: IRS Publication 17, IRC §164, Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2017).

---

## What Is the SALT Deduction

State and Local Tax (SALT) deduction allows itemizing taxpayers to deduct state and local taxes paid during the year. Components:
- State and local income taxes (or sales taxes, whichever is greater — not both)
- Real property taxes (primary residence, second home, investment property)
- Personal property taxes (vehicles, boats — must be based on value, not flat fee)

---

## The $10,000 Cap (TCJA 2017)

The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act capped the SALT deduction at $10,000 per return ($5,000 for Married Filing Separately). This cap applies regardless of actual taxes paid.

**Who is most affected:** High-income taxpayers in high-tax states. A California resident paying $50,000 in state income tax and $18,000 in property taxes can only deduct $10,000 total.

**States with highest SALT burdens:** California, New York, New Jersey, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Illinois, Maryland. Residents of these states often paid $30,000–$100,000+ in state taxes; the cap creates a permanent $20,000–$90,000+ loss of deduction.

**TCJA sunset:** The $10,000 SALT cap was scheduled to expire December 31, 2025. Congressional action determines whether the cap continues, increases, or reverts to unlimited deduction. As of 2025, multiple legislative proposals have been debated; no change has been enacted yet. Watch for 2026 law changes.

## Pass-Through Entity Tax (PTET) Workaround

Most states with income taxes have created a workaround for business owners. The IRS confirmed in Notice 2020-75 that the workaround is valid.

**How it works:**
1. S-Corp or partnership elects to pay state income tax at the entity level (PTET)
2. The entity deducts the state tax as a business expense — no $10,000 cap applies to business deductions
3. The individual partners/shareholders receive a credit on their state return for the state tax paid by the entity
4. Net effect: state income taxes on pass-through income are effectively fully deductible, bypassing the SALT cap

**States with PTET:** As of 2025, over 30 states offer PTET elections including California, New York, New Jersey, Texas (franchise tax context differs), Illinois, Minnesota, Massachusetts, and others.

**Limitation:** PTET only applies to business income flowing through pass-through entities. W-2 income and personal investment income remain subject to the $10,000 cap.

## Sales Tax vs. Income Tax Election

Taxpayers can deduct either state/local income taxes OR state/local general sales taxes — not both. The sales tax deduction is useful for:
- Residents of states with no income tax (Texas, Florida, Nevada, etc.) — they can deduct sales taxes
- Taxpayers who made large purchases in a year (new car, boat, home building materials) — actual sales taxes paid may exceed the IRS optional sales tax table amount

IRS provides optional tables based on state and income level; you can use actual receipts if higher. In practice, most residents of income-tax states use the income tax deduction.

## Property Tax Planning

Property taxes are included in SALT (subject to the $10,000 cap). Strategies:
- **Prepaying:** Some counties allow prepayment of next year's property taxes. However, the IRS has clarified that you can only deduct property taxes assessed in the current year — prepayment of not-yet-assessed taxes is not deductible. Most prepayment strategies under TCJA have been disallowed.
- **Investment property:** Property taxes on rental/investment property are deductible as a business expense on Schedule E, not subject to the SALT cap. Cap applies only to personal residences.
- **Property tax assessment appeals:** If your assessment is high relative to market value, appealing with a comparable sales analysis can reduce future taxes.

## AMT and SALT

Before TCJA, the Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) added back state taxes, effectively eliminating the deduction for AMT taxpayers anyway. The $10,000 cap partially addressed this by reducing state tax deductions across the board. Post-TCJA AMT exemptions are significantly higher ($88,100 single / $137,000 MFJ in 2025), meaning fewer taxpayers hit AMT. If TCJA sunsets and SALT cap is removed, AMT exposure may increase for high-income taxpayers who recapture large state tax deductions.

---

## Planning Considerations

**Timing deductions:** In states allowing it, paying Q4 state estimated taxes in December rather than January moves the deduction into the current year. Under the cap, this only matters if you haven't yet reached $10,000 total SALT — most affected taxpayers hit the cap with property taxes alone.

**Domicile change:** Moving from a high-tax state (California 13.3%, New York 10.9%) to a no-tax state (Texas, Florida, Nevada) eliminates state income tax permanently. Requirements for domicile change are state-specific and enforced aggressively by California and New York. Must genuinely establish new domicile: change driver's license, voter registration, where you spend time, professional affiliations, social ties.

**Mortgage interest interaction:** Both SALT and mortgage interest are Schedule A itemized deductions. With the high standard deduction ($30,000 MFJ), many taxpayers need both to benefit from itemizing. The $10,000 SALT cap makes it harder for moderate-income homeowners to exceed the standard deduction.

## The Itemization Math

To benefit from itemizing:
- SALT (capped at $10,000) + mortgage interest + charitable contributions + medical expenses > standard deduction

**Example for MFJ couple in Texas (no state income tax):**
- Property taxes: $8,000 (capped at $10,000 total with no state income tax)
- Mortgage interest: $18,000 (on $600K mortgage at 3% — decreases over time)
- Charitable: $5,000
- Total: $31,000 > $30,000 standard deduction. Barely worth itemizing.

**Example for MFJ couple in California:**
- State income tax: $40,000 (capped at $10,000)
- Property taxes: $18,000 (also capped — combined SALT = $10,000 max)
- Mortgage interest: $24,000
- Charitable: $10,000
- Total: $44,000 (vs. potentially $68,000 pre-cap). Still benefits from itemizing, but $24,000 of state taxes permanently lost.
