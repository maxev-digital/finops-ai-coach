# Disability Income Insurance: Protecting Your Income

## Why Disability Insurance Matters More Than Life Insurance

Disability is statistically more likely than premature death during working years:
- The Social Security Administration estimates that 1 in 4 workers entering the workforce today will become disabled before reaching retirement age
- The average long-term disability claim lasts 34.6 months (Council for Disability Awareness)
- The most common causes of long-term disability are not accidents — they are cancer, musculoskeletal disorders, heart disease, and mental health conditions

Yet most people insure their life at 10-12× income while leaving their income — the asset that generates all future wealth — underinsured or uninsured.

If you are 40 years old earning $150,000 per year and plan to retire at 65, you have approximately $3.75M in future earnings remaining. Disability insurance protects that asset.

## Social Security Disability: Why It's Not Enough

Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) denies 65% of initial applications and roughly 85% of reconsiderations. The average approval process takes 1-3 years from application. The definition of disability for SSDI is very strict: you must be unable to engage in any substantial gainful activity (SGA) — the legal definition is far harder to meet than most people assume.

Even if approved, the average SSDI benefit is approximately $1,500-$1,800/month (2025) — well below the income replacement needed for most middle-class or high-income workers. SSDI provides a safety net, not a plan.

## The Own-Occupation Definition: The Critical Policy Provision

The single most important feature of a disability policy is how it defines disability.

### True Own-Occupation Definition
You are considered disabled if you are unable to perform the specific material and substantial duties of your own occupation, even if you are capable of working in another occupation. Under this definition:

- A surgeon who loses fine motor control in one hand qualifies as disabled — even if they can still work as a hospital administrator
- An attorney who develops a serious stutter qualifies as disabled — even if they can work in a non-speaking legal role
- A dentist with severe back pain who cannot lean over patients qualifies

### Any-Occupation Definition
You are considered disabled only if you are unable to perform any occupation for which you are reasonably qualified by education, training, or experience. Under this definition:

- The surgeon above is NOT disabled — they can be a hospital administrator
- The attorney is NOT disabled — they can work in contract review
- This is also roughly the Social Security definition of disability

**The difference is massive**: True own-occupation disability policies are the standard for physicians, dentists, attorneys, CPAs, and other highly trained professionals. They cost more but provide income replacement for the job you were trained for.

### The Group Policy Compromise
Most employer group long-term disability policies use own-occupation for the first 2 years of disability, then switch to any-occupation. After 2 years, if you can work in any job, benefits stop — even if you cannot do your own job. For professionals, this is often insufficient.

## Income Replacement Target and Tax Treatment

### Target: 60-70% of Pre-Disability Gross Income

Social insurance conventions limit individual disability policies to 60-70% of pre-disability income to preserve the financial incentive to return to work.

### The After-Tax Math

**If you paid premiums with after-tax dollars** (individual policy, or employer plan where you pay 100% of the premium): disability benefits are income-tax-free.

**If your employer paid any portion of the premium**: That portion of the disability benefit is taxable as ordinary income.

**Practical implication**: 60% of $200,000 = $120,000/year in tax-free benefit, vs. your $200,000 gross (or approximately $140,000-$150,000 net of taxes). A 60% tax-free benefit often replaces 80-90% of your after-tax income — sufficient for most people to maintain their standard of living.

**Strategy for employer-provided plans**: If your employer offers group disability and pays the premium, consider paying the premium yourself (having it treated as compensation and paying income tax on the premium amount). This makes the benefit tax-free if you ever claim — a much better outcome for a potentially large, multi-year benefit.

## Key Policy Provisions

### Elimination Period
The elimination period is the waiting period before benefits begin — similar to a deductible in time rather than dollars. Common options: 30, 60, 90, 180, or 365 days.

- **90 days** is the most common sweet spot: long enough to reduce premiums meaningfully vs. 30-day elimination; short enough to bridge with an emergency fund
- **180-day or 365-day elimination**: Significantly cheaper; suitable only if you have sufficient liquid savings to cover 6-12 months of expenses
- You need an emergency fund sufficient to cover the elimination period — this is the primary reason emergency funds are critical for workers without large savings

### Benefit Period
How long benefits continue once disability begins:
- **2 years**: Covers the most common disability duration; cheaper but leaves long-term disability risk exposed
- **5 years**: Better; covers most claims
- **To age 65 or to age 67**: Most comprehensive and most expensive; eliminates the risk of a permanent disability that lasts to retirement

For young professionals with long careers ahead, "to age 65" benefit period is the recommended standard — the cost of a potentially 30-year benefit should be insured.

### Non-Cancelable and Guaranteed Renewable

**Non-cancelable**: The insurer cannot cancel the policy, change the terms, or raise premiums as long as you pay premiums. Your contract terms are locked in at issue.

**Guaranteed renewable**: The insurer must renew the policy but CAN raise premiums if raised for the entire rate class (not just you individually).

The gold standard is non-cancelable + guaranteed renewable (often written "non-can"). Anything less gives the insurer the ability to reprice or change your coverage at an inconvenient time.

### Cost of Living Adjustment Rider (COLA)

Automatically increases the disability benefit each year to keep pace with inflation, typically at the lesser of CPI or a cap (e.g., 3%). Critical for long-term disability benefits that may continue for 20+ years — a $5,000/month benefit in 2025 buys far less in 2045 without inflation adjustment.

### Residual/Partial Disability Rider

Pays a partial benefit if you can return to work but earn less than your pre-disability income due to the disability. Example: a surgeon who returns to practice part-time and earns 50% of prior income would receive partial disability benefits making up a portion of the income gap.

Without this rider, the policy is typically all-or-nothing — you're either fully disabled or not. Most real-world disability situations involve partial return to work.

### Future Purchase Option Rider

Allows you to purchase additional coverage as your income increases, without medical underwriting. Critical for young professionals whose income will grow substantially over their career — buy the option now at good health, exercise it later as income rises.

## The Market for Individual Disability Insurance

The premier carriers for true own-occupation individual disability insurance for professionals:
- **Guardian**: Highly rated; true own-occ available for most occupations; strong claims-paying history
- **Principal**: Strong for medical and dental professionals; own-occ policies
- **Ameritas**: Competitive; own-occ available; often slightly cheaper than Guardian/Principal
- **Mass Mutual**: Mutual insurer; strong dividend history on whole life and solid disability offerings
- **Unum**: Group market leader; individual policies available but own-occ definition narrower for some classes

Individual disability insurance is underwritten — your health, occupation, and income are all evaluated at application. Buy it while young and healthy; premiums and availability worsen with age and health conditions.

## High-Income Professionals: The Inadequacy of Group Coverage

For physicians, dentists, attorneys, and other high-earning professionals, employer group disability is almost universally insufficient:
- Typically limited to 60% of base salary only — not including bonuses, partnership distributions, or on-call pay
- Maximum monthly benefit caps (often $10,000-$15,000/month — inadequate for a physician earning $300,000+)
- Any-occupation definition after 24 months
- Coverage ends when you leave the employer

A physician earning $400,000 with a $15,000/month group disability benefit has approximately $17,000/month ($200,000/year) in income unprotected by the group plan. An individual own-occupation supplemental policy fills this gap.

## Self-Employed

Self-employed individuals have no employer group plan — individual disability insurance is the only option. Business overhead expense (BOE) insurance covers the business's fixed expenses (rent, staff salaries, utilities) during a disability, protecting the business while the individual DI policy covers personal income. Together, these allow the business to survive and resume operations after the owner recovers.

Some self-employed individuals can deduct disability insurance premiums as a business expense if the policy covers business-related income — consult a tax advisor for the specific structure.
