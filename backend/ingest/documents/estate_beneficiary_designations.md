# Beneficiary Designations: The Estate Planning Documents That Override Your Will

## Assets That Pass by Beneficiary Designation

Certain assets transfer directly to named beneficiaries at death, completely bypassing the will and probate process. These transfers are governed by contract law — the financial institution is legally bound to pay the named beneficiary, regardless of any contrary instruction in your will or trust.

Assets passing by beneficiary designation:
- **Traditional and Roth IRAs**: Beneficiary designation on file with the IRA custodian
- **401(k), 403(b), 457(b) plans**: Beneficiary designation on file with plan administrator
- **Life insurance policies**: Named beneficiaries receive death benefit proceeds
- **Annuities**: Named beneficiaries receive the remaining contract value
- **POD (Payable on Death) bank accounts**: Bank accounts with a named payable-on-death beneficiary
- **TOD (Transfer on Death) brokerage accounts**: Securities accounts with a transfer-on-death designation

For most American families, these assets represent the majority of accumulated wealth — particularly IRAs, 401(k)s, and life insurance. Getting beneficiary designations right is often more financially impactful than having a perfectly drafted will.

## Primary vs. Contingent Beneficiaries

**Primary beneficiaries**: Receive the asset directly. Multiple primary beneficiaries share the proceeds by specified percentage (50%/50%, or 33.3% each for three beneficiaries).

**Contingent beneficiaries**: Receive the asset only if all primary beneficiaries predecease the account owner or disclaim the inheritance. They are the backup.

**Best practice**: Always name both primary and contingent beneficiaries. If a primary beneficiary predeceases you and no contingent is named, the asset may default to your estate — triggering probate and losing favorable beneficiary treatment.

## Per Stirpes vs. Per Capita Distribution

When a named beneficiary predeceases you, the distribution method determines what happens to their share:

**Per Stirpes** ("by the branch"): If a primary beneficiary dies before you, their share passes to their descendants (children, then grandchildren). Example: You name your three children equally. One child predeceases you leaving two grandchildren. With per stirpes, those grandchildren split their deceased parent's 1/3 share.

**Per Capita** ("by the head"): If a primary beneficiary dies before you, their share is divided equally among the surviving primary beneficiaries. The predeceased beneficiary's descendants receive nothing from that account.

**Recommendation**: Most estate planners recommend per stirpes for retirement accounts and life insurance because it preserves the intended family distribution. Verify which elections are available on your custodian's beneficiary form — not all custodians offer per stirpes.

## Common Beneficiary Designation Mistakes

### Never Updated After Divorce

This is the most costly and common mistake. An ex-spouse who remains named as beneficiary on a life insurance policy or IRA will receive those assets, regardless of a divorce decree, new will, or remarriage. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Egelhoff v. Egelhoff (2001) that ERISA beneficiary designations cannot be automatically revoked by divorce for employer plans — federal law preempts state anti-divorce-revocation statutes for IRAs and 401(k)s.

**Immediate action required**: Update all beneficiary designations the day your divorce is final. Do not wait.

### Never Updated After Remarriage or New Children

A new spouse or child born after beneficiary designations were last updated receives nothing from accounts with old designations — even if your will provides for them. Beneficiary designations are account-specific and must be updated account by account.

### Estate Named as Beneficiary

Naming "my estate" as beneficiary on a retirement account:
- Forces probate on retirement assets (defeats the purpose of beneficiary designation)
- Eliminates the ability for individual heirs to do a 60-day rollover
- For IRAs: eliminates the 10-year rule availability — the estate must use the 5-year rule if owner died before RMD age, or the remaining life expectancy method if after RMD age (which may be less favorable)
- Removes flexibility for disclaimers and post-mortem planning

Never name your estate as beneficiary unless there is a specific, attorney-confirmed reason.

### Minor Named as Direct Beneficiary

A minor child cannot legally own assets. If a minor is named as direct beneficiary on an IRA or life insurance policy:
- A court must appoint a property guardian (guardian of the estate) to manage the assets
- The court process is expensive, public, and time-consuming
- The guardian must file annual accountings with the court
- At age 18 (or 21 in some states), the full amount passes to the child with no restrictions

**Correct approach**: Name a trust for the benefit of the minor child as beneficiary, with a trustee who can manage the funds and distribute for health, education, maintenance, and support on terms you specify.

### No Contingent Beneficiary Named

If a primary beneficiary predeceases you and no contingent is named, the account typically passes to the estate — losing all the benefits of beneficiary designation (probate avoidance, favorable tax rules, flexibility).

### Trust as IRA Beneficiary Without Expert Drafting

Post-SECURE Act, naming a trust as an IRA beneficiary is significantly more complex. See the section below on trust considerations.

## Surviving Spouse as IRA Beneficiary: Special Rules

A surviving spouse has unique options that no other beneficiary has:

**Option 1 — Spousal Rollover**: Roll the inherited IRA into their own IRA. The surviving spouse's own RMD rules apply (RMDs begin at age 73 or 75 per SECURE 2.0). This is typically optimal for a younger surviving spouse.

**Option 2 — Treat as Own**: Elect to treat the inherited IRA as their own account. Same result as spousal rollover.

**Option 3 — Remain as Inherited IRA**: Keep the account as an inherited IRA. RMDs are based on the deceased spouse's RMD schedule. This is sometimes preferable if the surviving spouse is under age 59½ — inherited IRA distributions avoid the 10% early withdrawal penalty, while distributions from a rollover to their own IRA would be penalized before 59½.

## The 10-Year Rule for Non-Spouse Beneficiaries (Post-SECURE Act)

For most non-spouse beneficiaries who inherit after December 31, 2019, the "stretch IRA" — taking distributions over their own life expectancy — is no longer available. Instead:

**10-Year Rule**: The entire inherited IRA must be fully distributed by December 31 of the 10th year after the year of the account owner's death.

**Annual RMD requirement within the 10-year period**: If the account owner died on or after their Required Beginning Date (April 1 following the year they reached age 73 or 75), the beneficiary must take annual minimum distributions in years 1-9 based on their single life expectancy, then distribute the remaining balance by year 10.

If the owner died before their RMD start date, no annual distributions are required in years 1-9 — the beneficiary can take nothing and empty the account in year 10 (but this strategy may create a tax spike in year 10).

**Eligible Designated Beneficiaries (EDBs)** who can still use the stretch/lifetime distribution rules:
- Surviving spouse
- Minor child of the account owner (until age 21, then transitions to 10-year rule)
- Disabled or chronically ill individuals
- Individual not more than 10 years younger than the account owner

## Trust as IRA Beneficiary

Naming a trust as IRA beneficiary can accomplish estate planning goals (control over distributions, spendthrift protection, special needs planning), but the rules are complex post-SECURE Act.

For a trust to qualify for the most favorable inherited IRA treatment ("see-through" trust), it must meet four requirements:
1. Valid under state law
2. Irrevocable at owner's death
3. Beneficiaries identifiable from trust instrument
4. Trust documentation provided to IRA custodian by October 31 of the year following the year of death

**Conduit trust**: Requires all RMDs to pass through to beneficiaries immediately; beneficiaries are treated as the account's direct beneficiaries for RMD purposes.

**Accumulation trust**: Can accumulate distributions inside the trust rather than distributing immediately; provides more control but generally results in less favorable tax treatment post-SECURE Act.

This area changed significantly with the SECURE Act. Any trust designed as an IRA beneficiary should be reviewed by an estate planning attorney familiar with post-SECURE Act rules.

## Annual Review Checklist

Beneficiary designations should be reviewed after every major life event:
- [ ] Marriage or remarriage
- [ ] Divorce or legal separation
- [ ] Birth or adoption of a child
- [ ] Death of a named beneficiary
- [ ] Change in relationship with a named beneficiary
- [ ] Beneficiary's divorce or financial difficulties (may affect whether they should receive large sums outright)
- [ ] Change in your estate planning goals or family circumstances
- [ ] Moving to a different state (some state laws affect spousal rights)
- [ ] Starting a new job (new retirement plan needs designations)

Minimum: review all beneficiary designations annually alongside your overall financial review. Keep a master list of all accounts and their current beneficiary designations.
