# Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) — SECURE 2.0 Rules

## RMD Age by Birth Year

SECURE 2.0 (enacted December 2022) changed the RMD starting age for the second time in three years. The applicable age depends entirely on the account owner's birth year:

| Birth Year | RMD Starting Age |
|---|---|
| Before 1951 | 70½ (pre-SECURE Act) or 72 (post-SECURE Act, pre-SECURE 2.0) |
| 1951–1959 | **73** |
| 1960 and later | **75** |

The transition for those born in 1951–1959 to age 73 was effective January 1, 2023. Those born 1960 and later will not reach their RMD age until 2035 at the earliest.

**Practical note:** For clients born in 1959, there was a temporary ambiguity in the law because SECURE Act established age 72, and SECURE 2.0 established both age 73 and age 75 in overlapping ways. IRS guidance clarified that those born in 1959 have an RMD starting age of **73**, not 75.

## Required Beginning Date (RBD)

The **Required Beginning Date** is the last date by which the first RMD must be taken:

**RBD = April 1 of the year following the year the account owner reaches their RMD age.**

Example: A client born in 1952 reaches age 73 in 2025. Their RBD is **April 1, 2026**. They may take their 2025 RMD at any point during 2025, or they may delay it until April 1, 2026 — but no later.

**All subsequent RMDs are due by December 31** of each calendar year.

## The Double-Distribution Trap in the First Year

Delaying the first RMD to the April 1 deadline in year two means **two RMDs must be taken in the same calendar year**:
- The prior year's RMD (taken by April 1)
- The current year's RMD (taken by December 31)

This double distribution could push the taxpayer into a higher tax bracket, increase Medicare IRMAA premiums for the following year, and increase Social Security benefit taxation.

**Best practice:** In most cases, take the first RMD during the actual RMD year (not deferred to April 1 of the following year) to avoid the double-distribution effect. Exceptions may apply if the client expects significantly lower income in the following year.

## Uniform Lifetime Table — Key Factors

The IRS Uniform Lifetime Table (updated effective 2022, still in use) determines the distribution period for calculating RMDs. The RMD for any year = Account Balance (as of December 31 of the prior year) ÷ Distribution Period Factor.

**Key factors to memorize:**

| Age | Distribution Period Factor |
|---|---|
| 72 | 27.4 |
| 73 | 26.5 |
| 74 | 25.5 |
| 75 | 24.6 |
| 76 | 23.7 |
| 77 | 22.9 |
| 78 | 22.0 |
| 79 | 21.1 |
| 80 | 20.2 |
| 82 | 18.5 |
| 85 | 16.0 |
| 88 | 13.7 |
| 90 | 12.2 |
| 95 | 8.9 |
| 100 | 6.4 |

**Example calculation:** Account balance December 31, 2024 = $500,000. Owner turns 75 in 2025. Distribution period = 24.6. RMD = $500,000 ÷ 24.6 = **$20,325**.

**Joint Life Expectancy Table exception:** If the sole beneficiary is a spouse more than 10 years younger than the account owner, the Joint Life and Last Survivor Table (which produces a longer distribution period and smaller RMDs) may be used instead.

## IRA RMD Aggregation Rules

**Multiple IRA accounts:** Calculate the RMD separately for each Traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, and SIMPLE IRA. **The total RMDs can be taken from any one IRA or any combination of IRAs.** The calculation is per-account, but the withdrawal can be aggregated.

Example: Three Traditional IRAs with individual RMDs of $5,000, $8,000, and $3,000 = total RMD of $16,000. The owner can take all $16,000 from the largest IRA if preferred.

**403(b) accounts:** Same aggregation rule applies — calculate per account, can take from any 403(b).

**401(k) plans:** Each 401(k) plan must calculate and take its own RMD separately. 401(k) RMDs cannot be aggregated across plans or with IRA RMDs.

**Roth IRA:** No RMDs during the owner's lifetime. (Roth 401(k) also eliminated RMDs starting 2024.)

## Still-Working Exception

An active employee who **has not separated from service** with their current employer can **delay RMDs from that employer's 401(k) plan** past their RMD age, as long as they continue working for that employer.

**Conditions:**
1. Must still be employed by the plan sponsor
2. Must not be a **5% owner** of the company. If the participant owns 5% or more of the company, the still-working exception does NOT apply — RMDs must begin regardless of employment status.

**IRA accounts are not covered by this exception.** RMDs from Traditional IRAs must begin at the standard RMD age regardless of employment.

**Planning implication:** A 74-year-old executive with a large 401(k) who continues working can keep those 401(k) assets growing tax-deferred and avoid RMDs until retirement — but must take RMDs from any IRAs and any prior employer 401(k)s.

## Roth 401(k): RMDs Eliminated by SECURE 2.0

Beginning January 1, 2024, Roth 401(k) accounts are **no longer subject to RMDs** during the owner's lifetime. This aligns Roth 401(k) treatment with Roth IRA treatment.

For participants who reached RMD age before 2024 and had been taking Roth 401(k) RMDs, those distributions are no longer required starting in 2024. Excess distributions already taken in prior years cannot be put back.

## Qualified Charitable Distribution (QCD)

A QCD allows an IRA owner **age 70½ or older** to transfer funds directly from an IRA to a qualifying public charity.

**2025 QCD limit:** Up to **$105,000** per taxpayer per year (annually indexed for inflation starting 2023 per SECURE 2.0).

**Tax treatment:** The QCD is **excluded from gross income** entirely — it never hits the tax return as income. This is more valuable than a standard charitable deduction because it reduces AGI directly, which affects:
- Social Security benefit taxation (provisional income calculation)
- Medicare IRMAA premium surcharges
- State income taxes in states that don't conform to federal itemized deductions
- Phase-outs of various credits and deductions

**QCD satisfies RMD:** A QCD counts toward the annual RMD. A taxpayer with a $10,000 RMD who makes a $10,000 QCD satisfies the RMD with $0 additional income.

**Requirements:**
- Distribution must go directly from the IRA custodian to the charity (check made payable to the charity, not the IRA owner)
- Charity must be a 501(c)(3) public charity (not a donor-advised fund, not a private foundation)
- Age 70½ requirement is based on the actual date of birth, not the RMD age — someone born in 1955 with an RMD age of 73 can start making QCDs at 70½

**QCD from Roth IRA:** Technically permitted but generally not advisable, as Roth IRA distributions are already tax-free.

## Penalty for Missed RMDs

Failure to take a required minimum distribution results in a penalty:

**25% excise tax** on the amount that should have been distributed but was not (reduced from 50% by SECURE 2.0).

**Correction window:** If the missed RMD is corrected within a **2-year correction window**, the penalty is further reduced to **10%**.

**Self-correction through Automatic Waiver:** The IRS has established a self-correction procedure. If the taxpayer takes the missed distribution and files Form 5329 with an explanation, the IRS may waive the penalty. Historically, the IRS has been lenient with first-time, good-faith errors.

**Best practice for advisors:** Set up a monitoring system to calculate and track each client's RMD annually, confirm distributions are completed before year-end, and establish a December-check workflow.

## Inherited IRA RMDs and the 10-Year Rule (SECURE Act)

Non-spouse beneficiaries who inherit retirement accounts from owners who died after December 31, 2019 are generally subject to the **10-year rule** — the entire account must be depleted within 10 years of the owner's death.

**Annual RMD requirement within the 10-year period:** IRS finalized regulations in 2024 clarifying that if the original owner had already reached their RMD age at the time of death, beneficiaries subject to the 10-year rule must take annual RMDs in years 1–9 AND deplete the account by the end of year 10. (The requirement for annual RMDs during the 10-year period was not enforced for 2021–2024, but applies going forward.)

**Eligible Designated Beneficiaries (EDBs)** who can use the lifetime stretch distribution instead of the 10-year rule:
- Surviving spouse
- Minor children of the deceased owner (switches to 10-year rule when they reach age of majority)
- Disabled or chronically ill individuals
- Beneficiaries not more than 10 years younger than the deceased owner

**Inherited Roth IRA:** Beneficiaries inherit the tax-free treatment but are subject to the 10-year rule. No annual RMD requirement during the 10-year period (since there were no RMDs for the original owner). The account must be emptied by end of year 10 — but all distributions are tax-free if the 5-year holding period was satisfied by the original owner.
