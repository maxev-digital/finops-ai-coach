# Irrevocable Trusts: Advanced Estate Tax and Wealth Transfer Strategies

## Why Irrevocable Trusts

An irrevocable trust is an estate planning tool where the grantor permanently transfers assets to the trust and gives up control. In exchange for losing control, the grantor achieves:
- **Estate tax reduction**: Assets in the trust are no longer in the grantor's taxable estate
- **Asset protection**: Creditors generally cannot reach assets in a properly structured irrevocable trust
- **Wealth transfer**: Assets (and all future appreciation) transfer to beneficiaries at reduced or zero gift/estate tax cost

The trade-off is absolute: you cannot change your mind, retrieve the assets, or alter the terms without court intervention. These trusts require careful advance planning and expert legal drafting.

## Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT)

### Purpose
Remove life insurance proceeds from the taxable estate. Without an ILIT, life insurance death benefits are included in the insured's estate if they held any "incidents of ownership" (right to change beneficiaries, right to borrow against the policy, right to surrender it).

### How It Works
1. Create an irrevocable trust and name it as owner and beneficiary of the life insurance policy
2. Grantor makes annual gifts to the trust in amounts sufficient to pay premiums
3. Trust owns the policy; grantor has no incidents of ownership
4. At death, proceeds are paid to the trust income-tax-free and estate-tax-free
5. Trustee distributes proceeds to family beneficiaries per trust terms

### Crummey Notices
For the annual premium gifts to the trust to qualify for the annual gift exclusion ($19,000 per beneficiary in 2025), beneficiaries must receive a temporary right to withdraw the contributed funds — a "Crummey power." The trustee sends written notice (a "Crummey notice") to each beneficiary giving them a window (typically 30-60 days) to withdraw their share of the gift. In practice, beneficiaries do not exercise this right, and the funds remain in trust to pay premiums. This notice requirement must be carefully followed to preserve the gift tax exclusion.

### Tax Result
Death benefit: income-tax-free (IRC §101) and estate-tax-free (outside the insured's estate). For a $5M policy on a $20M estate, this removes $5M from estate tax calculation, saving approximately $2M in estate tax at the 40% rate.

## Grantor Retained Annuity Trust (GRAT)

### Purpose
Transfer appreciation on assets to heirs at minimal or zero gift tax cost.

### Mechanics
1. Grantor transfers assets to an irrevocable trust
2. Trust pays the grantor a fixed annuity for a defined term (typically 2-10 years)
3. The annuity amount is calculated so the present value of all annuity payments equals the full value of the assets transferred — a "zeroed-out" GRAT
4. At the end of the term, any remaining assets in the trust (i.e., the appreciation above the IRS 7520 rate) pass to heirs gift-tax free

### The 7520 Rate
The IRS Applicable Federal Rate (Section 7520 Rate) determines how much the GRAT annuity must be to zero out the taxable gift. If assets earn more than this rate, the excess passes to heirs with no gift tax. If assets earn less (or the grantor dies during the term), the GRAT fails — assets return to the grantor's estate.

### When GRATs Work Best
- Rapidly appreciating assets: closely held business interests pre-liquidity event, stock in a company expected to appreciate significantly
- Lower 7520 rate environment: lower hurdle rate for the GRAT to "work"
- Rolling GRATs: create new 2-year GRATs annually to continuously harvest appreciation

### Mortality Risk
If the grantor dies during the GRAT term, all assets return to the estate as if the GRAT never existed. For older or unhealthy grantors, GRAT terms should be short to minimize this risk.

## Spousal Lifetime Access Trust (SLAT)

### Purpose
Remove assets from the grantor's estate while maintaining indirect access through the beneficiary spouse.

### How It Works
1. One spouse (the grantor) funds an irrevocable trust for the benefit of the other spouse
2. The trustee can distribute income and principal to the beneficiary spouse for health, education, maintenance, and support
3. Because assets pass to the spouse (not the grantor), the grantor has removed assets from their estate
4. The beneficiary spouse can access funds as needed; through the marriage, the grantor indirectly benefits

### The Reciprocal Trust Doctrine Risk
If both spouses create SLATs for each other (to each remove assets from their estates), the IRS may collapse the two trusts under the reciprocal trust doctrine — treating the exchange as if each spouse funded their own trust (no estate tax benefit). To avoid this, the trusts must NOT be mirror images: different trustees, different beneficiary classes, different timing, different terms.

### Key Risk
If the beneficiary spouse predeceases the grantor, or if the couple divorces, the grantor loses indirect access to the funds. The assets remain in trust for other beneficiaries (children). This is a permanent, irreversible consequence. SLATs should be funded with assets the grantor can genuinely afford to never access directly.

## Qualified Personal Residence Trust (QPRT)

### Purpose
Transfer a primary or vacation home to heirs at a discounted gift tax value.

### How It Works
1. Grantor transfers the home to an irrevocable trust
2. Grantor retains the right to live in the home for a defined term (e.g., 10-15 years)
3. At the end of the term, ownership passes to the beneficiaries (typically children)
4. The taxable gift is the present value of the remainder interest — discounted because the heirs don't receive the home until the term ends

### The Gift Tax Discount
Using the 7520 rate to calculate the present value of the future remainder interest, the taxable gift is substantially less than the full home value. Example: $2M home transferred to QPRT with 10-year term might generate a taxable gift of only $800,000-$1,200,000, depending on the 7520 rate and the grantor's age.

### Mortality Risk
If the grantor dies during the trust term, the home returns to the estate. No estate tax benefit is achieved; the planning fails.

### Post-Term Issues
After the term ends, the grantor technically no longer owns the home. If they want to continue living in it, they must pay fair market rent to the trust/beneficiaries. This rent payment, while inconvenient, actually transfers additional wealth to the beneficiaries tax-free (the rent reduces the grantor's estate and increases the beneficiaries' assets with no gift tax).

## Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT)

### Purpose
Provide an income stream to the grantor (or other beneficiaries) for life or a term, with the remainder passing to charity. Provides a charitable deduction and avoids capital gains on appreciated assets contributed.

### Types
- **Charitable Remainder Annuity Trust (CRAT)**: Pays a fixed dollar amount annually; no additional contributions after funding
- **Charitable Remainder Unitrust (CRUT)**: Pays a percentage of the trust's current value annually; fluctuates with trust value; allows additional contributions

### Tax Benefits
1. **Capital gains avoidance**: Transfer appreciated stock or real estate to the CRT; the trust sells and reinvests without recognizing capital gains
2. **Charitable deduction**: Deduct the present value of the charitable remainder interest in the year of funding (typically 30-50% of the contributed value)
3. **Income stream**: Receive distributions for your lifetime or a term of years

### Who This Serves
Retirees with highly appreciated assets (low-basis stock, real estate), strong charitable intent, and a desire for income. Not appropriate if the primary goal is wealth transfer to heirs — the remainder goes to charity, not family.

## Dynasty Trust

### Purpose
Transfer wealth across multiple generations, avoiding estate tax at each generation, within a perpetual trust structure.

### Key Features
- **Generation-skipping**: Properly drafted to avoid generation-skipping transfer (GST) tax when trust assets benefit grandchildren and beyond; uses the grantor's GST exemption ($13,990,000 in 2025, same as estate tax exemption)
- **Asset protection**: Trust assets are protected from beneficiaries' creditors and divorcing spouses
- **Perpetual duration**: Allowed in states without the common law Rule Against Perpetuities: South Dakota, Delaware, Nevada, Alaska, Wyoming are common choices for siting dynasty trusts
- **Governance**: Trust document specifies distribution standards and investment policies for all future generations

### Jurisdiction Selection
Dynasty trusts are commonly established in South Dakota, Delaware, or Nevada because:
- No state income tax on undistributed trust income (some states)
- No Rule Against Perpetuities (trusts can continue indefinitely)
- Strong trust decanting laws (allows modification of irrevocable trusts)
- Strong asset protection statutes
- Sophisticated trust administration infrastructure

A grantor in Texas can establish a South Dakota dynasty trust — the trust is governed by South Dakota law even though the grantor and beneficiaries live elsewhere.

## Tax Treatment of Grantor Trusts

Many irrevocable trusts are structured as "grantor trusts" for income tax purposes while being irrevocable for estate tax purposes. This means:
- The grantor reports all trust income on their personal return — even though the assets are outside their estate
- The grantor pays income taxes on trust income — which is itself a tax-free gift to the trust beneficiaries (the trust is not depleted by tax payments)
- Transactions between the grantor and the trust are not taxable events (the grantor is treated as the owner for income tax but not for estate tax)

This combination — irrevocable for estate tax, grantor trust for income tax — is a powerful planning technique used in intentionally defective grantor trusts (IDGTs) and installment sales to IDGTs.
