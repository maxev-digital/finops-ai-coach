# HELOC Strategies: Home Equity Line of Credit

## HELOC Definition and Structure

A Home Equity Line of Credit (HELOC) is a revolving credit facility secured by the equity in your home. Like a credit card, you can draw, repay, and redraw up to a credit limit — but secured by your home, so the interest rate is significantly lower than unsecured debt.

**Basic structure**:
- **Draw period**: Typically 10 years. During this period, you can borrow up to your credit limit as needed, at any time. Many HELOCs allow interest-only payments during the draw period (though paying principal is possible).
- **Repayment period**: After the draw period ends, you enter repayment — typically 10-20 years of principal + interest payments on whatever balance you carried from the draw period. No additional draws are allowed.
- **Variable interest rate**: Most HELOCs are variable rate, typically indexed to the Prime Rate plus a margin. As the Federal Reserve adjusts the federal funds rate, Prime Rate follows — and your HELOC rate adjusts accordingly.

**2025 rate context**: After the 2022-2023 rate hikes, the Prime Rate rose from 3.25% to 8.5%. Most HELOCs were at Prime + 0.25-1.0%, pushing rates to 8.75-9.5% — historically high levels. As the Fed potentially cuts rates, HELOC rates should decline, but variable rate risk remains.

## HELOC vs. Home Equity Loan

These are distinct products that serve different needs:

| Feature | HELOC | Home Equity Loan |
|---------|-------|-----------------|
| Type | Revolving line of credit | Lump sum installment loan |
| Rate | Variable (usually) | Fixed |
| Payment structure | Interest-only during draw; P+I in repayment | Fixed P+I from day 1 |
| Draw flexibility | Multiple draws over 10-year draw period | One-time disbursement |
| Closing costs | Lower (often $0-$500) | Higher ($1,000-$3,000+) |
| Best for | Ongoing or uncertain needs, flexibility | Known one-time large expense |

**Choose home equity loan when**: You have a specific, defined project cost (kitchen remodel at $50,000) and want payment certainty with a fixed rate.

**Choose HELOC when**: You have ongoing or uncertain needs, want to draw only what's needed (and only pay interest on the drawn amount), or want a backup line of credit you may not use.

## Qualification Requirements

To obtain a HELOC, lenders typically require:
- **Equity cushion**: After the HELOC, you must still have at least 15-20% equity in the home. Example: $500,000 home with $300,000 mortgage has $200,000 equity (40%). A lender allowing 80% LTV would allow a HELOC of up to $100,000 ($500,000 × 80% = $400,000 total debt; minus $300,000 existing mortgage = $100,000 HELOC).
- **Credit score**: Typically 680+ for competitive rates; some lenders require 720+
- **Debt-to-income ratio**: Below 43% including the HELOC payment
- **Stable income**: W-2 income is easiest; self-employed may require 2 years of tax returns

HELOC qualification is significantly easier when home values are high (more equity), credit is strong, and income is stable. Lenders verify all three — do not assume approval without meeting all criteria.

## Interest Tax Deductibility: Post-TCJA Rules

This is one of the most commonly misunderstood aspects of HELOCs.

**Pre-TCJA (before 2018)**: HELOC interest was deductible for any purpose up to $100,000 of home equity debt.

**Post-TCJA (2018 and forward)**: HELOC interest is deductible ONLY if the HELOC proceeds are used to "buy, build, or substantially improve" the home that secures the loan.

**Uses where interest IS deductible**:
- Kitchen remodel
- Bathroom addition
- Roof replacement
- HVAC system
- Major landscaping that increases property value

**Uses where interest is NOT deductible**:
- Paying off credit card debt
- Funding a vacation
- Buying a car
- Investing in stocks or bonds
- College tuition
- Business expenses (unless using a separate business deduction)

**Documentation requirement**: To claim the deduction, you need to trace the proceeds to qualified home improvement. Keep receipts, contractor invoices, and documentation showing the HELOC funds were used for qualified improvements.

**Maximum deductible interest**: Combined with the primary mortgage, the deductible home acquisition debt is limited to $750,000 (for loans taken out after December 15, 2017). Loans in excess of $750,000 generate non-deductible interest.

## Cash-Out Refinance vs. HELOC: The Decision

When you need to access home equity, two primary options compete:

**Cash-out refinance**:
- Replaces your existing first mortgage with a new, larger first mortgage
- Fixed rate available (same as primary mortgage)
- Single loan, single payment
- Higher closing costs (2-3% of the new loan amount; $6,000-$12,000 on a $400,000 loan)
- Resets amortization clock on the entire mortgage balance

**HELOC**:
- Second lien on the property (behind the first mortgage)
- Variable rate (usually)
- Separate payment in addition to your existing mortgage
- Lower closing costs ($0-$500 at many banks)
- Flexible: draw only what you need; interest only on drawn amount

**When to choose cash-out refi**:
- You can significantly improve your first mortgage rate at the same time (two goals, one transaction)
- You want fixed-rate certainty on the new debt
- Your current first mortgage rate is already high and refinancing makes sense independently

**When to choose HELOC**:
- Your current first mortgage is at a low rate (e.g., 3%) that you don't want to touch — a cash-out refi would reset your entire mortgage to current (higher) rates
- You want maximum flexibility on the draw amount and timing
- Lower upfront cost is important

The classic rule: if your existing first mortgage rate is good, the HELOC is the better access tool. If your first mortgage rate is already high and refinancing makes sense, a cash-out refi is more efficient.

## HELOC as Emergency Fund Backup

A strategic use: open a HELOC while you have strong income and equity (before you need it), but don't draw on it unless a genuine emergency occurs.

**Rationale**:
- HELOC lenders are less likely to approve you when you don't have income or when your financial profile has changed
- Pre-approved access provides a large backstop beyond liquid savings
- Unused HELOC costs nothing (most have no annual fee; a few charge $50-$100/year)
- Can serve as a bridge for a large unexpected expense rather than drawing down investments during unfavorable market conditions

**This is not a substitute for liquid emergency savings** — a HELOC is debt, and drawing on it in an emergency creates a repayment obligation. The optimal approach is: maintain 3-6 months of liquid emergency savings AND a HELOC as additional backstop.

## HELOC in Retirement: Sequence of Returns Management

An open HELOC provides a tool to manage sequence of returns risk — the danger of large portfolio withdrawals during a market downturn in early retirement.

**Strategy**:
1. Open (or maintain) a HELOC before retirement while income qualification is easy
2. In a year where portfolio has declined significantly (20-40% drawdown): draw from the HELOC to cover living expenses rather than selling equities at depressed prices
3. In subsequent years when markets recover: use portfolio withdrawals to repay the HELOC

This preserves more portfolio value during recoveries. The cost is HELOC interest during the draw period — often 5-9%. This may be less costly than permanently locking in equity losses by selling at the bottom.

**Limitation**: Lenders can freeze or reduce HELOC lines if home values decline materially (they did this widely during 2008-2009). Do not rely entirely on HELOC availability — it may not be there when most needed during a severe recession that also causes home value declines.

## HELOC Risks

**Variable rate risk**: When the Federal Reserve raises rates, HELOC rates rise with them. A homeowner who opened a HELOC at 4% in 2021 saw their rate rise to 8.5%+ by 2023 — more than doubling the cost of carried balances.

**Callable nature**: Lenders have the right to freeze or reduce HELOC credit limits if the borrower's financial condition deteriorates or if the home value declines. This is explicitly stated in most HELOC agreements. If you are relying on a HELOC for an ongoing project or as a financial backstop, the lender can eliminate that access.

**Foreclosure risk**: Unlike unsecured debt, a HELOC is secured by your home. If you cannot make HELOC payments during the repayment period, the lender has the right to foreclose. Drawing on home equity creates real downside risk for housing security.

**Over-borrowing trap**: Easy access to a large credit line creates behavioral temptation. HELOC debt grew significantly in the mid-2000s as homeowners treated rising equity as a piggy bank — and then faced foreclosure when values declined and they could not repay.
