# Medicare: Parts, Coverage, and Enrollment Guide

## Medicare Parts and What They Cover

### Part A: Hospital Insurance

**Coverage**: Inpatient hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care (following a qualifying hospital stay), hospice care, and some home health care.

**Premium**: $0 for most beneficiaries — you qualify for premium-free Part A if you (or your spouse) worked and paid Medicare taxes for at least 40 quarters (10 years). Those who don't qualify for premium-free Part A pay $518/month in 2025 (if they have fewer than 30 quarters of Medicare-covered work).

**Deductible**: $1,676 per benefit period in 2025. The benefit period is not the calendar year — it resets with each new hospital admission that begins more than 60 days after the previous inpatient stay ended. Multiple hospital stays in a calendar year can mean multiple Part A deductibles.

**Coinsurance after the deductible**:
- Days 1-60: Fully covered after deductible
- Days 61-90: $419/day coinsurance
- Days 91-150: $838/day coinsurance (lifetime reserve days)
- Days 151+: No coverage; patient pays 100%

### Part B: Medical Insurance

**Coverage**: Physician services, outpatient care, durable medical equipment, lab tests, preventive services, mental health outpatient services.

**Standard premium**: $185.00/month in 2025. Higher-income beneficiaries pay Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount (IRMAA) surcharges — see separate IRMAA document.

**Annual deductible**: $257 in 2025. After the deductible is met, Medicare pays 80% of covered services; beneficiary pays 20% (no out-of-pocket maximum in Original Medicare — a significant exposure).

### Part C: Medicare Advantage

Private health insurance plans that bundle Part A, Part B, and usually Part D into a single plan. Plans must cover all Medicare-covered services but can impose additional network restrictions, prior authorization requirements, and cost-sharing structures. May include additional benefits (dental, vision, hearing, transportation, fitness memberships) not covered by Original Medicare. See the Medicare Supplement document for the Advantage vs. Medigap comparison.

### Part D: Prescription Drug Coverage

Standalone plans (purchased alongside Original Medicare) or included in Medicare Advantage plans. The 2024 Inflation Reduction Act restructured Part D cost-sharing significantly:
- **2025 changes**: $2,000 out-of-pocket cap on Part D drug costs (new for 2025 — eliminated the catastrophic phase exposure)
- Plan premiums and drug formularies vary by plan; must compare annually during Annual Enrollment Period

## Medicare Eligibility

**Automatic enrollment**: If you are already receiving Social Security benefits when you turn 65, you are automatically enrolled in Medicare Parts A and B. Medicare sends your card approximately 3 months before your 65th birthday.

**Active enrollment required**: If you are not yet receiving Social Security at 65 (common for those who delay SS for higher benefits), you must actively enroll in Medicare during your Initial Enrollment Period (IEP).

**Initial Enrollment Period (IEP)**: A 7-month window:
- 3 months before the month you turn 65
- The month you turn 65
- 3 months after the month you turn 65

**Coverage start date effect**: If you enroll in the first 3 months of your IEP, coverage starts the first day of your birth month. If you enroll during or after your birth month, coverage is delayed 1-3 months.

## Part A Deductible: The Per Benefit Period Quirk

The Part A deductible is one of Medicare's most misunderstood features. Unlike most insurance deductibles that reset annually, the Part A deductible is per benefit period.

A benefit period begins when you are admitted to a hospital and ends when you have been out of the hospital (and skilled nursing facility) for 60 consecutive days. If you are re-hospitalized within 60 days of discharge, you are still in the same benefit period — no new deductible.

But if you are hospitalized, discharged, recover at home for 61+ days, then are re-hospitalized, a new benefit period begins — and a new $1,676 deductible applies. Two hospitalizations in a year can mean $3,352 in Part A deductibles.

## Part B Deductible and Coinsurance

**Annual deductible**: $257 in 2025. Reset January 1 each year.

**Coinsurance after deductible**: 20% of Medicare-approved amount with no out-of-pocket maximum. A major surgery or cancer treatment under Original Medicare with no supplement could expose the beneficiary to tens of thousands in 20% coinsurance. This is the primary driver of Medigap/Medicare Supplement demand.

## Medicare Supplement (Medigap) vs. Medicare Advantage

### Medicare Supplement (Medigap)

Works alongside Original Medicare to cover the cost-sharing gaps.

**Advantages**:
- See any provider who accepts Medicare — nationwide, no network restrictions
- Predictable costs: pay the Medigap premium, and most out-of-pocket costs are covered
- No referrals required; no prior authorization for Medicare-covered services
- Ideal for people with complex medical needs, frequent healthcare utilization, or who travel extensively

**Disadvantages**:
- Higher monthly premium (Plan G: approximately $100-$200/month depending on age and location)
- Does not include prescription drug coverage — must purchase Part D separately
- Outside of the Medicare Supplement open enrollment period, underwriting may be required

### Medicare Advantage (Part C)

**Advantages**:
- Often lower or $0 monthly premium
- May include dental, vision, hearing, fitness benefits
- Often includes Part D drug coverage in one plan
- Annual out-of-pocket maximum protects against catastrophic costs

**Disadvantages**:
- Network restrictions: must use in-network providers (HMO) or pay higher costs for out-of-network (PPO)
- Prior authorization requirements: certain procedures, specialist visits, or medications may require approval
- Unpredictable out-of-pocket costs: copays for each service can add up significantly for heavy users
- Switching back to Original Medicare + Medigap may require medical underwriting after the open enrollment window
- Star ratings vary significantly — plan quality is important

## Late Enrollment Penalties: Permanent and Significant

### Part B Late Enrollment Penalty

For every 12-month period you were eligible for Part B but did not enroll (without qualifying employer coverage), your Part B premium increases by 10% — permanently. This penalty applies for as long as you have Part B.

**Example**: A 65-year-old who had no qualifying employer coverage delays Part B enrollment by 2 full years. Their Part B premium is permanently increased by 20%:
- Standard premium: $185/month
- With 20% penalty: $222/month
- Over 20 years in retirement: approximately $8,880 in additional premiums from this one mistake

### Part D Late Enrollment Penalty

For every month you were without creditable prescription drug coverage after Medicare eligibility, your Part D premium increases by 1% per month — permanently.

**Example**: 15 months without creditable drug coverage = 15% permanent premium increase on Part D.

## Employer Coverage and Medicare Interaction

The interaction between employer health coverage and Medicare depends on employer size:

**Employer with 20+ employees**: Employer coverage is primary; Medicare is secondary. You can delay Part B enrollment without penalty while covered by an employer plan with 20+ employees. You have a Special Enrollment Period of 8 months after losing that coverage.

**Employer with fewer than 20 employees**: Medicare is primary; employer coverage is secondary. If you do not enroll in Medicare Part B when first eligible, you face the late enrollment penalty — the small employer coverage does not qualify as a creditable coverage alternative.

**COBRA and Medicare**: COBRA is not employer-sponsored active coverage — it is continuation coverage. If you lose employer coverage and elect COBRA, the 8-month Special Enrollment Period for Medicare begins at the loss of active coverage (not when COBRA ends). Many people make the mistake of assuming COBRA extends the Medicare enrollment window — it does not. See the separate COBRA bridge document for details.

## Medicare Savings Programs

For low-income Medicare beneficiaries, several programs reduce premiums and cost-sharing:

**Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB)**: Pays Part A and B premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance for beneficiaries below 100% federal poverty level

**Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary (SLMB)**: Pays Part B premium only; income 100-120% FPL

**Qualifying Individual (QI)**: Pays Part B premium; income 120-135% FPL

**Extra Help / Low Income Subsidy (LIS)**: Subsidizes Part D drug costs; separate application through Social Security Administration

These programs are administered by state Medicaid agencies and must be applied for separately from Medicare enrollment.
