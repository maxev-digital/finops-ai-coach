# Sequence of Returns Risk

## What Sequence of Returns Risk Is

Sequence of returns risk is the danger that the *order* of investment returns — not just the average return — significantly impacts retirement portfolio outcomes when money is being withdrawn.

Two retirees with identical **average annual returns over 20 years** can end up with dramatically different portfolio balances depending on whether the bad years come early or late in retirement. For the retiree who experiences poor returns in the first 5–10 years while withdrawing from the portfolio, the outcome can be catastrophic even if later years produce excellent returns.

This is distinct from the general concept of investment risk. Sequence of returns risk is specifically a **distribution-phase phenomenon** — it applies only when withdrawals are being made from the portfolio. During accumulation (contributions going in), the same risk does not exist in the same damaging way.

## Mathematical Demonstration

Consider two retirees, each starting with $1,000,000 and withdrawing $50,000 per year (5% initial withdrawal rate). Both experience the same annual returns but in reverse order:

**Retiree A — Bad returns early:**

| Year | Return | Withdrawal | Year-End Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | -25% | $50,000 | $700,000 |
| 2 | -15% | $50,000 | $545,000 |
| 3 | -8% | $50,000 | $451,400 |
| 4 | +5% | $50,000 | $423,970 |
| 5 | +12% | $50,000 | $424,847 |
| 6–20 | Good returns | ... | Portfolio depleted by year 17 |

**Retiree B — Good returns early:**

| Year | Return | Withdrawal | Year-End Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +25% | $50,000 | $1,200,000 |
| 2 | +15% | $50,000 | $1,330,000 |
| 3 | +12% | $50,000 | $1,439,600 |
| 4 | +5% | $50,000 | $1,461,580 |
| 5 | -8% | $50,000 | $1,294,654 |
| 6–20 | Poor returns | ... | Portfolio survives to age 85+ with substantial balance |

**Both experienced the same returns, same average, same withdrawal amount. One ran out of money; the other did not.** The difference is entirely sequencing.

## Why Accumulation Is Different

During the accumulation phase (working years), the same bad sequence of returns does not cause the same problem:

- A market crash in year 10 of a 30-year accumulation period allows the worker to continue buying shares at lower prices
- New contributions during market downturns purchase more shares, improving average cost basis
- Poor returns early in accumulation are actually favorable — cheaper shares purchased mean more shares compound over the remaining period
- Returns in the final 5 years before retirement matter most in accumulation (large balance, less time to recover)

**The transition from accumulation to distribution is the inflection point.** On day one of retirement, the mathematical regime changes entirely. Now, selling shares during a downturn to fund withdrawals permanently removes those shares from future recovery.

## Why Early Losses Are So Destructive

The mechanics of permanent impairment:

1. Portfolio starts at $1,000,000
2. Market drops 30% in year 1: portfolio falls to $700,000
3. Client withdraws $50,000 for living expenses: $650,000 remaining
4. To recover to $1,000,000, the portfolio now needs a **+53.8% gain** (not the same 30% that was lost)
5. The $50,000 withdrawal consisted of shares sold at depressed prices — those shares are gone and cannot participate in the recovery
6. The mathematics of percentage gains and losses asymmetrically punishes drawdowns: a 30% loss requires a 42.9% gain to recover, a 50% loss requires a 100% gain

Each year of withdrawals during a bear market sells more shares at depressed prices. The remaining portfolio is too small to generate the recovery needed to fund 20–30 more years of withdrawals.

## Mitigation Strategy 1: Cash Buffer / Bucket Strategy

Hold 1–2 years of living expenses in **cash or short-term instruments** (money market, short-term Treasury bills, short-term CDs) separate from the investment portfolio.

**How it works:** When markets decline, fund living expenses from the cash buffer rather than selling equity positions. This avoids locking in losses at depressed prices. The buffer is replenished from the investment portfolio when markets recover.

**Why it works:** A bear market that lasts 12–24 months is absorbed by the cash buffer. Historically, even the worst bear markets in U.S. history have produced positive cumulative returns within 3–5 years. A 2-year buffer provides a runway to avoid the most damaging phase of forced selling.

**Cost:** Cash earns little real return after inflation. The "drag" of holding 2 years of expenses in cash (say $80,000 on a $1,000,000 portfolio) versus investing it is real — roughly 2–4% per year on the cash balance. Over 20+ years, this is not trivial. But the insurance value against sequence risk often exceeds this cost.

## Mitigation Strategy 2: Flexible Spending

**Reduce discretionary spending by 10–20% during market downturns.** This is the simplest and most powerful mitigation strategy because it directly reduces the withdrawal rate — the core driver of sequence risk.

- Good market year: spend normally or slightly more (the "guardrails" approach)
- Bad market year (portfolio down 15–25%): cut discretionary spending by 10–20%

A flexible retiree who can cut from $60,000/year to $50,000/year during a bear market reduces portfolio withdrawals by 17%, dramatically improving portfolio survival probability.

**Research:** Studies show that retirees who spend flexibly can sustain initial withdrawal rates of 5–6% with similar success probabilities as rigid 4% spenders. The behavioral constraint is that many retirees resist spending cuts, even discretionary ones.

## Mitigation Strategy 3: Part-Time Income

Even modest earned income dramatically reduces portfolio withdrawal rates:

- **$10,000–$20,000/year** of part-time work in early retirement reduces required portfolio withdrawals by 25–50% in those years
- This directly mitigates the critical early-retirement period when sequence risk is highest
- Part-time work is often available, socially beneficial, and provides structure — many retirees enjoy it

**Example:** A $1,000,000 portfolio needing to provide $50,000/year (5% withdrawal) is severely stressed. The same portfolio needing to provide $30,000/year while the retiree earns $20,000/year part-time (3% effective withdrawal) is well within safe withdrawal territory.

The part-time income need not be permanent — even 3–5 years of modest work during the highest-risk early retirement period substantially improves long-term outcomes.

## Mitigation Strategy 4: Delay Social Security

A retiree who delays Social Security from 62 to 67 or 70 receives a permanently higher benefit — but must fund living expenses from the portfolio (or other sources) during the waiting period.

**The sequence risk benefit:** Once Social Security begins at a higher amount, the required portfolio withdrawal rate drops permanently. A client spending $60,000/year from the portfolio who starts receiving $28,000/year in Social Security now needs only $32,000/year from the portfolio — a 47% reduction in withdrawal rate.

**The trade-off:** Higher portfolio withdrawals during the delay period increases sequence risk before SS begins. This is why the strategy works best when the retiree has other income (part-time work, spouse's income, pension) to reduce portfolio draws during the delay window.

## Mitigation Strategy 5: Bond Tent (Rising Equity Glide Path)

Hold a **higher bond allocation at the start of retirement**, then gradually shift toward more equities over the first 10 years.

**Mechanics:** At retirement, allocate 40–50% bonds (higher than typical 30–35%). Each year, sell bonds and buy equities until reaching a target allocation of 60–70% equities at year 10.

**Why it works:** In a bear market at age 65–70, the high bond allocation means the retiree is selling bonds (which hold value in equity bear markets) to fund spending, not equities. By age 75, when the most dangerous sequence-risk period has passed, the allocation has shifted back toward equities for inflation protection.

**Research basis:** Michael Kitces and Wade Pfau published analysis showing that a declining equity glide path (increasing bonds through retirement) actually performs worse than a rising equity glide path (decreasing bonds) in retirement. The bond tent at retirement onset provides protection exactly when sequence risk is highest.

## Mitigation Strategy 6: HECM Reverse Mortgage Line of Credit

A **Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM) reverse mortgage line of credit** established in early retirement can serve as a sequence-of-returns buffer.

**How it functions as a buffer:**
- Establish the HECM line of credit at retirement (age 62+)
- Do not draw on it in good market years
- During a bear market, draw from the HECM line of credit for living expenses instead of selling equity positions
- When markets recover, replenish the line of credit from the portfolio if desired (optional — not required)

**Key HECM line of credit feature:** The unused credit line grows over time at the same rate as the loan interest rate. A $200,000 line established at 62 may grow to $350,000 by age 72. This "standby capacity" increases the buffer available in later years.

**Caution:** HECM loan balance accrues interest, reducing home equity. This is a real cost. But for clients with substantial home equity, using the HECM as a sequence-risk buffer rather than an emergency measure can preserve portfolio longevity.

## The "Go-Go, Slow-Go, No-Go" Spending Reality

Academic research and behavioral studies consistently show that retiree spending is **not flat or linearly inflation-adjusted** over time. Instead, actual retirement spending follows a declining real-dollar pattern after the early active years:

- **"Go-Go" years (ages 62–75):** Travel, hobbies, entertaining, gifts — peak discretionary spending
- **"Slow-Go" years (ages 75–85):** Less travel, fewer activities, home-centered life — spending declines in real terms
- **"No-Go" years (ages 85+):** Minimal discretionary spending, healthcare costs increase (offset partially by other spending reductions)

**The Blanchett "spending smile" research** (David Blanchett, Morningstar) shows that real spending declines approximately 1–2% per year after the early retirement period, with a potential uptick in the final years from healthcare costs.

**Implication for safe withdrawal rates:** A retiree who applies a rigid 3% inflation adjustment to spending each year will systematically overfund their portfolio — they will die with far more money than they spent because real spending declined while nominal withdrawals grew. Dynamic spending approaches (VPW, guardrails) implicitly capture this reality. The "early retirement is the critical risk period" conclusion is reinforced — if the client survives the first 10 years with the portfolio intact, the later years typically require less from the portfolio, not more.
