# Business Entity Selection: Tax and Legal Structure for Business Owners

## Sole Proprietorship: The Default Starting Point

A sole proprietorship is the default structure for an unincorporated business operated by one individual. No formal filing is required — you simply start conducting business.

**Tax treatment**:
- All business income and expenses reported on Schedule C of your personal Form 1040
- Net profit is subject to self-employment (SE) tax: 15.3% on the first $176,100 of net self-employment income (2025), then 2.9% above that
- One-half of SE tax is deductible as an above-the-line adjustment
- All net income subject to SE tax regardless of whether you draw cash from the business

**Legal exposure**: Unlimited personal liability. No legal separation between you and the business. Business debts, lawsuits, and judgments can reach personal assets — home, savings, investments.

**Appropriate for**: Very early-stage testing, minimal revenue businesses, and freelancers with low liability exposure. Most serious business owners should quickly move beyond sole proprietorship to an entity structure.

## Single-Member LLC: Liability Shield with Flexible Tax Treatment

A single-member LLC (SMLLC) is formed by filing Articles of Organization with the state (typically $50-$200 filing fee). It provides a legal liability shield while defaulting to sole proprietorship tax treatment.

**Default tax treatment (disregarded entity)**:
- Same as sole proprietorship for taxes: Schedule C, SE tax on all net income
- The LLC is "disregarded" for federal tax purposes — transparent to the owner

**Legal protection**: The LLC creates a legal separation between the business and owner. Business liabilities generally cannot reach personal assets (with proper maintenance of formalities — separate bank accounts, no commingling, reasonable capitalization).

**Elective tax treatment**: An SMLLC can elect to be taxed as:
- **S-Corporation**: Elect on Form 2553 — allows salary/distribution split to reduce SE tax
- **C-Corporation**: Rare for small businesses; creates double taxation

**When SMLLC is appropriate**: Any solo business owner who wants liability protection without complex governance. A freelance consultant, independent contractor, or small service business should typically operate through an SMLLC at minimum.

## Multi-Member LLC: Partnership Taxation with Liability Protection

A multi-member LLC with two or more members defaults to partnership taxation.

**Default tax treatment**:
- Files Form 1065 partnership return
- Issues Schedule K-1 to each member with their allocable share of income, deductions, and credits
- Members pay self-employment tax on their distributive share of income from active participation (passive members generally do not pay SE tax)
- Flexible profit/loss allocation: LLC operating agreement can allocate profits and losses differently from ownership percentages (subject to substantial economic effect rules)

**Legal protection**: Each member has limited liability — personal assets protected from business debts and liabilities.

**Elective tax treatment**: Multi-member LLC can elect S-Corp or C-Corp tax treatment by filing the appropriate election.

## S-Corporation: The Tax-Savings Election

The S-Corporation election transforms the LLC or corporation into a pass-through entity with a crucial advantage: the owner can split business income between W-2 wages (subject to payroll taxes) and distributions (not subject to payroll taxes).

### How the S-Corp Saves Taxes

Self-employment tax is 15.3% on the first ~$176,100 of net income (employee + employer share combined). An S-Corp owner avoids this tax on the portion of income received as distributions (not wages).

**Example**:
- Business profit: $200,000
- Sole proprietor: $200,000 × 15.3% SE tax (minus deduction) ≈ $27,000 in SE tax
- S-Corp with $80,000 salary: Payroll taxes on $80,000 ≈ $12,240 (7.65% employer + 7.65% employee); $120,000 as distribution — no payroll tax
- Annual savings: approximately $11,000-$14,000

This is significant, recurring, and entirely legal. The IRS encourages S-Corps — it's the intent of the structure.

### S-Corp Election Requirements

**Eligibility requirements for S-Corp election**:
- Domestic corporation
- No more than 100 shareholders
- All shareholders must be US citizens or permanent residents (no foreign shareholders)
- Only one class of stock (all shares have equal economic rights, though voting rights can vary)
- No partnership, LLC, or corporate shareholders (with limited exceptions for certain trusts)

**Election mechanics**:
- **Form 2553** must be filed with the IRS
- To be effective for the current tax year: must be filed by **March 15** of the tax year, OR within 75 days of the LLC/corporation's formation
- Late election relief: the IRS routinely grants late S-Corp elections when there is reasonable cause; consult a tax advisor if you missed the deadline

**Annual compliance**:
- File Form 1120-S (S-Corp tax return)
- Issue K-1s to shareholders
- Run payroll for the owner-employee (including quarterly payroll tax deposits, W-2 at year-end)
- Additional compliance cost: payroll service ($50-$150/month) and business tax return preparation ($500-$2,000+/year)

### S-Corp Reasonable Compensation Requirement

The IRS requires owner-employees of S-Corps to pay themselves "reasonable compensation" as W-2 wages before taking distributions. The IRS has challenged and won cases where S-Corp owners paid themselves $0 or very low salaries and took all profit as distributions.

**Reasonable compensation determination**:
- What would a comparable employee earn for the same work in the same industry and market?
- Reference: BLS Occupational Employment Statistics, comparable salary surveys, industry benchmarks
- Rule of thumb for service businesses: 30-50% of total pass-through income is a commonly used starting point, but the actual standard is "reasonable for the services provided"

**Consequences of unreasonable compensation**: IRS can reclassify distributions as wages, assess back payroll taxes plus penalties and interest.

## C-Corporation: When It Makes Sense

**Tax treatment**: C-Corps are separate taxable entities. The corporation pays corporate income tax at a flat 21% rate. Profits distributed to shareholders as dividends are taxed again at the shareholder level (qualified dividends: 0/15/20%) — the "double taxation."

**When C-Corp is appropriate despite double taxation**:

1. **Seeking VC or institutional investment**: Most venture capital investment requires a Delaware C-Corp with multiple share classes (common and preferred). If you plan to raise VC funding, C-Corp is effectively required.

2. **Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) planning**: §1202 QSBS exclusion applies to C-Corp stock. Up to $10M of gain on qualifying C-Corp stock held 5+ years can be excluded from federal tax. This extraordinary benefit requires C-Corp status.

3. **International operations**: C-Corps have access to certain favorable international tax provisions not available to pass-throughs.

4. **Retained earnings reinvestment**: If the business retains most earnings for growth rather than distributing, the 21% corporate rate can be more favorable than the individual rate (up to 37%) on pass-through income.

5. **Fringe benefits**: C-Corps can deduct certain fringe benefits (health insurance, disability insurance) for owner-employees; S-Corps have more limited fringe benefit deductions.

## Payroll Tax Savings: S-Corp vs. Sole Proprietor Example

For a profitable service business with $200,000 in annual net income:

**Sole Proprietor / Single-Member LLC (default)**:
- Net self-employment income: $200,000
- SE tax on first $176,100: $26,943 (after deduction for ½ SE tax, effective SE tax burden: ~$23,000)

**S-Corp with $80,000 reasonable salary**:
- W-2 salary: $80,000
- Employer payroll taxes: $6,120 (7.65%)
- Employee payroll taxes (withheld): $6,120
- Total payroll tax: $12,240
- Distribution: $120,000 — no SE/payroll tax
- Net savings vs. sole proprietor: approximately $10,760-$14,000/year (varies with income level and exact SE tax calculation)

**Breakeven for S-Corp conversion**: The additional compliance cost (payroll service ~$1,800/year + S-Corp return preparation ~$1,000-$2,000/year) = approximately $3,000-$3,800/year. Net savings start around $50,000-$80,000 in business profit — below this level, the savings may not justify the complexity.

## State-Level Considerations

Business entity selection has state-specific implications:
- **State income tax**: S-Corp distributions may be subject to state income tax in some states (not all states follow federal S-Corp pass-through rules)
- **Franchise taxes**: Some states charge annual franchise fees or taxes on LLCs and S-Corps (California: $800/year minimum franchise tax on LLCs; Texas: franchise tax on entity gross receipts)
- **State payroll taxes**: S-Corp wages subject to state unemployment insurance and state income tax withholding
- **Delaware vs. your state**: Many businesses incorporate in Delaware for legal flexibility, but must register as a foreign entity in the owner's home state if operating there

Engage a CPA or business attorney familiar with your specific state's rules before selecting entity type.
