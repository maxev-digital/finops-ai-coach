# Medicare Enrollment: Deadlines, Penalties, and Critical Decisions

## The Initial Enrollment Period (IEP): Your First Window

When you first become eligible for Medicare at age 65, you have a 7-month window called the Initial Enrollment Period:

- **3 months BEFORE the month you turn 65**
- **The month you turn 65**
- **3 months AFTER the month you turn 65**

**Why timing within the IEP matters for coverage start**:

| When you enroll | Part B coverage begins |
|-----------------|----------------------|
| During the 3 months before your birth month | First day of the month you turn 65 |
| During the month you turn 65 | First day of the month AFTER you sign up |
| 1 month after your birth month | First day of 2 months after you sign up |
| 2-3 months after your birth month | First day of 3 months after you sign up |

If you want coverage to begin on your 65th birthday, enroll in the 3 months before that month. Waiting until your birth month or after creates a gap in coverage — potentially months without Medicare while your prior coverage has ended.

## Part A: Who Is Auto-Enrolled vs. Who Must Actively Enroll

**Automatic Part A enrollment**:
- If you are already receiving Social Security retirement or disability benefits when you turn 65, you are automatically enrolled in both Medicare Part A and Part B. Your Medicare card arrives approximately 3 months before your 65th birthday.

**Manual enrollment required**:
- If you are NOT receiving Social Security benefits (for example, because you are delaying SS to earn delayed retirement credits), you must actively sign up for Medicare during your IEP.
- Enroll online at ssa.gov or visit a local Social Security office.

**Part A premium-free eligibility**: Premium-free Part A requires 40 quarters (10 years) of Medicare-covered employment. Those with 30-39 quarters pay $285/month; those with fewer than 30 quarters pay $518/month in 2025.

**Even if you pay a premium for Part A, enrolling is almost always wise**: Hospital costs without Part A coverage can reach tens of thousands per admission.

## Part B: The Most Critical Enrollment Decision

Part B is the component where late enrollment mistakes are most costly. Part B premiums are $185/month in 2025 (for most beneficiaries).

### Late Enrollment Penalty: Permanent and Significant

For every 12-month period you were eligible for Part B but did not enroll (without having qualifying active employer coverage as a valid reason for deferral), your Part B premium increases by **10% permanently** — for as long as you have Part B.

**Example of the penalty math**:
A 65-year-old who delayed Part B enrollment by 3 full years without qualifying employer coverage:
- Penalty: 30% added to standard premium
- 2025: $185.00 × 1.30 = $240.50/month
- Additional cost vs. no penalty: $55.50/month
- Over 20 years in retirement: $55.50 × 240 months = **$13,320 in additional premiums**

From one enrollment mistake.

The penalty compounds with future premium increases — if the standard premium rises to $250/month, the 30% penalty makes it $325/month.

## Delaying Part B Without Penalty: The Employer Coverage Exception

You can defer Part B enrollment without penalty if you have active employer-sponsored health coverage based on current employment (your own or your spouse's).

**Requirements for qualifying coverage**:
- Coverage through an employer with **20 or more employees** (the employer plan is primary)
- Based on active employment — the key person must currently be working, not retired
- COBRA and retiree health coverage do NOT count as active employer coverage for this purpose

**If employer has fewer than 20 employees**: Medicare is PRIMARY, not secondary. You must enroll in Part B when first eligible or face the late enrollment penalty, even if you have employer coverage.

## Special Enrollment Period (SEP): After Losing Employer Coverage

When you lose active employer coverage (whether because you retire, your employer terminates coverage, or you lose eligibility), you have a Special Enrollment Period:

**Duration**: 8 months from the LATER of:
- The date the employment ends, OR
- The date the employer group health coverage ends

**Critical COBRA misunderstanding**: COBRA coverage does NOT extend the SEP. The SEP clock begins when active employment ends — not when COBRA ends.

**Example of this costly mistake**:
- Worker retires at 67; ends active employer coverage on June 30
- Elects COBRA; expects 18 months of COBRA coverage
- Assumes 8-month SEP begins when COBRA ends (December of the following year)
- Does NOT enroll in Part B during the 8-month SEP
- Result: 8-month SEP expires in February of the following year; COBRA is still active
- The worker is now past the SEP; they must wait for General Enrollment Period (January 1 - March 31); coverage begins July 1; late enrollment penalty permanently applies

This scenario plays out regularly. The 8-month SEP is often not communicated clearly by employers or COBRA administrators.

**To avoid this**: Enroll in Medicare Part B within 8 months of losing active employment, even if COBRA is still covering you. You can stop COBRA when Medicare starts.

## Part D Enrollment: Drug Coverage Deadlines

Medicare Part D provides prescription drug coverage (standalone plan or included in Medicare Advantage).

**Penalty for late Part D enrollment**: 1% of the national average Part D base beneficiary premium × number of months without creditable coverage. This penalty is also permanent.

**Creditable coverage**: Prescription drug coverage that is at least as good as Medicare Part D is "creditable coverage" — you may defer Part D enrollment without penalty while covered by creditable employer prescription coverage.

**Verification**: Employers must notify employees annually whether their prescription drug coverage is creditable. Keep this documentation.

**When to enroll in Part D**: Enroll when you:
- First become eligible for Medicare AND don't have creditable employer drug coverage
- Lose creditable employer drug coverage

## General Enrollment Period: The Penalty-Still-Applies Option

For those who missed their IEP and SEP without qualifying coverage, there is a General Enrollment Period:
- **Dates**: January 1 through March 31 each year
- **Coverage begins**: July 1 of that year
- **Penalty**: Yes — late enrollment penalties still apply for months without coverage

This is the last resort. The coverage delay (enrollment January-March; coverage July 1) can leave a 3-6 month gap. And the permanent premium penalty begins.

## Medicare and HDHP/HSA: A Critical Interaction

Once you enroll in Medicare Part A, you can NO longer contribute to a Health Savings Account (HSA). Medicare Part A enrollment terminates HSA contribution eligibility.

**The trap**: If you claim Social Security benefits before age 65, the SSA automatically enrolls you in Medicare Part A (6 months retroactively in some cases). This can result in inadvertent HSA overcontributions if you were still contributing to an HSA on an HDHP.

**Scenario**: 64-year-old files for Social Security at 64 years, 9 months. Social Security grants retroactive coverage of up to 6 months of Part A. Medicare Part A becomes effective at age 64 years, 3 months. If the person was contributing to an HSA during those 6 months, those contributions are excess contributions — subject to income tax and 6% excise tax.

**Solution**: Do NOT claim Social Security if you want to continue HSA contributions past age 65. Delay Social Security until you are ready to stop HSA contributions and enroll in Medicare. Stop HSA contributions at least 6 months before enrolling in Medicare to account for the potential retroactive Part A enrollment.

## Medicare Advantage Enrollment and Disenrollment

**Initial enrollment**: Can choose Medicare Advantage at initial Medicare enrollment instead of Original Medicare.

**Annual Enrollment Period (AEP)**: October 15 – December 7 each year. Switch between Medicare Advantage plans, switch from Advantage to Original Medicare, change Part D plans. Coverage changes effective January 1.

**Open Enrollment Period (OEP)**: January 1 – March 31. Switch Medicare Advantage plans once, or switch from Advantage to Original Medicare (and add a Part D plan). Cannot use OEP to switch from Original Medicare to Advantage.

**Special Enrollment Periods (SEPs) for Medicare Advantage**: Various qualifying events (moving out of plan service area, plan terminating, gaining Medicaid eligibility) trigger SEPs allowing changes outside the regular enrollment windows.

**Returning to Original Medicare + Medigap**: When switching from Medicare Advantage to Original Medicare during AEP, enrolling in a Medigap supplement plan may require medical underwriting in most states (except in states with continuous guaranteed issue). Health conditions developed while on Advantage may result in denial or higher premiums for Medigap — this is the lock-in risk discussed in the Medigap document.

## Summary of Key Enrollment Deadlines

| Action | Deadline | Penalty for Missing |
|--------|----------|---------------------|
| Part B enrollment at 65 (no employer coverage) | 7-month IEP | 10% permanent penalty per year missed |
| Part B enrollment after losing employer coverage | 8 months from active coverage end (NOT COBRA end) | 10% permanent penalty per year missed |
| Part D enrollment | 63 days after prior creditable coverage ends | 1% per month without creditable coverage, permanent |
| Medigap open enrollment (guaranteed issue) | 6 months from Part B enrollment start | May face underwriting outside this window |
| Choosing Medicare Advantage at initial enrollment | IEP | Annual window to switch during AEP |
| Annual election changes | October 15 – December 7 | Cannot change until next AEP |
